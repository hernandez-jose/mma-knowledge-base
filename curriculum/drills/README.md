# The Drill Library

> Every drill in this curriculum, in one place, with an ID you can write on a
> whiteboard. The [modules](../modules/) tell you *what* to learn. These files
> tell you *how to make it stick* — and the last file tells you how to blend all
> of it back into a live roll.

---

## The five files

| File | ID prefix | What's in it |
|---|:-:|---|
| [Solo drills](solo-drills.md) | `S-` | The physical alphabet. No partner, no excuse. Warm-ups and home practice. |
| [Partner drills](partner-drills.md) | `P-` | Cooperative to progressively resistant reps. Where a technique becomes a motor pattern. |
| [Positional games](positional-games.md) | `G-` | Constrained live rounds with a win condition. Where a motor pattern becomes a skill. |
| [Chain drills](chain-drills.md) | `C-` | Attack A → their defense → attack B. Where isolated skills become a game. |
| [Integration rolls](integration-rolls.md) | `R-` | Roll formats that fold everything above into open sparring without losing it. |

Cite drills by ID. "Warm-up: S-01, S-03, S-07" is a complete instruction, and a
training partner who has read this file knows exactly what to do.

**Every card carries an `Atlas:` line** naming the table — and the row — in
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) that the drill trains. Live formats resolve to
[XIV.a — Drilling and rolling formats](../../jujitsu.md#xiva--drilling-and-rolling-formats),
which is the atlas's table of every way to run a round.

## The ladder every technique climbs

A technique is not learned when you can perform it. It is learned when it
appears without you deciding to use it. Everything in this library exists to
move a technique up these five rungs:

| Rung | Format | Resistance | What it builds | You've outgrown it when |
|:-:|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Solo (`S-`) | none | The movement itself | The shape is automatic and symmetrical |
| **2** | Cooperative partner (`P-`) | 0–20% | Where your body goes relative to theirs | You stop thinking about step order |
| **3** | Resistant partner (`P-` at pace) | 40–70% | Timing and grip sequence | It works on a partner who knows it's coming |
| **4** | Positional game (`G-`, `C-`) | 70–100%, constrained | Recognition — *when* to use it | You win the game more often than you lose it |
| **5** | Integration roll (`R-`) | live | Selection under chaos | It shows up in a roll you weren't thinking about it in |

**The most common training error is living on rung 2 and calling it practice.**
The second most common is jumping to rung 5 and calling everything else boring.
Rungs 3 and 4 are where skill is actually built, and they're the two that get
cut when class runs long.

## Why games, and not only reps

Repetition builds the movement. **Games build the appetite.**

A drill with a win condition changes the room. People count. People come back
to the same partner to settle it. People stay after class to run it again. That
is not a side effect to be tolerated — it's the mechanism. Skill acquisition
research and every gym that has ever been fun agree on the same thing: a
constrained game with a clear score produces more relevant reps, more decisions
per minute, and more *desire to do it again* than the same fifteen minutes spent
on cooperative repetition.

So the rule for this library is:

> **Every technique block ends in a game, and every game ends in a roll.**

You never learn a movement and leave it stranded. It gets a score attached to
it the same day, and it gets folded into live rolling the same week. That is
what "blending it in" means, and [integration-rolls.md](integration-rolls.md)
is the file that does it.

## Building a drill block

Any 60-minute session can be built from four numbers:

| Minutes | Block | Pull from |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8 | Warm-up | 3–4 `S-` drills, chosen to rehearse today's movement |
| 8–20 | Technique reps | 1–2 `P-` drills, cooperative first, then at 50% |
| 20–32 | Chain | 1 `C-` drill built on today's technique |
| 32–45 | Game | 1–2 `G-` games starting from today's exact position |
| 45–58 | Roll | 1 `R-` format — task, handicap, or open with a constraint |
| 58–60 | Notes | Three lines: what worked, what didn't, what to ask |

Two techniques drilled to competence beat six demonstrated beautifully. The
same is true of drills — running four of these well beats sampling twelve.

## Dosage and honesty

- **Reps beat minutes.** "20 reps each side" is a real instruction; "drill it for
  five minutes" is a suggestion people ignore at rep eight.
- **Both sides, always.** A technique you own on one side is a technique you
  own half of. If time is short, cut the drill, not the weak side.
- **Intensity is a dial, not a switch.** 50% means your partner can still do
  their job. 70% means they have to be right. 100% means somebody is about to
  learn something about their ligaments.
- **Call the number out loud before the round.** Most training injuries happen
  because two people were rolling at different percentages and neither said so.

## Safety notes that apply to every drill in this library

- **Tap early, tap loudly, release instantly.** Releasing fast is the higher
  skill, and it's the one that keeps you with training partners.
- **Leg locks:** apply slowly and never as a reflex. Rotational attacks (heel
  hook, toe hold) are drilled at a crawl, with a coach, and defense is taught
  first. See [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part XII for belt legality.
- **Neck:** no cranks in drilling, ever, including "just showing" a twister or a
  can opener. Inversions and granby rolls load the neck — build volume slowly.
- **Announce injuries before the round starts**, not while it's happening.
- **Games have win conditions, not stakes.** The moment a game round turns into
  a fight about who won, it stopped being a drill.
