# MMA — The Complete Atlas

> Every position, strike, entry, defense, transition and decision that makes up mixed martial arts — rated for **difficulty**, **how often you'll actually see it**, and **what it costs you when it fails**.
>
> Companion to **[jujitsu.md](jujitsu.md)**, which is the same treatment for the grappling half. Where a technique is pure jiu-jitsu, this file names it and links there rather than repeating three hundred rows. What you'll find here is everything that changes *because there are strikes, a cage, five-minute rounds and judges*.

---

## How to read the tables

Every technique gets three numbers. MMA needs a third one that jiu-jitsu doesn't.

### Difficulty (`Diff`) — how hard it is to actually land

| # | Level | What it means |
|:-:|---|---|
| **1** | Day one | Taught in a first class and usable that night on the bag. |
| **2** | Easy | Weeks of drilling. Forgiving of small errors. |
| **3** | Moderate | Needs timing, a setup, and a read. Amateur-competition level. |
| **4** | Hard | Long setups, tight windows, or specific attributes — flexibility, explosiveness, reach. |
| **5** | Expert | Specialist. A handful of people in the world make it look routine. |

### How common (`Com`) — how often it appears in real MMA

| # | Level | What it means |
|:-:|---|---|
| **5** | Everywhere | Every fight, every card. Non-optional knowledge. |
| **4** | Very common | Core of most fighters' games. Expect it every event. |
| **3** | Common | Regular sighting. A named tool that many fighters own. |
| **2** | Occasional | Situational or stylistic. Shows up when the matchup allows. |
| **1** | Rare | Highlight-reel or specialist-only. Worth recognising, not building on. |

### Risk (`Risk`) — what it costs you when it fails

**This column is why MMA is not kickboxing plus jiu-jitsu.** A technique that misses in kickboxing costs you a moment. The same technique in MMA can cost you the fight, because your opponent is allowed to take you down while your leg is in the air.

| # | Level | What failure costs |
|:-:|---|---|
| **1** | Nothing | You reset. No positional consequence. |
| **2** | Position | You give up a step, a grip, or the centre. |
| **3** | The exchange | They counter cleanly, or you concede a takedown attempt. |
| **4** | The position | You're taken down, put on the fence, or badly countered. |
| **5** | The fight | Missing it hands them your back, a knockdown, or a finish. |

**A note on honesty:** `Com` is calibrated against what actually appears in professional MMA — judged by fight footage and published finish/strike statistics — blended with what happens in a normal gym. `Diff` and `Risk` are coaching judgment. All three vary with your body, stance, division and opponent.

**Markers:** ⛔ = illegal under the Unified Rules · 🅐 = commonly illegal in amateur MMA · 🩸 = high-damage / low-volume tool · 🔒 = position-first technique (safe to throw, hard to punish).

> **On sources:** [`jujitsu.md`](jujitsu.md) carries an individually verified reference link on every row. This file does not, because the network in the environment it was written in could not reach the sources to verify them, and an unverified link is worse than no link. Grappling entries point at `jujitsu.md`, which is sourced. Everything else is written from coaching knowledge and should be checked against your own coach and the current ruleset before you rely on it.

## Part I — The Map

### The five ranges

An MMA fight is a fight to be in the range you're good at and to deny theirs. Everything else is detail.

| Range | Distance | Weapons | Who wants it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Free / out of range** | Nobody can hit anybody without a step | Feints, footwork, reading | Whoever is behind on the cards, and whoever is faster |
| **Kicking range** | Their lead hand cannot reach your head | Low kicks, teeps, front kicks, long knees | Taller fighters, karate and taekwondo bases |
| **Punching range** | Both of you can land clean | Everything, and the level change | Boxers — and the highest-risk range in the sport |
| **Clinch** | Chest contact, grips live | Knees, elbows, dirty boxing, takedowns | Wrestlers, Thai fighters, the physically stronger |
| **Ground** | One of you is down | Ground and pound, submissions, getting up | Whoever is on top, almost always |

**The rule that organises the sport:** *every range transition is where fights are won.* Most knockouts happen entering or exiting punching range. Most takedowns happen while somebody is committed to a strike. Nobody gets finished in a range they chose to be in and were ready for.

### The MMA position ladder

The BJJ ladder in [`jujitsu.md`](jujitsu.md#part-i--the-map) still applies on the ground — but strikes change what a position is worth. Guard is far worse than in sport grappling. Standing is far better.

| Value | Position | Why it's worth that in MMA |
|:-:|---|---|
| **+5** | **Back control with hooks** | Their neck is available, they cannot strike you, and the choke that ends fights lives here. |
| **+4** | **Mount** | Full weight, both hands free, and they cannot defend their head and buck at the same time. |
| **+3** | **Side control / knee-on-belly / crucifix** | Elbows and short punches with almost no return. |
| **+2** | **Half guard top / postured in their guard** | You can strike; they mostly cannot. Every second is damage. |
| **+1** | **Standing, neutral, in your range** | The best place to be if you're winning the striking. |
| **0** | **Clinch, neutral grips** | Somebody is about to score. Usually whoever wins the underhook. |
| **−1** | **Standing, in their range, back to the fence** | Losing space, losing rounds, one shot from worse. |
| **−2** | **Bottom half guard with a knee shield** | Defensible, but you are being hit and the clock is theirs. |
| **−3** | **Bottom closed guard** | In sport BJJ this is neutral. Under strikes it is losing. |
| **−4** | **Bottom side control / mount** | Being hit with your hips pinned. Escape or the referee will. |
| **−5** | **Back taken with hooks** | Worst position in the sport. |

**The two rules of the ladder in MMA:**

1. *Position before submission* still holds — but so does **position before damage**. A position that lets you hit them is worth more than a position that merely holds them.
2. **Standing up is always an option, and usually the right one.** In sport grappling, bottom is a place with an attacking game. Under strikes, bottom is a place you leave.

## Part II — Stance, footwork and distance

| Item | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Orthodox stance** | Left foot and hand forward. Default for right-handed fighters. | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| **Southpaw stance** | Right foot and hand forward. Roughly 1 in 4 fighters, and an advantage until your opponent has trained for it. | 1 | 4 | 1 |
| **Switch stance / stance switching** | Changing lead mid-fight or mid-combination. Opens new angles; costs base while you switch. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **The MMA stance** | Narrower than a wrestler's, longer than a boxer's, weight centred, lead leg light enough to check a kick and heavy enough to sprawl. The compromise *is* the technique. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Level of the hands** | High enough to catch a head kick, low enough to frame on a shot. Every fighter's hand position is a bet about which threat is real. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Step-and-slide** | Push off the back foot, land the lead, recover the stance. The default way to travel. | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| **Pivot** | Turning on the lead foot to change the angle without changing the distance. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Angle step (off-line)** | Stepping outside their lead foot so their power hand is aimed at where you were. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Circling out** | Moving laterally away from their power side, especially off the fence. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Cutting the cage** | Stepping to *where they are going* rather than chasing where they are. How pressure fighters trap people. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Backing straight up** | The most common beginner error in the sport. Listed here so it can be named and killed. | 1 | 5 | 5 |
| **Level change** | Bending the knees, not the waist, to threaten the legs or to duck under. The most important movement in MMA. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Blast step / burst entry** | Covering distance in one explosive step behind a strike. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Open stance vs closed stance** | Orthodox vs southpaw is *open* — lead feet outside, rear kicks and lead hooks open up. Same-stance is *closed* — the jab and rear cross dominate. Every technique's value changes with this. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Lead-foot position battle** | Getting your lead foot outside theirs in an open stance. Small, invisible, decides who lands the power hand. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Distance management** | Knowing which of the five ranges you are in and choosing it deliberately. | 3 | 5 | 3 |

## Part III — Punches

| Punch | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Jab** | Lead straight. Measures distance, sets everything up, scores rounds. The most valuable strike in the sport. | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| **Double / triple jab** | Repeated jabs to walk them backwards or hide the entry behind the last one. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Body jab** | The same punch to the solar plexus. Drops their hands and hides the level change. 🔒 | 2 | 3 | 2 |
| **Pawing jab** | A soft, deliberately non-committal jab used to gauge reaction and blind them. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Cross (rear straight)** | Rear-hand straight, hips rotating. The most common knockdown punch in MMA. | 1 | 5 | 3 |
| **Lead hook** | Horizontal lead-hand punch, elbow at 90°. Best off a slip or after a jab. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **Rear hook** | Rear-hand hook. Shorter range, big commitment, big consequence. | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| **Check hook** | A lead hook thrown while pivoting away from a charging opponent. Punishes forward pressure. | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| **Lead uppercut** | Rising lead punch through the middle. Punishes a low head and a shot. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Rear uppercut** | Rising rear punch. The clinch and cage-entry punch, and the classic answer to a level change. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Shovel hook** | Half hook, half uppercut, at 45°. Fits the gap when neither pure punch does. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Overhand** | Looping rear hand over the top of their jab. Ugly, effective, and the signature punch of wrestlers who learned to strike. | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| **Body cross / rip to the liver** | Rear hand to the ribs and liver. Slow, cumulative, and it wins late rounds. 🩸 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Hook to the body** | Lead hook under the elbow. The punch that quietly ends more fights than the head shot that follows it. 🩸 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Superman punch** | A faked kick or step that turns into a jumping rear straight. Distance-covering, hugely committal. | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| **Backfist** | Whipping strike with the back of the fist, usually off a pivot. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Spinning backfist** | Full turn into a backfist. Spectacular; the whole time you cannot see them. | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| **Hammerfist** | Downward strike with the bottom of the fist. Mostly a ground weapon, and a legal one when a head strike is not. | 1 | 4 | 1 |
| **Short punches in the clinch (dirty boxing)** | Uppercuts and hooks thrown with one hand controlling. Scores where the referee can see it. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Ground punches from top** | Posted or postured strikes from inside their guard, half guard, side or mount. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Up-punches from bottom** | Strikes thrown off your back. Legal, mostly ineffective, occasionally exactly right. | 2 | 3 | 3 |

## Part IV — Kicks

| Kick | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Low kick (outside)** | Shin into the outside of their lead thigh. Compounds over three rounds. 🔒 | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Calf kick** | The same kick lower, into the peroneal nerve below the knee. Faster to compound and harder to check; it has reshaped modern MMA. 🩸 | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Inside low kick** | Into the inside of their lead thigh. Turns their stance and opens the power hand. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Body roundhouse** | Shin into the ribs or liver. High damage, high risk — a caught body kick is a takedown. 🩸 | 2 | 5 | 4 |
| **Head kick (roundhouse)** | The classic highlight finish. Best hidden behind a low kick or a punch. 🩸 | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| **Teep / push kick (lead)** | Front-leg push to the belly or hip. Kills forward pressure and measures range. 🔒 | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Rear teep** | Same kick, rear leg. More power, more commitment, slower recovery. | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Front kick to the face** | Snapping front kick up the middle, toes or heel. Rare, devastating, and it beats a fighter who ducks. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Oblique kick / stomp kick** | Push kick into the knee or thigh to stop forward movement. Legal, effective, widely disliked. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Side kick (body or knee)** | Linear kick from a bladed stance. Long range, hard to counter, awkward to set up. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Question mark kick** | A kick that starts low and finishes high. Beats the fighter who has learned to check. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Switch kick** | Switching stance mid-motion to kick with the other leg. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Spinning back kick** | Turning kick with the heel to the body. Enormous power up the middle. | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| **Spinning heel kick / wheel kick** | Full-rotation head kick. The single highest-variance strike in the sport. | 5 | 1 | 5 |
| **Hook kick** | Kick that passes their guard and pulls back into the head. | 4 | 1 | 4 |
| **Axe kick** | Rising then falling heel strike. Rare and situational. | 4 | 1 | 4 |
| **Crescent kick** | Inside-out or outside-in arc, usually to clear the guard rather than to finish. | 3 | 1 | 4 |
| **Flying / jumping kicks** | Any of the above with both feet off the floor. All of them are gambles. | 5 | 1 | 5 |
| **Up-kick from bottom** | Kick from your back at a *standing* opponent. Legal; kicking a **grounded** opponent's head is not. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Soccer kick / stomp to a grounded head** ⛔ | Legal in a few rulesets worldwide, banned under the Unified Rules. Know it exists and know it is a disqualification here. | — | 1 | 5 |

## Part V — Knees, elbows and the rest

| Strike | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Straight knee (clinch)** | Rear knee up the middle from a collar tie or body lock. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Curve knee** | Knee arcing into the ribs or liver from the side. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Knee to the body from double collar tie** | The Thai plum, and what it exists for. 🩸 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Knee to the head in the clinch** | Same position, higher target. Fight-ending when it lands. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Flying knee** | Jumping knee, usually as a counter to a level change. Spectacular; catastrophic when it misses. | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| **Knee to the head of a grounded opponent** ⛔ | Illegal under the Unified Rules. The definition of "grounded" is the most-argued rule in the sport — know your commission's version. | — | 2 | 5 |
| **Knee to the body of a grounded opponent** | Legal. Underused, and available from side control and half guard top. | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| **Horizontal elbow** | Elbow across the jaw or temple. The most reliable cut-producing strike. 🩸 🅐 | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Upward elbow** | Rising elbow from close range, often through the middle of the guard. 🅐 | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| **Downward (12-to-6) elbow** ⛔ | Historically banned outright under the Unified Rules; several commissions have moved to amend this. **Check the current ruleset for your event before you rely on it.** 🅐 | 2 | 1 | 5 |
| **Spinning back elbow** | Turning elbow at close range. Shorter and safer than a spinning punch, still blind. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Elbow from top position** | Short elbows from inside their guard, half guard, side or mount. How ground damage is actually done. 🩸 🅐 | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Elbow from bottom** | Upward elbows off your back when they posture inside your guard. | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| **Forearm / shoulder pressure** | Not a strike — a legal, exhausting, face-grinding pressure tool in the clinch and on the fence. | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| **Headbutt** ⛔ | Banned. Listed so it is recognised, and so accidental clashes are understood as fouls, not tactics. | — | 1 | 5 |
| **Strikes to the back of the head or spine** ⛔ | Banned. The most common accidental foul in the sport, usually from the back or in a scramble. | — | 3 | 4 |
| **Groin strikes, eye pokes, fish-hooking, throat grabs, small-joint manipulation** ⛔ | All banned. Eye pokes are the sport's most frequent fight-ending foul — an open, extended hand is a habit worth coaching out. | — | 3 | 5 |

## Part VI — Striking defense

Four families. Most fighters own one and are beaten by whatever the other three answer.

| Defense | Family | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **High guard** | Block | Gloves at the temples, elbows in. Absorbs; does not solve. | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| **Long guard** | Block | Lead arm extended into their face to measure and blind. Thai answer to pressure. | 2 | 3 | 2 |
| **Philly shell** | Block | Lead shoulder high, rear hand at the jaw, lead arm across the body. Elite counter-striking shell — and a bad shape to defend a takedown from. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Parry** | Deflect | Small redirection of a straight punch with the palm. Cheap, fast, keeps you in range to counter. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Catch** | Deflect | Receiving a straight punch on the rear glove. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Slip** | Head movement | Moving the head off the line of a straight punch. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Roll / bob and weave** | Head movement | Rolling under a hook. Excellent against punches, dangerous against knees and takedowns. | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| **Pull / lean back** | Head movement | Withdrawing the head just out of range. Requires distance judgement most people don't have. | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| **Level change under a strike** | Head movement | Ducking a punch into a shot — the defense that is also an offense. 🔒 | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Check (low kick)** | Block | Turning the shin outward to meet the kick with bone. The only real answer to leg kicks. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Catch the kick** | Deflect | Trapping a body kick in the arm, then sweeping, punching, or taking them down. 🔒 | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Teep / knee as an interception** | Distance | Meeting a forward step with a front kick or a raised knee. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Step-back and reset** | Distance | Taking a full step out of range and re-establishing your stance. | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| **Angle off** | Distance | Leaving the line by stepping to the side rather than backwards. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Clinch entry as defense** | Range change | Closing the distance until strikes have no room. The wrestler's answer to a striker. 🔒 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Shell and return** | Block | Covering deliberately to draw a combination, then countering the gap at the end of it. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Framing on the head or biceps** | Block | Bone frames in the clinch and on the ground to deny elbow and knee range. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Covering on the ground** | Block | Elbow-and-forearm shell under ground and pound while you work your hips. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **The takedown tax** | — | Not a technique. Every defensive choice above must also survive a level change — which is why boxing head movement, used unmodified in MMA, gets people taken down. | 4 | 5 | 4 |

## Part VII — Combinations, feints and counters

| Item | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **1-2 (jab–cross)** | The spine of striking. Everything else is a variation. | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| **1-2-3 (jab–cross–hook)** | Straight, straight, around. Beats a fighter who blocks in straight lines. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **Jab–cross–low kick** | The MMA combination. The kick lands because the hands made them cover high. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Low kick–high kick** | The same delivery, two heights. The oldest trick in Thai boxing and still works. | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| **Body–head split** | Punch the body, punch the head, in either order. Hands cannot be in two places. | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| **Feint high, strike low** | A shoulder or hand feint that lifts their guard, then the leg or the level change. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Feint the shot, throw the hand** | A level-change feint that makes them post their hands down, then the overhand. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Strike into the takedown** | Any punch used as cover to change levels. The single most reliable takedown entry in MMA. 🔒 | 3 | 5 | 3 |
| **Takedown into the strike** | The threat used the other way — they sprawl, and you come up with the uppercut. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Counter over the jab** | Rear hand over the top of their extended lead. The most common knockdown in the sport. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Counter under the hook** | Slipping inside and answering with an uppercut or a shot. | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| **Intercepting knee** | Meeting a forward level change with a knee. Effective, and one of the easiest fouls to commit by accident. | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| **The catch-and-return** | Receive their strike deliberately, then return before they reset. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Blitz** | Multi-strike burst covering distance in a straight line. Karate-derived, and it works until they circle. | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| **Open-stance cross-body kick** | Rear kick to the open side against a southpaw or orthodox mismatch. Free damage most fighters never take. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Reading the tell** | Naming the thing they do before each attack — a dipped shoulder, a widened stance, a held breath — and hitting it. | 5 | 4 | 2 |

## Part VIII — The clinch

The clinch is a position, not a pause. Whoever understands that wins it.

| Position | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Collar tie (single)** | One hand on the back of the neck. Controls posture, sets snap-downs and knees. | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| **Double collar tie (Thai plum)** | Both hands behind the neck, elbows squeezed. Owns the head; loses the hips. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Underhook (single)** | Arm under theirs to the shoulder blade. The most valuable grip in the sport. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Double underhooks** | Both arms in, hands clasped. Takedowns, lifts, and fence pressure. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Over-under** | One in, one out. The neutral clinch, and where most cage exchanges live. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Whizzer / overhook** | Overhook on their underhook, hips heavy. Defensive, and a real counter-attacking grip. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Body lock (front)** | Hands clasped around their waist. Takedowns, lifts, and complete control of the hips. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Rear body lock** | Same, from behind. Near-total control; the most efficient path to the back. | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **Russian tie / 2-on-1** | Two hands on one arm. Drags, back-takes and takedowns from a low-risk grip. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Wrist control** | Cheapest control in the game. Kills their punch before it exists. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Cage clinch (pinning)** | Any of the above with their back on the fence. Doubles the value of every grip. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Head position** | Forehead against their cheek or jaw. Not a grip — the thing that makes grips work. | 2 | 5 | 1 |
| **Pummelling** | Alternating underhooks to regain inside position. The clinch's basic literacy. | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| **Dirty boxing** | Striking with one hand while controlling with the other. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Framing and separating** | Forearm frames into the neck or hip to create the space to leave. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Breaking grips** | Attacking the thumb, two-on-one, and hip movement — the same skill as gi grip fighting, without cloth. | 2 | 4 | 1 |

## Part IX — Takedowns in MMA

Only a subset of [`jujitsu.md` Part III](jujitsu.md#part-iii--takedowns) survives punches. These are the ones that do, plus the MMA-only entries.

| Takedown | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Double leg** | Level change, penetration step, both legs, drive through the corner. | 3 | 5 | 4 |
| **Reactive double off a strike** | The same shot hidden behind a punch, at the moment their weight is committed. 🔒 | 3 | 5 | 3 |
| **High single** | One leg held at chest height, finished with a run-the-pipe, trip, or dump. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **Low single** | Attacking one ankle from a deep level change. Fast, and hard to guillotine. | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| **High crotch** | Head-inside single entry, arm between the legs, elevate and turn the corner. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Body lock takedown** | Clasp the waist, break their posture to one side, trip or dump. The most MMA-friendly takedown there is. 🔒 | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Cage-assisted body lock** | Their back on the fence, hips in, walk their legs out. Where most modern MMA takedowns actually happen. | 3 | 5 | 1 |
| **Chain wrestling on the fence** | Double → single → body lock → trip, cycling until one works. The real skill, more than any single entry. | 4 | 4 | 2 |
| **Knee tap** | With an underhook, drive across while your hand blocks the far knee. Short, safe, high percentage. 🔒 | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Ankle pick** | Post the head one way, pick the same-side ankle the other. Low commitment. 🔒 | 2 | 3 | 2 |
| **Duck under** | Lift their elbow, level change through the window, arrive at the back. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Snap down to the front headlock** | Not a takedown — the thing that happens when their posture breaks. Feeds go-behinds and chokes. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Arm drag to the back** | Drag one arm across, take the back standing. Highest-value outcome available in the clinch. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Outside trip (osoto-style)** | Load their weight over one heel and reap it. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Inside trip (ouchi-style)** | Hook the near leg from inside and drive backwards. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Foot sweep** | Sweeping the foot as their weight lands. Free when it works, nothing when it doesn't. | 4 | 2 | 2 |
| **Hip throw from the clinch** | Judo hip throws with no gi. Rare, and enormous when a judoka lands one. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Catch-kick takedown** | Catch their body kick and run them down or sweep the standing leg. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Suplex** | Rear body lock, arch, land them. Legal. Also the fastest way to injure both of you. | 4 | 1 | 5 |
| **Guard pull** | Legal, almost never correct in MMA — you concede position, the ladder, and the round. | 1 | 1 | 5 |
| **Spiking / piledriving** ⛔ | Banned. Any throw that lands them on the crown of the head. | — | 1 | 5 |

## Part X — Takedown defense in MMA

| Defense | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Stance and motion** | Not being where the shot is going. The first and best defense. | 2 | 5 | 1 |
| **Hand fighting / posting** | Beating their grips and framing at the head before the level change. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Down block** | Palm to their forehead or shoulder as they change levels. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Sprawl** | Hips to the mat, legs back, chest heavy, then **circle**. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Crossface** | Forearm across their face to turn their head and kill the drive. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Whizzer** | Overhook plus hips back against a single. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Limp arm** | Slipping the arm free when they have an underhook. | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| **Hip heist / hip switch** | Turning the hips through to face them after a partial shot. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Front headlock** | Catching the head and arm off their failed shot. Feeds guillotines, D'Arces and back takes. 🔒 | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **Guillotine threat on the shot** | Threatening the choke to make them let go. Effective; costs you the position if you commit and miss. | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| **Underhook and turn the corner** | Getting the underhook on the fence and circling out. The core wall-wrestling escape. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Wall walk** | Back to the fence, frames in, feet under you, slide up without turning away. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Tricep pinch / arm-frame escape** | Pinching their arm to your side to break a body lock on the fence. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Fighting the hands (clasp break)** | Attacking the clasp of a body lock before the takedown exists. | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **Knee shield to stand** | Inserting a knee frame and standing rather than accepting bottom position. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Kimura counter to the single** | Trapping the far arm as they hold your leg — ends the takedown and starts a submission. | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| **Going out the back door** | Spinning behind them as their shot passes. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Sit to guard** ⚠ | Sitting down and closing guard rather than losing the scramble. In sport BJJ this is neutral. **In MMA it hands them the top position and the round.** | 1 | 3 | 5 |
| **Grabbing the fence** ⛔ | Illegal. Also the most-committed foul in the sport, and it is always seen on the replay. | — | 3 | 4 |

## Part XI — Cage craft

The cage is not a boundary. It's the third fighter, and it works for whoever understands it.

| Item | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Centre control** | Occupying the middle and making them travel. Scores with judges, and it is not the same as pressure. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **Cutting the cage** | Stepping to their escape route rather than following them. How a pressure fighter traps a mover. | 4 | 4 | 2 |
| **Circling out** | Leaving the fence toward their weak side before the grips exist. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Framing off the fence** | Forearm frames to create the inch you need to circle. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Pinning with the head** | Head under their jaw, hips in, chest heavy — the cheapest way to hold somebody on the fence. | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **Fence-assisted stand-up** | Using the cage to climb back to your feet under pressure. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Fence-assisted takedown** | Using the fence as a wall that their hips cannot escape through. | 3 | 5 | 1 |
| **Knee-tap and trip on the fence** | Short takedowns available only because they cannot step backwards. | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **The referee restart** | Knowing when a stalled position gets stood up, and using the clock accordingly. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Fighting off your back near the fence** | Your hips against the cage remove the space every escape needs. Get your head to the centre. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **Corner-to-corner escapes** | Recognising when you are being walked into a corner two steps before you arrive. | 4 | 4 | 3 |

## Part XII — Top game and ground and pound

Top position in MMA is a damage position first and a submission position second. The whole game is the trade between **posture** (which lets you hit) and **pressure** (which stops them escaping).

| Item | Position | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Posture in closed guard** | Their guard | Spine straight, hips back, hands on their biceps or chest. Everything starts here. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **Posture-and-strike** | Their guard | Rising to strike, dropping to re-control. The rhythm of ground and pound. | 3 | 5 | 3 |
| **Pressure-and-pass** | Their guard | Staying heavy and passing instead of striking. Safer, slower, better against a good guard. | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| **Wrist pin to strike** | Any top position | Pinning one wrist to the mat and hitting the exposed side. The highest-percentage GnP setup there is. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Half guard top GnP** | Half guard | Crossface and underhook, short elbows, free the leg last. | 3 | 5 | 1 |
| **Side control GnP** | Side control | Knees and elbows to the body, short elbows to the head, hips heavy. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Knee-on-belly strikes** | KOB | Posture tall, punches down, hop to mount when they turn. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Mount GnP** | Mount | Ride high, hands free, and force them to choose between the neck and the strikes. | 2 | 5 | 2 |
| **S-mount to strikes** | Mount | Riding one leg high to kill the buck and open the head. | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| **Back control GnP** | Back | Punches into the side of the head to force the neck open for the choke. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Crucifix** | Crucifix | Both their arms trapped, one free hand. The most one-sided position in the sport. | 4 | 2 | 2 |
| **Standing over the guard** | Their guard | Disengaging to your feet to strike down, or to force the referee to stand it up. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **The stand-up decision** | Any | Whether to stay on top or let them up and strike. Round score, clock, and who is winning standing all decide it. | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| **Ground elbows** | Any top position | The most damaging legal ground strike, and the one that ends fights on cuts. 🩸 🅐 | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Strikes to a turtled opponent** | Turtle top | Legal to the body and the side of the head; **not** to the back of the head. | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Passing under strikes** | Any | Guard passing where the guard player also has to defend their face — see [`jujitsu.md` Part V](jujitsu.md#part-v--guard-passing) for the passes themselves. | 3 | 4 | 2 |

## Part XIII — Bottom game under strikes

Everything in [`jujitsu.md` Part VI](jujitsu.md#part-vi--sweeps-and-reversals) and [Part VII](jujitsu.md#part-vii--escapes-and-survival) applies here, with one enormous change: **you are being hit, and the clock is not your friend.** Bottom is a place you leave.

| Item | What it is | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **Punch-block series** | High frame on their bicep, other forearm across, knees in, hips moving. The fundamental survival shape. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Closed guard with posture control** | Ankles crossed high, head pulled down, wrists controlled. Neutralises striking almost entirely. | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| **Wrist control from bottom** | Owning one of their hands means owning half their offense. | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Knee shield half guard** | The best bottom position in MMA: distance, frames, and a path to standing. 🔒 | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Butterfly / wrestle-up** | Hooks in, come up to a single. The main scoring action from bottom. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Technical stand-up** | Post, kick through, stand facing them. The correct answer far more often than any sweep. | 1 | 5 | 2 |
| **Wall-assisted stand-up** | The same movement using the fence, under pressure. | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| **Hip escape to knee shield** | Re-inserting the frame that makes everything else possible. | 2 | 5 | 1 |
| **Up-kicks and up-elbows** | Legal strikes from the bottom at a standing or posturing opponent. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **Overhook / underhook from bottom** | Killing their striking arm from underneath while you work your hips. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **Rubber guard and high guard** | Real submission attacks that also control posture — and a real risk of holding on while you get hit. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **Open guard under strikes** ⚠ | Everything spider, lasso, DLR and X — excellent in sport grappling, and mostly a way to get punched in MMA. | 3 | 2 | 5 |
| **Inverting** ⚠ | Do not invert under strikes. This is the clearest sport-to-fight translation failure in the sport. | 4 | 1 | 5 |
| **Covering and recovering** | Shell up, survive the flurry, then move — in that order, never simultaneously. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **Getting up rather than sweeping** | The strategic default of MMA bottom play, and the thing sport grapplers take longest to accept. | 3 | 4 | 2 |

## Part XIV — Submissions that work in MMA

Everything in [`jujitsu.md` Part VIII](jujitsu.md#part-viii--submissions) exists in MMA. Only some of it works. Gloves, sweat, strikes and short rounds change the arithmetic — `Com` here is how often it actually finishes fights, not how often it appears in a gym.

| Submission | Why it works, or doesn't, in MMA | Diff | Com | Risk |
|---|---|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| **[Rear naked choke](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | The single most common submission finish in MMA. No grips needed, and the back is easier to take when they are hurt. | 2 | 5 | 1 |
| **[Guillotine](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Second most common. Punishes the lazy shot, and the shot happens constantly. | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| **[Arm triangle](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Excellent — sweat helps it, and mount and side control are common finishing positions. | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| **[Triangle choke](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Works, but posture control under strikes is the hard part, not the legs. | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| **[D'Arce](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** and **[anaconda](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Front-headlock chokes off failed shots. Tailor-made for MMA. | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **[Armbar](jujitsu.md#viiib--arm-and-shoulder-locks)** | Common, especially from mount and from bottom guard against a posturing striker. Sweat and gloves make it slip. | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| **[Kimura](jujitsu.md#viiib--arm-and-shoulder-locks)** | More valuable as a control and a sweep than as a finish. | 2 | 3 | 2 |
| **[Americana](jujitsu.md#viiib--arm-and-shoulder-locks)** | Rare as a finish; a good way to make them turn into a choke. | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| **[Heel hook](jujitsu.md#viiic--leg-locks)** | Legal and increasingly common as leg-lock literacy spreads. Enormous damage, very little warning. | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| **[Kneebar](jujitsu.md#viiic--leg-locks)** and **[toe hold](jujitsu.md#viiic--leg-locks)** | Legal, occasional, usually from a scramble rather than a hunt. | 3 | 2 | 4 |
| **[Straight ankle lock](jujitsu.md#viiic--leg-locks)** | Legal and safe to attempt — but giving up top position for it, under strikes, rarely pays. | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| **[Von Flue choke](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | The punishment for holding a guillotine too long. Pure MMA and grappling crossover. | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| **[North–south choke](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Works, rare, needs a specific scramble to arrive. | 4 | 1 | 2 |
| **[Peruvian necktie](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles)** | Front-headlock finish off a stalled shot. | 4 | 1 | 3 |
| **[Twister](jujitsu.md#viiid--spinal-locks-and-cranks)** | Legal in MMA (unlike IBJJF), and about as rare as a finish gets. | 5 | 1 | 3 |
| **Neck cranks** | Legal in MMA. Effective, and hard on your training partners — drill them with care or not at all. | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| **Gi-dependent chokes** | Every collar and lapel choke in [`jujitsu.md`](jujitsu.md#viiia--chokes-and-strangles) — there is no cloth in MMA. Ignore them for this sport. | — | 1 | — |

**The pattern:** *chokes finish, joint locks tap*. And in MMA, the positions that produce chokes — the back and the front headlock — are the same positions that produce damage. That is not a coincidence; it is the argument for building your grappling around them.

## Part XV — Styles and what each one gives you

| Style | What it contributes | What it leaves you exposed to |
|---|---|---|
| **Boxing** | Hands, head movement, combination punching, distance in punching range | Kicks and takedowns — boxing head movement without modification gets you taken down |
| **Muay Thai** | Kicks, knees, elbows, the clinch, and durability | Level changes; the classic Thai stance is heavy on the lead leg |
| **Kickboxing / Dutch** | Combination striking that flows punch-to-kick | Same as boxing, plus fewer clinch answers |
| **Karate (Shotokan, Kyokushin)** | Distance, explosive linear entries, unusual angles and timing | Sustained pressure and the clinch |
| **Taekwondo** | Kicking variety, head-kick speed, switch and spinning attacks | Hands, and a base that is easy to take down |
| **Freestyle / folkstyle wrestling** | Takedowns, takedown defense, scrambles, top control, pace | Submissions and strikes — a wrestler's stance invites the guillotine |
| **Greco-Roman wrestling** | The clinch, body locks, throws, upper-body control on the fence | Leg attacks and defending them |
| **Judo** | Throws, grip fighting, and the best breakfalls in combat sport | No gi means most grips vanish |
| **[Brazilian jiu-jitsu](jujitsu.md)** | Submissions, guard, escapes, and the ability to be safe on the bottom | Sport habits that get you hit — see [Part XIII](#part-xiii--bottom-game-under-strikes) |
| **Sambo** | Throws plus leg locks, and a stance already built for both | Striking, in most sambo backgrounds |
| **Catch wrestling** | Top pressure, cranks, and a submission game that doesn't need a guard | Modern leg-lock and guard systems |
| **Nothing — trained MMA from day one** | No translation problems, no habits to unlearn, coherent from the start | Depth: specialists will beat you inside their own range until you build one |

> **The honest summary:** there is no best base. There is only what you have and what you're missing. Wrestling is the most *convenient* base because it decides where the fight happens — but a wrestler who can't strike is a fighter with one plan, and one plan is not a game.

## Part XVI — Fight IQ

The part that decides fights between fighters of equal skill, and the part nearly every gym under-teaches.

| Item | What it means |
|---|---|
| **The game plan** | Three sentences, not thirty: where you want the fight, how you get it there, and what you do when it goes to their range. If it doesn't fit on a card, you will not remember it in round two. |
| **Reading tendencies** | Every fighter has a rhythm and a tell. Finding it in round one is worth more than any technique you own. |
| **The adjustment** | The ability to change what you are doing between rounds, on instruction, under fatigue. Rarer than it should be. |
| **Round management** | Knowing whether you are winning the round, and what the last 30 seconds are worth to the judges. |
| **Damage economy** | Not every exchange is worth having. A fighter who takes three to give two is losing, however aggressive it looks. |
| **Fighting from behind** | Needing a finish changes correct play. Knowing *when* you need one is the skill. |
| **Fighting ahead** | Protecting a lead without stalling into a penalty or a stand-up. |
| **Pace as a weapon** | Choosing a rate of exchange your opponent cannot sustain, and being able to sustain it yourself. |
| **The corner** | One instruction between rounds, given clearly, is worth more than five shouted ones. This is a skill for the corner *and* for the fighter. |
| **Composure after getting hurt** | Recognising a flash knockdown, covering, recovering, and not chasing. The most valuable 20 seconds in the sport. |
| **The finish instinct** | Recognising a hurt opponent and closing — without abandoning your defense to do it. |
| **Knowing the criteria** | Judges score effective striking and grappling first, then control, then aggression. A fighter who doesn't know this loses decisions they earned. |

## Part XVII — Rules, fouls and scoring

> Rules vary by commission, promotion, and amateur vs professional. This reflects the **Unified Rules of Mixed Martial Arts** as generally adopted. **Check the exact ruleset for your event — this is a study aid, not a legal document.**

### The fouls

| Category | Fouls |
|---|---|
| **Head and neck** | Headbutting · strikes to the spine or back of the head · throat strikes and grabbing the trachea · knees or kicks to the head of a **grounded** opponent · stomping a grounded opponent |
| **Eyes, face, hands** | Eye gouging · fish-hooking · biting · spitting · hair pulling · clawing or pinching · small-joint (finger) manipulation |
| **Body** | Groin strikes · heel kicks to the kidney · downward (12-to-6) elbows *(historically banned; several commissions have amended this — verify for your event)* |
| **Throws** | Spiking or piledriving onto the head or neck · throwing an opponent out of the cage |
| **Holding** | Grabbing the fence · holding the shorts or gloves |
| **Conduct** | Timidity or avoiding contact · faking an injury · attacking during a break or after the bell · attacking an opponent under the referee's care · ignoring the referee · abusive language · unsportsmanlike conduct causing injury · corner interference · applying a foreign substance |

**The "grounded opponent" definition is the most-argued rule in MMA** and it differs between commissions — most commonly, any part of the body other than the soles of the feet touching the mat. Know which version is in force before you throw a knee.

### The scorecard (10-point must system)

| Score | When |
|:-:|---|
| **10–9** | The standard round. One fighter did more. |
| **10–8** | Clear dominance — significant damage, or overwhelming control and volume. Rarer than it should be, and awarded more often than it used to be. |
| **10–7** | Total dominance with damage. Very rare. |
| **10–10** | Genuinely nothing between them. Judges are discouraged from using it. |

**Judging criteria, in order:** effective striking and grappling → effective aggressiveness → control of the fighting area. *Damage is the primary consideration within effective striking and grappling.* Control that produces nothing scores below damage that does.

### Weight classes (professional, Unified Rules)

| Class | Upper limit |
|---|---|
| Strawweight | 115 lb / 52.2 kg |
| Flyweight | 125 lb / 56.7 kg |
| Bantamweight | 135 lb / 61.2 kg |
| Featherweight | 145 lb / 65.8 kg |
| Lightweight | 155 lb / 70.3 kg |
| Welterweight | 170 lb / 77.1 kg |
| Middleweight | 185 lb / 83.9 kg |
| Light heavyweight | 205 lb / 93.0 kg |
| Heavyweight | 265 lb / 120.2 kg |

The full ABC list also defines intermediate classes (super lightweight, super welterweight, super middleweight, cruiserweight) that most promotions do not contest. Title fights are five rounds; other bouts are three. Rounds are five minutes with one minute between.

### Amateur MMA — what changes

| Change | Why |
|---|---|
| **No elbows to the head** (most jurisdictions) | Cuts end amateur fights and amateur cards have no cutmen |
| **Shin guards, sometimes headgear** | Injury reduction |
| **No knees to the head, in some rulesets** | Same |
| **Shorter rounds in some sanctioning bodies** | Conditioning and safety |

If you intend to compete, learn your sanctioning body's rules **before** the camp, not during the fight-week rules meeting. Fighters lose amateur bouts to fouls they didn't know existed.

## Part XVIII — Physical preparation and damage management

| Area | What matters | The honest note |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy systems** | MMA is repeated high-intensity bursts on an aerobic base. Both matter; the aerobic base is what lets you repeat the bursts. | You cannot out-condition bad technique, but bad conditioning makes good technique unavailable in round three. |
| **Strength** | Hinge, squat, push, pull, carry, and rotational power. Twice a week is plenty in camp. | Lifting is preparation, not the sport. It should never eat skill sessions. |
| **Neck training** | Isometrics and controlled range work in every direction. | The single most valuable prehab in combat sport — for both concussion resilience and cranks. |
| **Grip and hands** | Grip work, finger care, and wrapping properly every time. | Hand injuries end more camps than knees do. |
| **Mobility** | Hips and thoracic spine above all. | Kick height and guard retention are both hip-mobility problems wearing a technique costume. |
| **Recovery** | Sleep 7–9 hours, eat enough, and take a full deload week every 6–8 weeks. | Sleep is the highest-return intervention available to any fighter, and the first thing camps sacrifice. |
| **Weight management** | Compete at a weight you can reach with nutrition and normal training. Work with a qualified professional — a dietitian or a doctor — for anything beyond that. | Extreme dehydration cuts have killed fighters. A cut that leaves you flat is worse than fighting a division up, and the medical risk is real, not theoretical. Do not improvise this from the internet. |
| **Head trauma** | Cap hard sparring volume, use headgear where appropriate, take real time after any knockdown or concussion, and be honest about symptoms. | The evidence on repeated sub-concussive impacts is serious enough that the burden of proof runs the other way. Sparring hard every week is a choice about your brain, not about toughness. |
| **Cuts and skin** | Wash gear after every session, cover open wounds, treat skin infections immediately. | Staph and ringworm take out whole gyms and whole camps. |
| **Eyes, ears, joints** | Eye protection habits, ear drainage or headgear, knee and shoulder prehab. | Cauliflower ear is a choice; nobody tells beginners that until it's set. |
| **Camp structure** | 8–10 weeks: general preparation → specific preparation → sharpening → taper. Hard sparring peaks in the middle, not the end. | The last two weeks are for sharpening and rest. Nobody has ever been saved by a hard spar in fight week. |

## Part XIX — The syllabus: what to teach and when

> Ordered by when a fighter needs it, not by how impressive it looks. Each tier assumes the one before it.

### Tier 0 — Before you are hit (first session)

Mat and cage etiquette · how to tap and how to release · glove, wrap and mouthguard basics · hygiene and skin checks · the injury conversation *before* the round · **what a light spar means, agreed out loud, every time** · the difference between training and fighting.

### Tier 1 — Movement (weeks 1–4, forever after)

Stance · step-and-slide · pivots · circling · level change · sprawl · technical stand-up · breakfalls · shadowboxing with intent.

### Tier 2 — Not getting hurt (weeks 1–8) — *the highest-value tier*

| Skill | Content |
|---|---|
| **Striking defense** | High guard, parry, catch, check, step-back, angle off |
| **Takedown defense** | Sprawl, crossface, whizzer, front headlock, hand fighting |
| **Wall defense** | Underhook, wall walk, framing, circling out |
| **Ground survival** | Punch-block series, knee shield, covering, technical stand-up |
| **Submission defense** | See [`jujitsu.md` Part VII.b](jujitsu.md#viib--submission-defense) — chokes first |

### Tier 3 — The basic weapons (weeks 4–12)

Jab · cross · lead hook · low kick · calf kick · teep · body kick · knee from the collar tie · double leg · high single · body lock takedown · the four-punch and punch-kick combinations that connect them.

### Tier 4 — Positions (weeks 8–20)

Clinch positions and pummelling · cage clinch both ways · top position and posture · half guard top and bottom · closed guard under strikes · back control · the [BJJ positional ladder](jujitsu.md#part-i--the-map), re-valued for strikes.

### Tier 5 — Damage (weeks 12+)

Ground and pound from every top position · elbows · knees to the body · wrist pinning · the posture-and-strike rhythm · the stand-up decision.

### Tier 6 — Finishing (weeks 16+)

Chokes first — rear naked choke, guillotine, arm triangle, D'Arce · then arm locks · then legs · then the strike-to-submission and submission-to-strike chains.

### Tier 7 — The full striking game (months 6+)

Elbows · spinning attacks · switch kicks · the counter game · open-stance tactics · feint systems · pressure and the blitz.

### Tier 8 — Competition (before any fight)

Rules and fouls · scoring criteria · weigh-in and fight-week procedure · corner communication · warm-up protocol · sparring taper · amateur ruleset differences · how to lose gracefully.

### Tier 9 — Career and body (from month one, forever)

Sparring volume and head-trauma management · weight management with a professional · sleep and recovery · injury reporting · note-taking after every session · video study of yourself, not just of champions · choosing a style that fits your body · knowing when a camp is going badly and saying so.

## Part XX — Session templates and sparring formats

### The 90-minute MMA session

| Minutes | Block |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Warm-up: movement, shadow, mobility specific to today's material |
| 10–20 | Striking technique — one or two things, taught in layers |
| 20–35 | Striking drilling: pads, partner reps, then light resistance |
| 35–50 | Grappling or clinch technique for the day, drilled the same way |
| 50–65 | Integration: the thing that connects them (strike-to-takedown, takedown-to-strike, wall to ground) |
| 65–85 | Situational sparring — see the formats below |
| 85–90 | Cool-down, notes, injury check |

### The weekly split (4 sessions)

| Session | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| 1 | Striking + takedown defense |
| 2 | Wrestling and clinch + top game |
| 3 | Grappling and submissions + bottom game |
| 4 | Integration and situational sparring |

Strength work twice a week, on separate days or after skill sessions — never before them.

### Sparring formats

**This table is the most important one in the file for your long-term health.** "Spar" is not an instruction. Name the format and the intensity out loud before the round starts.

| Format | What it is | Intensity | What it trains |
|---|---|:-:|---|
| **Technical / flow sparring** | Continuous exchange, everything at 30–40%, nothing landed hard | 30–40% | Volume, timing, ring craft — the format most fighters should do most of the time |
| **Positional sparring** | Start from one situation — back to the fence, one in the clinch, one on the bottom — reset every 90 s | 50–70% | The specific position, at three times the reps of a free round |
| **Task sparring** | One fighter has an objective (get the takedown; stay standing; work only the jab) | 50–70% | Making a specific tool appear under pressure |
| **Limited-weapons sparring** | Boxing only · kicks only · clinch only · grappling only · takedowns only | 50–70% | Depth in one range without the others rescuing you |
| **One-way sparring** | Only one fighter attacks; the other defends | 60% | Defense, and safe rounds for the less experienced partner |
| **Handicap sparring** | Higher-level fighter constrained — one hand, defense only, starts in a bad position | 60–80% | Real rounds for mismatched pairs |
| **Situational restarts** | Coach resets the position every 60–90 s regardless of what's happening | 70% | Fight rhythm and the removal of stalling |
| **Hard sparring** | Competitive rounds at fight pace | 80–90% | Fight readiness — **capped, scheduled, and never the default** |
| **Fight simulation** | Full rounds, fight-length, with a corner and a clock | 80–90% | Everything at once, mid-camp only |
| **Shark tank** | Fresh opponents every round against one fighter | 90% | Conditioning and composure. Peak weeks only, never for beginners |

> **The sparring rule this file will die on:** hard sparring is a small, deliberate part of a camp — not a weekly ritual. The fighters with the longest careers are the ones who did the least unnecessary damage in the gym. Flow sparring builds skill; hard sparring only tests it, and the test is expensive.

## Part XXI — Gap analysis: what MMA gyms leave out

### XXI.a — Things missing from almost every MMA gym

| Gap | Why it gets left out | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **The transitions between ranges** | Each coach teaches their own range | Almost every knockout and takedown happens *between* ranges, not inside one |
| **Wall wrestling as its own subject** | It isn't striking or grappling, so it belongs to nobody | A large share of modern MMA happens on the fence, and most gyms have no curriculum for it |
| **Getting up off the bottom** | BJJ coaches teach guard; wrestling coaches don't teach bottom | The correct MMA answer to being on the bottom is usually to stand, and almost nobody drills it |
| **Striking-aware grappling** | The BJJ class doesn't have punches in it | A guard game built without strikes will not survive them |
| **Grappling-aware striking** | The striking class doesn't have takedowns in it | Head movement and stance that ignore the level change get you taken down |
| **Sparring intensity control** | Considered a soft skill | It is the difference between a ten-year career and a three-year one |
| **Rules literacy** | Assumed | Fighters lose to fouls and to judging criteria they never studied |
| **The corner as a skill** | Coaches assume they're good at it | One clear instruction between rounds is a trainable skill and most corners are noise |
| **Damage economy** | Aggression is easier to praise | Fighters are rewarded for toughness and punished by their thirties |
| **Fighting hurt** | Uncomfortable to train | Recognising a flash knockdown and recovering is a skill, not a personality trait |
| **Weight management done properly** | Everyone thinks they know it | It has killed people. It needs a professional, not gym folklore |
| **Note-taking and self-video** | Feels like homework | Watching your own tape is the fastest correction loop available, and it's free |

### XXI.b — Domains this file under-serves

| Domain | What's missing | Where to go |
|---|---|---|
| **Grappling depth** | The full technique atlas — 300+ positions, escapes, sweeps, passes and submissions | [`jujitsu.md`](jujitsu.md) and the [technique library](techniques/) |
| **A session-by-session program** | The day-to-day schedule | [`training-plan.md`](training-plan.md) for an 8-week MMA camp; [`complete-program.md`](complete-program.md) for the 96-session grappling program |
| **Pad work systems** | Thai pads, focus mitts, and the coaching skill of holding them well | A striking coach — this genuinely cannot be learned from a document |
| **Amateur pathway** | Sanctioning bodies, medicals, licensing, matchmaking | Your local commission |
| **Women's MMA** | Divisional realities, strength-disparity strategy, and the fact that most gym sparring pools are male | A gym that has actually thought about it |
| **Youth MMA** | Age-appropriate contact rules, and the argument for no head contact before adulthood | A coach with a youth qualification |
| **Officiating** | Refereeing and judging from the inside | A commission officials' course — a weekend of it will improve your fighting |

### XXI.c — The honest final gap

The list above is not the skill. A fighter who genuinely owns **a jab, a low kick, a level change, a sprawl, a wall escape, a top game with elbows, a punch-block series, a stand-up, a back take and a rear naked choke** will beat a fighter who can name everything in this file.

**Depth beats breadth. Defense beats offense. Getting up beats guard. Position beats damage, and damage beats control.**

Use this file to know what exists and to decide what to ignore. Use the gym, a coach who watches you, and a training partner who tells you the truth to actually get good.
