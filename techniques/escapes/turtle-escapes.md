# Turtle Escapes

> Turtle is a shelter with a clock on it. Everything on this page is about spending as little time there as possible — and about denying the seatbelt and the hooks before they exist, because after they exist you are reading a different page.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom turtle (**−1 to −4**, depending entirely on how fast you leave) |
| **Comes from** | A stuffed takedown, a sprawl, a scramble you lost, rolling away from side control or north–south, a broken guard retention |
| **Difficulty** | 2–3/5 · per escape, see §3 |
| **How common** | 3/5 · sit-out, granby and stand-up in base are each rated Com 3; turtle itself is rated Com 4 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** referee's position bottom, all fours, defensive turtle

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part II.b on turtle: *"Not a guard — a shelter. Learn it or you'll donate your back for years."* And Part XV.a lists turtle as one of the things missing from almost every syllabus: *"It looks like losing, so gyms skip it. Everyone ends up there. Untrained, it's a free back-take donation for your entire white-belt career."*

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Parts IV and VII.a.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Turtle is unusual in this library: it is not really *their* position, it is yours — a defensive shell you chose. What makes it dangerous is what they are building on top of it. **Turtle top / back exposure** is rated **Diff 2 / Com 4** and the atlas calls it *"a pivotal transitional spot that decides who gets the back."* The transition **turtle → back control** is rated **Diff 2 / Com 5** — one of the highest-frequency entries in the whole sport, and the reason turtle has such a bad reputation.

What the top player is actually building, in order:

1. **A waist grip or a body lock**, which stops you standing up and sets up the mat return.
2. **The seatbelt** — one arm over your shoulder, one under your far armpit. This is the real prize; once it is locked, your escapes shrink dramatically.
3. **The first hook**, which converts a ride into back control.
4. **Both hooks or a body triangle** — **+4**, four IBJJF points, and the [rear naked choke](../submissions/rear-naked-choke.md).

Running parallel to that is the **front headlock** system (atlas: Diff 3 / Com 4), which attacks from the other end: a chin strap or a hand under your chin plus an arm around one shoulder, leading to the [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md), the [darce](../submissions/darce.md), the [anaconda](../submissions/anaconda.md), the [peruvian necktie](../submissions/peruvian-necktie.md), and — in the gi — the clock choke.

**The one thing to take from this section:** turtle is not a bad position. Turtle is a *timed* position. A turtle you leave in three seconds is a neutral scramble; a turtle you sit in for thirty seconds is a back take with paperwork. Every escape here is fast, and the ones that are not fast are wrong.

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames.** Your frames in turtle are unusual: they are your **elbows against your own thighs** and your **forearm against their shin**. The elbow-to-thigh seal is what denies hooks — a hook needs a gap between your elbow and your leg to enter, and if there is no gap, there is no hook. It costs nothing to maintain and it is the highest-value thing you do in this position.

**Tight turtle, defined.** Knees under your hips (not stretched back), elbows on or inside your thighs, forehead up rather than tucked toward your chest, hands ready to protect your neck or post. Your spine should be a rounded shell over a compact base, not a long flat table.

**Elbow–knee connection.** Here it is literal: **your elbows are already touching your knees, and your job is to keep them there.** That is the whole defensive structure. Every hook, every crossface, every arm-drag onto your back begins with one of those two contacts breaking. When you feel a hand or a foot arriving on one side, that side's elbow should get *tighter*, not looser.

**Breathing under pressure.** Turtle is a compressed, folded position with a heavy person on top of your spine, and most people hold their breath the entire time. **Breathe into your back and your sides**, nasally, shallowly. If you cannot breathe in turtle, you cannot sit out of turtle either — and the atlas is direct that panic is mostly oxygen debt.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never flatten out.** Belly to the mat with your legs extended is a back take, a crucifix, and a truck all at once. If your hips are lower than your knees, fix it now.
- **Never extend an arm out to post away from your body.** A far-posted arm is the front headlock, the [darce](../submissions/darce.md) and the [anaconda](../submissions/anaconda.md). Post short, close, and with the elbow bent.
- **Never let your head get pushed below your hips.** A head lower than your hips means every choke in the front headlock family is available and none of your escapes are.
- **Never stay still.** Turtle rewards nothing over time. Stalling here is not defense, it is a delayed donation.
- **Never stand up with a body lock on.** You will be mat-returned, and often with more force than the original takedown.
- **Never turn your back to them to get up.** Standing up facing away is worse than turtle.

## 3. The escapes

### Denying the hooks and the seatbelt before they arrive

Not separately rated; this is prevention and it is worth more than every escape below combined.

1. **Elbows glued to your thighs.** A hook requires a gap. Deny the gap and the hook cannot enter regardless of how good they are.
2. **When you feel a foot arriving on one side, drop that hip to the mat.** Sit your weight down onto the side they are attacking. **A hook cannot go in underneath your own hip** — you have simply removed the place it goes. This is faster and more reliable than trying to fight the foot with a hand.
3. **Deny the seatbelt.** If an arm comes over your shoulder, both of your hands take it and pull it down to your chest, exactly as in [back escapes](./back-escapes.md). Two hands on one, always. Your near elbow stays pinned to your ribs so the under-arm has nowhere to thread.
4. **One hook without a seatbelt is not back control.** If they have a hook and no upper-body connection, move *now* — that is the most fragile version of the position they will ever have, and it lasts about a second.
5. **A seatbelt without hooks is the moment to roll.** Sit your hips down toward their underhooking side while turning your shoulders back toward their choking arm, so that arm ends up pinned between your back and the mat. **Your two ends go opposite ways.** If your shoulders and hips travel the same direction you have rotated in place and they are still on your back.
6. **The test at the end of any roll or sit-through: is their choking arm pinned under you, and are your hips outside theirs?** If yes, you went the right way, whatever anybody calls it.

### Sit-out

**Diff 3/5 · Com 3/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a and IV)

The wrestler's answer, and the best escape against a heavy, forward-pressuring opponent.

1. From a tight turtle with your weight balanced, post both hands.
2. **Pick the side away from their committed weight.** If they are driving over your right shoulder, sit out to your left.
3. **Kick your far leg through underneath you**, sitting your hip through to the mat, so your legs end up in front of you and your bum lands on the floor.
4. **Turn your shoulders the same way and look at them.** Your head comes up and around first — this is the step that matters.
5. Post your near hand, come up onto your hip, and finish facing them.
6. From there: seated guard, a single leg, a wrestle-up, or a front headlock of your own.

**The two details that matter:** *your head turns first — a sit-out with your head down is a [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md)* — and *do not sit out into the side where they already have a grip.*

### The switch

**Diff 3/5 · Com 2/5**

The sit-out's sibling. Where the sit-out takes you away from them, the switch takes you *under and behind* them.

1. From turtle with them riding your hips, **post one hand back between your own body and theirs.**
2. **Sit your hips through to that side** — a hip heist — turning your chest toward the ceiling and driving your hips underneath theirs.
3. As you sit through, **catch their near leg or their hips with your inside arm.**
4. Continue the rotation and come up over the top, arriving at their hip or on top.
5. If the reversal stalls, you are still facing them with your hips free — take the scramble.

**The detail that matters:** *your hips must clear before your shoulders turn.* Turning your chest first, without the hip heist, simply rolls you onto your back underneath them.

### Granby roll

**Diff 3/5 · Com 3/5**

1. **Prerequisite: their weight must be committed forward, over your shoulders, and your neck must be completely clear.** No chin strap, no arm around your head. If they have your neck, do a different escape.
2. Tuck your chin hard and load one shoulder.
3. **Roll over the shoulder — never the head or the neck.**
4. Kick your legs over as you invert, spinning your hips underneath them.
5. Come out facing them with your legs between you: seated guard, open guard, or out the back door.
6. If they are riding light or hip-heavy rather than shoulder-heavy, do not attempt this — you will roll straight into their front headlock.

The atlas rates inversion as a neck-loading movement to build slowly. If you cannot do a clean shoulder roll in the warm-up, do not do this one live.

### Standing up in base

**Diff 2/5 · Com 3/5** — the atlas calls it *"under-taught and effective"*, and it is right.

1. **Clear their hands off your waist first.** You cannot stand with a body lock on, and trying is how people get slammed back down. Peel one hand at a time, or step a leg outside theirs to break the lock's angle.
2. Bring one knee up so that foot is flat on the mat, hands still posted, elbows in.
3. **Drive up with your hips back and your head up.** Do not straighten your legs before your hips are underneath you.
4. **Turn to face them the instant you are up.** Standing with your back to someone is worse than turtle, not better.
5. Hand fight immediately — clear grips, establish your own, and re-set your stance (atlas Part IV: stance and motion prevents more takedowns than every technique below it combined).

**The two details that matter:** *hands off your waist before you stand*, and *turn to face them, always.*

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** Turtle is the position where the top player's **leg length** and **weight distribution** change your correct answer most sharply — and where the standard advice ("just sit out") is wrong about half the time.

The one-line version of this whole section:

> **Heavy, forward-pressuring riders: sit out. Light, mobile riders: stand up. Hip-heavy riders: switch. Choosing the wrong one against the wrong build is why your turtle escapes work on some people and never on others.**

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Long legs** | Their hooks reach around your hips from an angle a shorter opponent could not use, and the body triangle threat is real once they get the back | Seal the elbow-to-thigh gap earlier and tighter; sit a hip to the mat *proactively* rather than reactively; never fight a hook from all fours |
| **Much heavier than you** | They flatten you rather than ride you; standing up with them attached is impossible | Break the waist lock first, always; the sit-out becomes your primary *because* their forward pressure is committed; never carry their weight on a flat spine |
| **Much lighter than you** | They ride, spin and hunt hooks constantly instead of flattening you — and rotational escapes just feed their movement | Go vertical, not horizontal: stand up in base. Sit-outs and granbys against a light, mobile rider often hand them your back |
| **Very tall / long arms** | The seatbelt and the front headlock both arrive earlier, from further away, and the chin strap is nearly free for them | Head up and neck short; keep a hand at your own collar line whenever their arm is near your head; take the switch or the granby, since their base is high |
| **Short and stocky** | They ride your *hips* rather than your shoulders, so there is no forward weight for a sit-out to use | Break the waist lock and stand; if you must go horizontal, use the switch — it attacks the hips, which is where their weight actually is |
| **Very strong grippers** | A collar grip from turtle is a clock choke in progress, and a belt/waist lock kills the stand-up | Strip the collar grip **before** you move — movement tightens it for them. Attack the clasp, not the arms |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | Your size makes standing easy, but your bigger frame leaves physically bigger gaps for their hooks | Stand up as your default; pay *more* attention to the elbow–thigh seal, not less; control the stand so you don't drop them |

### Opponents with long legs

- **Their hooks arrive earlier and from further out.** A long-legged rider can insert a hook while their chest is still off to one side — a position from which a short-legged opponent has nothing. The practical consequence is that **"their weight isn't on me yet" is not evidence that you are safe.** Seal the gaps on the basis of where their legs are, not where their chest is.
- **Sit a hip to the mat proactively.** Against most opponents you can wait to feel the foot arrive. Against long legs, you should already be sitting one hip down on whichever side they are working, because by the time you feel the hook it is in. This costs you a little mobility and saves you the back.
- **Never fight a hook from all fours.** Reaching down with a hand to strip a long hook takes your elbow off your thigh and your hand off your neck, and their leg is longer than your arm. **Drop the hip instead** — a hook has nothing to hold when your hip is on the floor.
- **Take the trade upward.** Long-legged riders tend to ride high and light because their legs give them reach without needing to be heavy. A high ride is exactly what the **switch** and the **granby** are for. Take that trade; it is available against this build and not against a short, low rider.
- **If they get the back, read the body triangle section of [back escapes](./back-escapes.md) before you need it.** Long legs plus back control means a triangle, and a triangle is a five-minute problem rather than a one-minute one.

### Much heavier than you

- **Never carry their weight on a flat spine.** The first thing a much heavier rider will do is flatten you — driving your hips down and your legs back until you are lying face-down. From there you have no escapes and they have all of them. **Get your knees back under your hips, or sit one hip to the mat.** If you can only do one thing under a heavy rider, do this.
- **The sit-out is your primary, and this is the counterintuitive part: it gets *better* the heavier they are.** A heavy opponent controls turtle by driving forward pressure over your shoulders. The sit-out uses exactly that pressure — you remove the thing they are leaning on, and their own commitment carries them past you. Against a light, non-committal rider the sit-out has much less to work with. **So the escape that feels hardest against big people is actually the one designed for them.**
- **Break the waist lock before you consider standing.** Standing in base with a much heavier opponent's body lock attached is not an escape, it is a mat return with extra steps (atlas Part III.a rates the mat return Diff 2 / Com 3 — it is not a hard skill for them).
- **Their weight makes them slow to chase.** Once you commit to a sit-out against a heavy opponent, you will usually get all the way out, because they cannot follow a rotation quickly. The escape is more binary against heavy people — it works or it doesn't, rather than half-working.
- **The granby is available but expensive.** Heavy forward pressure is exactly the condition the granby wants, but it also means a lot of weight passing over your neck. Only with a genuinely clear neck and a controlled shoulder roll.

### Much lighter than you

- **They will ride, not flatten.** A lighter opponent cannot pin you, so they will stay light and mobile, spinning around your turtle and hunting for a hook or a front headlock. Everything about your escape selection changes as a result.
- **Stand up. This is the answer.** You can stand with a lighter person attached to you, and standing deletes every attack they have — no hooks, no seatbelt on the ground, no clock choke, no front headlock finish. Against a light, mobile rider, **going vertical beats going horizontal every time.**
- **Sit-outs and granbys often feed them.** This is the specific failure people do not expect: rotational escapes require you to turn, and a light, quick opponent can follow your rotation more easily than they could follow your weight. A granby against a small, fast rider is frequently just a slow way of handing over your back.
- **Grips matter more than pressure.** Their control is the waist lock and the seatbelt clasp, not their weight. Strip the connection and there is nothing left holding you.
- **Expect the front headlock family.** A lighter opponent will often attack from the front rather than the back, because the [darce](../submissions/darce.md), [anaconda](../submissions/anaconda.md), [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md) and [peruvian necktie](../submissions/peruvian-necktie.md) do not care about size. **Head up, neck short, elbows in.**

### Very tall opponents with long arms

- **The seatbelt and the chin strap arrive earlier.** Long arms mean they can reach your far armpit and your chin from an angle and a distance that a compact opponent cannot. You get less warning.
- **Keep your neck short and your head up.** Shrug your shoulders toward your ears in turtle against a long-armed opponent — it shortens the target and denies the chin strap the space it needs. And **keep one hand near your own collar line whenever their arm is anywhere near your head**; against this build, the front headlock is the more likely attack, not the back take.
- **Their base is high, so the switch and the granby find room.** A tall rider has to bend a long way down to be heavy, and most of the time they are not — which leaves space underneath for rotational escapes. This is the build where the granby is most likely to work.
- **Do not sit out toward their long-armed front headlock side.** Sitting out puts your head into the space their arm already occupies. Pick the other side.

### Short, stocky opponents

- **They ride your hips, not your shoulders.** A short, thick opponent cannot easily get their weight over your shoulders, so they will sit back on your hips and control your waist instead. This changes the whole calculation.
- **The sit-out is much weaker against this build**, for a simple reason: the sit-out borrows their forward commitment, and there isn't any. You kick your leg through and they are still sitting on your hips.
- **Use the switch instead.** The switch attacks the hips, which is exactly where their weight is. Sit through, catch their leg or hip, and come up. **Forward-weighted riders → sit out. Hip-weighted riders → switch.** That pairing is the single most useful thing on this page.
- **Break the waist lock and stand.** A short opponent's arms are shorter, so their body lock has less depth and breaks more easily than you would expect from how strong it feels.
- **Do not try to bump them off.** A low centre of gravity plus a wide base means nothing you do with your back will move them.

### Very strong grippers

- **In the gi, a collar grip from turtle is an emergency, not an inconvenience.** It is a clock choke (Diff 3 / Com 3) or a [bow-and-arrow](../submissions/bow-and-arrow.md) in progress, and here is the non-obvious part: **moving tightens it for them.** Every rotation you make while they hold your collar feeds them slack. **Strip the collar grip *before* you start any escape**, with two hands, using a body turn to create slack in the cloth first.
- **The waist lock is what kills the stand-up.** Attack the **clasp** — the point where their hands connect — rather than trying to peel a strong individual hand. A gable grip breaks at the seam.
- **Never grip fight with the hand that should be protecting your neck.** If one hand goes down to work, the other stays up.
- **No-gi:** their strong grip is the seatbelt clasp and the chin strap. Same rule — attack the connection, and get your chin down and your hands high before you worry about their arms.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **Standing up in base is your default and it is excellent.** A smaller rider cannot hold your hips down, cannot mat-return you reliably, and loses everything the moment you are vertical. Break the waist lock and stand.
- **But your bigger frame leaves bigger gaps.** This is the part people get wrong: the distance from your elbow to your thigh is physically greater than a smaller person's, which means the hole a hook can enter through is larger. **Hook denial takes more attention when you are bigger, not less** — and a smaller opponent's legs will find a gap on you that they could never find on someone their own size.
- **Do not rely on shrugging them off.** A light rider is not held on by weight, so removing them is not a weight problem. Use structure: seal the gaps, break the grip, stand.
- **Do not sit out onto a smaller partner's leg**, and do not switch through their knee. You will hyperextend something.
- **Safety on the stand-up.** If they cling to your back as you rise, you can drop them on their head. Stand with control, and if they are hanging on, lower them rather than shaking them off. This is a real injury mechanism and it is entirely on you.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Long limbs.** Your elbow-to-thigh gaps are larger and your turtle is naturally looser. Compensate by consciously compacting: knees further forward under your hips, elbows actively pressed in. Your reward is that the stand-up and the sit-out both have more range.
- **Short limbs.** Your turtle is naturally tighter and harder to hook — genuinely an advantage here. The cost is less reach on the sit-out; make the switch and the stand-up your primaries.
- **Long torso.** More distance between your shoulders and your hips means a rider can be heavy on one end and absent at the other. Read which end they are on and pick sit-out or switch accordingly.
- **Thick torso or restricted hip mobility.** The granby is probably not for you; skip it rather than doing a bad one. The stand-up asks almost nothing of your hips and is the better investment.
- **Strong wrestling background.** The sit-out and the switch are already yours; the thing to add is the **grip and neck discipline** that wrestling does not teach, because in jiu-jitsu the sit-out is punished by a guillotine rather than by two points.
- **Older or less explosive.** Stand up in base. It is the lowest-power escape on the page, the atlas already flags it as under-taught, and it removes every submission from the equation at once.
- **Previous neck injury.** No granby. Turtle already loads the neck under someone's weight; do not add an inversion to it.

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "They get a hook in every single time" | Your elbow is off your thigh, or you are trying to fight the hook with a hand from all fours | Seal the elbow to the thigh; when you feel a foot, drop that hip to the mat instead of reaching |
| "I sit out and they take my back" | Your head stayed down, or you sat out into the side they had committed to | Head and shoulders turn first, and sit out away from their weight and their grips |
| "I sit out and they're still on my hips" | You are sitting out against a hip-heavy rider, so there is no forward commitment to borrow | Switch instead. Sit-out uses their forward pressure; the switch attacks their hips |
| "I stand up and get slammed back down" | You stood with a body lock still attached | Break the waist grip first. Every time, without exception |
| "I granby and end up in a front headlock" | Their weight was not committed forward, or your neck was not clear | Granby only against committed forward pressure with a completely free neck |
| "I get flattened and can't do anything" | Your hips dropped below your knees and your spine went long | Knees back under your hips, or sit a hip to the mat. Fix the shape before you attempt anything |
| "They choked me from turtle before I moved (gi)" | You started escaping with their collar grip still on, which tightened it | Strip collar grips *before* moving. Movement is what finishes a clock choke |
| "Every escape works in drilling and not live" | You are too slow — you are treating turtle as a position to hold rather than to leave | Turtle is timed. Decide within about two seconds; a late correct escape is a wrong escape |
| "I'm exhausted after ten seconds of turtle" | You are holding your breath under compression | Nasal, shallow, into the back and sides. You cannot escape on empty lungs |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from turtle.** Top player starts riding with a waist grip, no seatbelt, no hooks. **Bottom wins by facing them or standing; top wins by getting the seatbelt plus one hook.** **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Note that the win conditions are both *intermediate* — that is deliberate, and it is what makes the round train the right moment.

**2. Hook denial, no escaping.** Top player does nothing but hunt hooks. Bottom player may not escape — the only job is to keep both elbows sealed and drop the correct hip. **60 seconds each, three rounds.** The single highest-return drill on this page.

**3. Escape selection round.** Do the same turtle round against three different partners: the heaviest person in the room, the lightest, and the tallest. Same starting position, same three escapes available. Then write down which escape worked against which build. You should independently rediscover the sit-out/stand-up/switch split from §4.

**4. Stand-up chain.** Partner holds a waist lock at 70%. You practise breaking the clasp and standing in base, then turning to face them. **Ten reps.** Most people have never practised the grip break, only the stand.

**5. Solo, in the warm-up.** Sit-outs and switches from a turtle base, ten each side, plus five granby rolls if your neck is happy. The atlas puts sit-through/hip heist in the Tier 1 movement alphabet for a reason — it belongs in every warm-up, not in a special class.

## 8. Safety

**Neck.** Turtle is a neck-loading position even when nothing is going wrong, and the granby adds an inversion on top of that. Roll over the **shoulder**, never the head. If you have any history of neck problems, take the granby out of your game entirely — the stand-up does the same job with none of the risk.

**Front headlock chokes.** The [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md), [darce](../submissions/darce.md), [anaconda](../submissions/anaconda.md) and [peruvian necktie](../submissions/peruvian-necktie.md) all live here and all arrive quickly from a head-down turtle. Several of them also compress the shoulder and the jaw on the way in, which makes them feel survivable for longer than they are. **Tap on tight, not on desperate.**

**Neck cranks.** A front headlock with a chin strap and no strangle is a crank. IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt (atlas Part XII). If your neck is what hurts rather than your head, tap and say so.

**Getting slammed.** Standing up with a body lock on invites a mat return, and mat returns are one of the more common ways people land badly. Break the grip first. Slams are banned in almost every ruleset, but a legal mat return performed enthusiastically still puts you on the floor hard.

**Their safety, if you are bigger.** Standing up with a smaller partner clinging to your back can drop them on their head. Stand with control and lower them. Do not sit out or switch through someone's planted knee.

**Legality.** Every escape on this page is legal at every belt in every ruleset.
