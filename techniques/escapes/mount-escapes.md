# Mount Escapes

> The four ways out from under the worst upper-body pin in grappling — and the reason you should stop thinking of them as four separate techniques and start thinking of them as one chain.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom mount (**−3** on the atlas ladder) |
| **Comes from** | A passed guard, a failed sweep, a collapsed turtle, a lost scramble, side control → mount |
| **Difficulty** | 1–3/5 · per escape, see §3 |
| **How common** | 5/5 · you will be mounted in your first class and in your last one |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets — escapes are never illegal |

**Also known as:** bottom mount survival, upa/bridge escapes, elbow-knee/shrimp escapes

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years. You cannot attack from a position you can't survive."* Mount escapes are the first item on that list, because mount is the position where being bad at escaping costs you the most: **−3** on the ladder, one step from the back, and the launch point for the entire upper-body submission menu.

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII.a. Where an escape or a variation is not separately rated in the atlas, that is stated explicitly.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Mount is worth **+3** because it costs them almost nothing to hold and gives them everything to attack with. They are astride your torso with their knees on the mat and their hips low. Their weight runs through your sternum and ribcage, which does two things at once: it stops you generating any upward force, and it stops your diaphragm expanding properly. Anyone who has ever said "I have no cardio in bottom mount" is describing a mechanical problem, not a fitness problem.

Three structures hold you there, and every escape on this page is an attack on one of them:

1. **Their knees in your armpits.** As long as their knees are above your elbows, your elbows cannot get inside their thighs, and without an elbow inside there is no route for a knee. This is why **high mount** (atlas: Diff 3, Com 4) is so much worse than low mount — it is not more painful, it is geometrically closed.
2. **Their weight over your chest line.** Weight forward makes them heavy and hard to bridge; it also makes their hips light, which is why weight-forward mount leaks the elbow-knee escape. Weight back makes them mobile and hard to shrimp against, but suddenly rollable. They cannot have both, and the one they choose tells you which escape to use.
3. **Their posts.** Two hands and a head, plus two legs that can flare out or grapevine. Every one of those is a base point, and the upa only works when you have taken enough of them away.

What they get from all this: armbar, arm triangle, americana, cross collar, ezekiel, straight armlock, and the road to technical mount and the back (atlas: *Mount → technical mount → back*, Diff 2, Com 5 — "where most back takes actually come from"). The submission threat is real, but the *positional* threat is worse. Losing mount badly usually means losing your back, and back control is **+4** with roughly 45% of finishes at a recent IBJJF Worlds coming from the rear naked choke alone.

**The clock matters.** Low mount is escapable. High mount is much harder. S-mount is nearly a submission already. Every second you spend deciding is a centimetre they climb. Mount escapes are an early-position skill, and the biggest single improvement most people can make is doing the *same* escape they already know, four seconds sooner.

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames.** Frames are bone structures that hold space without muscular effort — forearms and shins, not biceps and hands. In bottom mount your frames are the **forearm across their hip bone** and, later, the **shin across their thigh**. Frames go on hips and thighs. They do not go on chests and shoulders. Pushing on their chest with straightened arms does three things, all bad: it makes them heavier (you're driving your own shoulder blades into the mat), it burns your arms, and it hands them the armbar and the arm triangle. If you can see your own elbow in your peripheral vision, it is in the wrong place.

**The elbow–knee connection.** This is the win condition for the whole position. Every mount escape, without exception, is trying to reconnect **one elbow to the same-side knee**. When your elbow and your knee touch, a leg can come through, and a leg through means half guard. Half guard is **0 to −1** on the ladder instead of **−3**. You do not need to reach full guard. You need one knee in. Say it to yourself in the position: *elbow to knee, one side, that's all.*

**Breathing under pressure.** The atlas is blunt: *"Panic is 80% oxygen debt."* You cannot inflate your chest against someone sitting on it, so stop trying. Breathe **nasally and shallowly, and exhale as they settle** — their weight will do the exhale for you if you let it. Take your breath in the trough, in the half-second after they adjust and before they resettle. Never take a big breath, hold it, and explode; two of those and you are done for the round. A calm bottom-mount player who is doing nothing is in far better shape than a frantic one who is bridging every four seconds.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never straighten an arm** to push them off. That is the armbar and the arm triangle in one gift.
- **Never let both elbows come above their knees.** Once your elbows are outside, high mount arrives free of charge.
- **Never turn belly-down to escape.** Turning your back under mount is the single worst trade available to you: you convert **−3** into **−4** and hand them the technical-mount road to back control. Turn to your *side*, never past it.
- **Never bridge without trapping something first.** A naked bridge is a free repositioning opportunity for them and a free rep of cardio for you. Trap an arm, trap a foot, or don't bridge.
- **Never cross your ankles or clamp your legs together.** There is nothing to hold; you are just deleting your own base.
- **Never lie flat with your legs straight.** Heels near your bum, knees live. Straight legs put every kilogram they own onto your chest and remove your bridge.
- **Never grab their collar or reach for their head.** Both hands live on your own collarbones, your own ribs, or their hips.

## 3. The escapes

### Upa / trap-and-roll

**Diff 1/5 · Com 5/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a)

The oldest escape in the book and still the most valuable, because it is the one that works when you are exhausted and the one that works with strikes present.

1. **Pick a side and trap the arm on it.** Both of your hands go to their wrist and pull it across to your chest, pinning their hand flat against your own sternum or shoulder. **Their palm must not be able to reach the mat.** A trapped wrist with a free elbow still bases; if you can, take the arm at the elbow crease as well.
2. **Trap the same-side foot.** Step your foot over their instep or hook your heel behind their heel and pin it to the mat. **Foot before bridge.** Without the foot, they simply post the leg and your bridge dies against it.
3. Bring both your heels close to your bum. Knees up, feet flat, weight ready.
4. **Bridge over your shoulder, not toward the ceiling.** Drive your hips up *and* diagonally toward the trapped-arm side, finishing with your ear travelling toward the mat. You are rotating them over one point of contact, not lifting them off two.
5. Follow them over — turn your chest into them as they go, so you land in their closed guard rather than beside them.
6. Posture up immediately. The upa is a positional trade (**−3** to **−1**), not a win; the person underneath is still the person underneath.
7. **If they post the free hand to stop the roll — stop rolling.** That post is the trigger for the hip heist below. Never repeat a failed upa on the same side.

### Elbow-knee escape (shrimp escape)

**Diff 2/5 · Com 5/5**

The escape you will use most often for the rest of your life. It is slow, unglamorous, and it works against people you cannot move.

1. **Get your elbows inside their knees.** Everything else is downstream of this. Hands on your own collarbones, elbows squeezed to your ribs, and fight this battle continuously — every time they climb, wedge your elbows back in.
2. Frame: one forearm across the hip bone on the side you're escaping to, the other hand on their far hip or their knee. Elbows stay bent.
3. **Small bridge.** Not an upa — just enough to unweight your hips off the mat so they can slide. One or two inches of lift is plenty.
4. As you come down, **shrimp your hips away** from the side you want the knee on. To get your right knee in, your hips travel left.
5. Drive the knee through the gap the moment it appears, and immediately turn the shin across their thigh so the shin becomes the frame. Now the space is held by bone, not by effort.
6. Get onto your side and build a knee shield. **Half guard is the destination.** Holding out for full guard while a good half guard is available is how people lose the position they just earned.

### Heel drag / foot drag

**Diff 2/5 · Com 4/5**

The cheapest escape on the page and hugely underused. It costs no explosiveness at all, which makes it the escape that ages well.

1. Frames on the hips, elbows inside the knees, heels near your bum.
2. Hook your heel over the top of their foot or behind their ankle on one side.
3. Small bridge to lighten their leg, then **drag their foot toward your own centreline** (or toward their other foot) with your heel while your hips shrimp away in the opposite direction.
4. As their knee peels off the mat, their base on that side is gone — thread your knee into the gap.
5. Land in half guard, get on your side, knee shield.
6. **The heel drag beats the upa against people you cannot lift**, because it removes a base point without asking you to displace any mass.

### Hip heist to half (the combination escape)

**Diff 3/5 · Com 4/5**

The atlas describes this one exactly right: *"Combination escape: fail the upa on purpose so they post, then shrimp into the space."*

1. Set up a genuine upa on your right side — trap their left arm, trap their left foot, heels in.
2. **Bridge for real.** They must believe it. A half-hearted bridge produces a half-hearted post and no space.
3. They post their free (right) hand wide to your left to stop the roll. Their weight commits to that post, and their left knee — the one on your right side — comes light.
4. **The instant you feel the post, abandon the roll.** Drop your hips back to the mat rather than fighting to complete it.
5. Shrimp your hips toward their posting side (your left) and drive your right elbow to your right knee.
6. Thread the right knee through, shin across their thigh, and turn onto your right side into half guard.

### The combination principle

This is the actual content of this page, and it is why the four escapes above are listed in the order they are.

**No mount escape works on its own against anyone competent.** Each one asks them for a specific defensive commitment, and each of those commitments opens a different door:

| They defend by | Which means | Take |
|---|---|---|
| Posting a hand wide against your upa | Their base is committed to one side and one knee is light | Hip heist / elbow-knee on the light side |
| Widening the knees and dropping weight forward against your shrimp | Their hips are now high and their hands are on the mat, not on you | Trap an arm and upa |
| Grapevining your legs to kill the bridge | Their legs are committed and they cannot climb to high mount | Heel drag or elbow-knee — their legs can't chase your hips |
| Sitting back and posturing to attack your collar | Their weight is off your chest and their arm is extended | Upa to that side — the attacking arm cannot post |
| Climbing to high mount | Their hips are past your elbows but their base is now narrow and tall | Upa. High mount is bad for shrimping and good for rolling |

**The rule to take away: never attempt the same escape twice in a row.** If the first attempt failed, they are now in the exact defensive shape that the *other* escape is designed to beat. Repeating it is the most common mistake in bottom mount, and it is the one that turns a survivable round into an exhausting one.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** Escaping mount from a 60 kg opponent and a 110 kg opponent are not the same problem with different amounts of effort — they are different problems, with different escapes, different timing and different definitions of success. Most instruction pretends otherwise, teaches the upa against a same-size partner, and leaves you to discover on your own that it stops working.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Much heavier than you** | The upa asks you to displace their mass vertically, and you cannot generate the force; every failed bridge costs you far more than it costs them | Bridge diagonally over the trapped shoulder, trap the foot first, and treat the upa as a *space-maker* for the elbow-knee escape rather than a finish |
| **Much lighter than you** | They ride the bridge instead of being displaced by it, and their mount is held by grips and speed rather than weight | Break grips first; bridge twice in quick succession and chase them with your chest on the second; stay square, because they will take your back the instant you turn |
| **Very tall / long limbs** | Their post reaches so far out that your bridge runs out of rotation before they run out of base; long legs make grapevines free | Own the arm above the elbow, not at the wrist; free one leg from the grapevine before bridging at all; a one-legged bridge beats a two-legged one with both legs tied |
| **Short and stocky, low centre of gravity** | Their mass sits low and close to your midline, so the bridge has almost no rotational lever — you are trying to tip a bollard | Stop displacing vertically. Walk their hips toward your knees with frames, pulse-bridge in small repeated arcs, and use the heel drag as your primary |
| **Very strong grippers** | A deep collar or wrist grip pins your upper body so they can ride your bridge without needing base | Roll toward the gripping arm — a gripping hand cannot post. Break grips with two hands and hip rotation, never with grip strength |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | Strength gets you out, so you never learn the mechanics; and a smaller opponent's real threat is your back, not the pin | Escape with structure anyway; stay square, keep the far shoulder down, and control your bridge so you don't launch a lighter partner |

### Much heavier than you

This is the case the whole library exists for, so it gets the most detail.

**Why the standard upa fails.** The textbook upa is taught as a vertical action: bridge, lift, roll. Against someone 25–35 kg heavier, the vertical component is simply not available. You are attempting to raise a mass through a distance using hip extension, from a position where your chest is compressed and you cannot take a full breath before you start. It is not a matter of bridging harder. Every rep of "bridge harder" produces a small lift, no rotation, an exhale, and a slightly better mount for them.

Four concrete changes:

1. **Stop bridging up. Bridge diagonally, toward the trapped-arm shoulder.** A straight-up bridge asks you to move their whole mass over a two-point base — both of your shoulders. A bridge angled roughly 45 degrees toward the trapped side asks you to rotate them over a *single* point of contact: that one shoulder. You are no longer lifting; you are tipping. The felt difference is enormous, and it is a change of direction, not a change of effort.
2. **Trap the foot first, before the arm if you have to choose.** Against a heavy opponent the leg post is what actually kills the roll — they need only one leg out to absorb 100% of the rotation, and their legs are the strongest thing they own. Get your heel over their instep and pin it to the mat before you commit to anything upstairs. If you can only take one thing, take the foot.
3. **Redefine what success looks like: the upa is a setup, not the escape.** Use the bridge to buy **three inches — about 8 cm — of space at your hip**, then convert immediately to the elbow-knee escape while they are still recovering their base. Expect to get a knee in. Do not expect the roll. If the roll happens, take it as a bonus. This single reframe is the difference between an escape system that works against heavier people and one that doesn't.
4. **Escape on their movement, never against their base.** The atlas calls this *timing over strength*, and against a heavy opponent it stops being an efficiency tip and becomes the only route. Their base is stable but slow. Move when they move: as they climb toward high mount, as they reach for a grip, as they shift weight to attack an arm. A heavy opponent cannot chase your hips quickly — that is your entire advantage, and it only exists if you move at the right moment.

**High mount is the real emergency.** Against a much heavier opponent, high mount is functionally unescapable by the elbow-knee route, because your elbows cannot physically reach their knees. So the escape starts before mount is even settled: **hands on your own collarbones, elbows welded to your ribs, and every time they inch upward, push their hips back down toward your own hips with your forearm frames.** Preventing the climb *is* the escape. If you only get one job in bottom mount against a big opponent, it is this one.

**Breathing.** Exhale as they settle, breathe shallowly through the nose, and take your air in the gaps. Do not do the big-breath-and-explode cycle — under a heavy opponent it produces oxygen debt with no positional return, and after two of them your escape quality collapses.

**Escape selection.** In rough priority order against someone much heavier: (1) prevent high mount, (2) heel drag, (3) elbow-knee, (4) upa as a setup only. The heel drag is unusually good here because heavier opponents tend to hold mount with weight rather than with grapevines, which leaves their feet lazy and available.

**Say something.** Bottom mount and bottom side control under a much heavier partner are two of the most common places to get a rib injury in training. If you cannot breathe, say so. That is not quitting; it is the same category of skill as tapping.

### Much lighter than you

The mirror problem, and people underrate how different it is. A lighter opponent's mount is not held up by weight at all — it is held by grips, hooks and speed. That changes almost everything.

- **The upa works, but only if you catch them.** A lighter opponent will ride the bridge like a surfer: you lift, they float, they land back where they were. The fix is not more lift, it is **follow-through** — as you reach the top of the bridge, turn your chest into them and drive your top shoulder across, chasing them over with your torso rather than waiting for gravity to finish the job.
- **Bridge twice.** The first bridge makes them adjust. The second, half a second later, catches them mid-adjustment with their base momentarily narrow. Against a heavy opponent this is a waste of energy; against a light one it is the whole technique.
- **Attack the grips, not the weight.** If their mount is held by a cross-collar grip, a wrist tie or grapevines, break that first and the mount collapses on its own. Against a much lighter opponent, grip fighting *is* mount escaping.
- **Stay square — this is the serious one.** Turning onto your side under a light opponent is far more dangerous than under a heavy one, because they can follow your hip instantly and ride to technical mount. Keep your shoulders flatter, escape with the elbow-knee more squarely than you would otherwise, and get the knee in *before* you turn.
- **Expect submissions, not pressure.** A lighter person in mount is there to attack. Armbar and triangle threats arrive earlier than you're used to, so arm discipline matters more, not less.

### Very tall opponents with long limbs

- **Their post reaches further, so the upa needs a deeper trap.** Wrist control is not enough against long arms: you can pin their hand to your chest and their elbow will still find the mat and base. **Take the arm above the elbow** — cup the elbow crease or overhook the whole arm — and pull it across your centreline so their shoulder passes over your shoulder. That is what removes the post.
- **Grapevines are the real problem.** Long legs make grapevines cheap for them, and **you cannot bridge with your legs stretched out.** Free one leg first: swing that leg out wide to the side to strip the hook, or lift it vertically and shake the hook off, then bring the heel back to your bum. **A bridge with one free leg beats a bridge with two grapevined legs**, every time. Do not try to free both — you will lose the position while you're negotiating.
- **Long limbs make their high mount arrive faster and their submissions arrive earlier.** Cross collar, arm triangle and armbar are all more available to a long-armed opponent. Elbows in.
- **The upside: length is fragile at the extremes.** Their base points are far from their centre of mass, which means a post they have committed to is slow to recover. If you trap the far arm as they post it, they cannot get it back quickly — the hip heist is unusually productive here.

### Short, stocky opponents with a low centre of gravity

The build people complain about most and understand least. A short, thick opponent is genuinely harder to bridge than a taller one of the *same weight*, because bridging is a rotation and rotation needs height to work with.

- **Stop trying to displace them vertically.** Their mass sits low and close to your midline, so your bridge has a very short lever. Instead, displace them **horizontally**: use your forearm frames to walk their hips down toward your own knees. Every centimetre their hips travel toward your knees is a centimetre your elbows recover over their knees. That is the escape.
- **Pulse-bridge.** Rather than one maximal effort, use small repeated bridges — six or eight little arcs. A low centre of gravity resists a single big displacement but accumulates small ones, and each one lets your frames walk their hips a little further down.
- **Turn one hip 45 degrees rather than escaping square.** Their knee pinch is tight but *shallow* — short thighs mean the pinch does not extend far down your ribcage. Angling one hip gives that side's knee a clear line in.
- **The heel drag is your highest-percentage escape here.** Short legs mean their feet sit close to your hips and are easy for your heel to reach. Take it.
- **Their short arms are the gift.** The post that kills your upa is short, so they must post *close to their own body*, which means a small bridge produces a disproportionately large base commitment. The hip heist combination is therefore better against short-armed opponents than against anyone else on this page: fake the upa, they post tight, the hole opens right next to you.

### Very strong grippers

- **A gripping hand cannot post — so roll toward it.** This is the most useful single fact about strong grippers in mount. If their right hand has a deep grip on your collar or your wrist, that arm is committed and cannot base out to their right. Trap that arm (their own grip does half the trapping for you — their hand is already stuck to your chest), trap the same-side foot, and upa in that direction. Strong grippers hand you the trapped arm for free.
- **Break grips with two hands and your hips, not with your hands.** The rule is two-on-one, attack the thumb side of the grip, and generate the break by turning your body away from the grip rather than by pulling with your arms. You will lose a pure grip-strength contest and you will lose your fingers over the years doing it — the atlas is explicit that BJJ finger arthritis is "real and near-universal."
- **Break the lower grip first.** A belt, pant or hip grip is what stops your shrimp. A collar grip only stops your bridge. If they have both and you can only break one, break the one on your hips.
- **In no-gi:** the equivalent grips are a wrist tie and a head-and-arm control. Attack the *connection* — their clasped hands — rather than trying to peel a single strong hand.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

You will be mounted by people much lighter than you, and the situation is genuinely different — not easier in the way you expect.

- **Your strength will work, and that is the problem.** You can bench a lighter person off you for about eighteen months. Then you meet someone technical at your own size and discover you have no mount escapes at all. Use the size gap as a laboratory: if you cannot escape a much lighter opponent using only frames, hip movement and timing, you cannot do the escape.
- **You are harder to mount but easier to hold on your back.** A bigger body is slower to shrimp — there is simply more of you to move — so your frames have to be set earlier and your escapes started sooner. Do not let the fact that you *feel* fine turn into lying there.
- **Grapevines don't work on you.** A lighter opponent cannot meaningfully control your legs, so the heel drag and the hip heist are cheap and reliable. Make them your default.
- **The real danger is your back, not the mount.** A smaller opponent will abandon mount happily to take technical mount and the back, because for them that is a much better position. Stay squarer than you think you need to. Do not turn to your side to "make space" — get your knee in first.
- **Safety, and this is not optional.** A hard bridge with a 60 kg person attached to you can throw them off the mat, into the wall, or into another pair. Bridge with control, look before you roll, and if they are hanging on as you come up, bring them with you rather than launching them.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your frames don't reach their hips from a flat position. Compensate by getting to your side earlier — a side-lying frame is shorter than a supine one — and by converting to **shin frames** as fast as possible. Your shin is longer than your forearm and never gets tired.
- **Long legs.** Heel drag and knee insertion are both easier for you than for most people; a long leg reaches their far foot and gets a knee in through a smaller gap. Make the heel drag your first choice.
- **Heavy, thick torso or limited hip mobility.** Your shrimp is short, so you need more of them and you need frames that hold the ground you gain. Get the shin in early even if the half guard is ugly, and skip anything requiring inversion.
- **Light and fast.** You generate more bridge relative to your own mass than anyone else on the mat, but you also get ridden more easily. The hip heist is your primary: fake, let them commit, and move before they can rebase.
- **Older, or less explosive than you used to be.** The upa is an explosive escape and it degrades with age faster than anything else here. Rebuild your game around the elbow-knee and the heel drag, and demote the upa to a feint. Also invest the effort upstream: for you, the best mount escape is a guard retention drill.
- **Previous rib, lower back or shoulder injury.** Bridging loads the lumbar spine and the mount itself loads the ribs. Warm up the bridge before you need it, tell your partner about the injury *before* the round (atlas, Tier 0), and prefer the heel drag, which asks for almost no spinal extension.

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I bridge and they just stay there" | You are bridging vertically, or you never trapped the foot | Bridge diagonally over the trapped shoulder; trap the foot *before* the arm |
| "My arms are exhausted after thirty seconds" | You are pushing on their chest with muscle instead of framing on their hips with bone | Elbows bent and glued to your ribs, forearms on hip bones, load through the skeleton |
| "I can't get my elbow under their knee" | They are already in high mount — you lost this one step earlier | Prevention: hands on your own collarbones, and push their hips back down every time they climb |
| "I make space but they re-mount instantly" | You are recovering to flat half guard instead of getting onto your side | Turn the shin across their thigh and build a knee shield before you do anything else |
| "I keep getting armbarred while escaping" | An arm is straightening, or an elbow is drifting away from your ribs | Both hands on your own body or their hips; if you can see your elbow, move it |
| "I turn to escape and they take my back" | Your shoulders turned before your knee was in | Knee in first, then turn. Under mount, side is fine; belly-down is never |
| "I get the roll but immediately get swept or submitted" | You landed in their guard flat and stayed there | The upa ends at **−1**, not at zero. Posture up the instant you land |
| "I can't breathe and then I panic" | You are holding your breath, bridging on empty lungs, and paying oxygen debt | Nasal, shallow, exhale as they settle. Escape on the breath, not against it |
| "The escape works in drilling and never in rolling" | You are attempting it against a set base instead of on their movement | Escape as they climb, adjust or reach. Timing, not force |
| "Every escape I try fails" | You are repeating one escape instead of chaining | Never the same escape twice in a row — use their defence to pick the next one (§3) |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from mount.** The highest-value fifteen minutes in any class, per the atlas. Start in settled mount. Bottom player wins by getting **one knee in** — not by reaching guard, not by sweeping. Top player wins by holding for the round or advancing. **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Positional rounds produce roughly three times the reps of the relevant situation compared with open rolling.

**2. The chain drill (upa → hip heist).** Partner's only job is to defend the upa by posting a hand. Every time they post, you abandon the roll and take the elbow-knee on the light side. **10 reps per side**, no resistance beyond the post. This drill builds the single most important reflex on the page: *feel the post, change escapes.*

**3. The weight-differential round.** Do the same escape from mount against the heaviest and the lightest partners in the room, back to back, three minutes each. Then write down what changed — which one needed the diagonal bridge, which one needed you to chase them, which one rode your bridge. Three lines after class, per the atlas note-taking habit.

**4. High-mount prevention.** Partner starts in low mount and does nothing but try to climb above your elbows. You are not allowed to escape — your only job is to keep them below your armpits for **60 seconds**. Two rounds each. This trains the part of the position that decides everything and that nobody practises.

**5. Solo, in the warm-up.** Twenty bridges, twenty shrimps, then twenty **bridge-shrimp combinations** — bridge, land, shrimp, repeat — travelling the length of the mat. The combination is the actual movement; the isolated versions are just its parts.

## 8. Safety

**Your ribs.** Bottom mount under a much heavier partner is a genuine rib-injury position, and rib injuries take months, not weeks. If you cannot get a breath, say so out loud and reset. Verbalising discomfort is the same category of skill as tapping, and the atlas is explicit that the tap is a technique rather than a failure.

**Your neck.** Bridge **over your shoulder**, never over the crown of your head. A wrestler's bridge onto the top of the head loads the cervical spine under someone else's bodyweight — it is how people accumulate neck problems they still have ten years later. Ear toward the mat, weight through the shoulder.

**Your lower back.** Bridging is spinal extension under load. Warm it up before rolling and stop bridging repeatedly through pain rather than through fatigue.

**Their safety.** If you are considerably bigger, control the roll. Look where you are going before you upa — mats are crowded and a launched partner lands wherever physics puts them. If they are clinging on as you come up, take them with you.

**Submissions to respect while escaping.** The [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) and the [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md) both come directly from escape mistakes, and both can arrive faster than you expect from high mount. Tap early to both — the arm triangle in particular gives very little warning between "uncomfortable" and "out."

**Legality.** Nothing on this page is restricted at any belt in any ruleset. Escaping is legal everywhere, which is a reasonable argument for learning it first.
