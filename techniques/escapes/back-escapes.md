# Back Escapes

> Getting out of the best position in grappling, in the right order: neck first, shoulders second, hips third, legs last — and never the other way round.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — back taken (**−4** on the atlas ladder, the worst position in the sport) |
| **Comes from** | Turtle, technical mount, a failed guard retention, an arm drag, a leg drag, a front headlock, any scramble you lost |
| **Difficulty** | 2–3/5 · per escape, see §3 |
| **How common** | 5/5 · the hand fight and slide is rated Com 5 in the atlas |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** back defense, escaping the seatbelt, hand fighting from the back

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years."* Nowhere is that more true than here. Back control is **+4**, the atlas calls it the highest finish rate in the sport, and the [rear naked choke](../submissions/rear-naked-choke.md) alone accounts for around 45% of finishes at a recent IBJJF Worlds. This is the position where being bad at defense doesn't cost you points — it ends the round.

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Parts VII.a and VII.b.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Back control is the only dominant position where you cannot see what is happening to you and cannot reach the person doing it. That is the whole thesis of the position, and it is worth **+4** for exactly that reason.

They need three things, and they need all three at once:

1. **The seatbelt** — one arm over your shoulder, one under the opposite armpit, hands connected. This stops you rotating, keeps their chest glued to your spine, and delivers the choking arm to your neck without any further work.
2. **The legs** — either **hooks** (heels inside your thighs, atlas Diff 3 / Com 5) or a **body triangle** (figure-four around your torso, Diff 3 / Com 4). The legs stop your hips escaping and stop you turning in.
3. **Your chin.** The choke needs a gap between your chin and your chest. Nothing else about the position matters if that gap does not exist.

The arithmetic that makes this position so bad: **you have two hands to protect one neck, and they have two arms plus gravity plus a choke that only needs one arm to finish.** You cannot win that exchange by trading hand for hand. You win it by taking away one of their three requirements — and the good news is that removing *any one* degrades the whole system.

**The hierarchy of what you're actually defending.** Being choked is a loss. Being held on the back is a losing position. Those are not the same emergency, and confusing them is the most common error here. Your chin and your hands deal with the choke. Your shoulders and hips deal with the position. **You never trade the first for the second** — nobody has ever been happy about clearing a hook while an arm went under their chin.

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames.** Your frames in back control are not your arms — your arms are busy. Your frames are your **shoulder against the mat** and your **hip against their hip**. The shoulder frame is what stops them staying behind you; the hip frame is what stops them re-aligning. Think of both as structural contact points you are trying to establish, not things you push with.

**Chin defense, before anything.** Chin tucked hard to your chest, and **turned toward the side their choking arm comes over** — you want your chin inside the crook of their elbow, not out past their hand. Turning the other way feeds your throat straight down their forearm. Do this *before* the arm arrives; the atlas rates chin-down-and-hand-fight at Diff 2 / Com 5 precisely because the early version is simple and the late version is not. Late defense against a locked rear naked choke is not a technique, it is a tap.

**Elbow–knee connection.** It exists here too, just later in the sequence. Your elbows stay pinned to your ribs to deny the second hook and the body triangle; your knees stay bent with your heels near your body so a hook has nothing straight to ride on. Once your shoulders reach the mat, the elbow-to-knee connection is what turns "I got out of the back" into "I got to half guard."

**Breathing under pressure.** Their chest is on your spine, so your back cannot expand — breathe into the front and sides instead, nasally and shallowly. A body triangle actively compresses your ribcage (the atlas lists **body triangle squeeze** as a legal submission in its own right, Diff 3 / Com 2), so under a triangle you will have measurably less air than usual. Do not spend it. The single most common way people lose the back position is a thirty-second panic burst that achieves nothing and leaves them unable to defend the choke that follows.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never grab both of their arms at once.** One hand on each arm is one hand on each arm — you have lost both exchanges. It is always **two hands on one**.
- **Never turn toward the choking arm's *hand* side.** That rotates your throat into the strangle.
- **Never let your hands leave the neck to fight the hooks.** **Legs clear legs; hands defend the neck.** If both hands go down to a hook, the choke goes on for free.
- **Never sit up.** Sitting up leaves them behind you with a better angle and gives them your weight to work with. Your back belongs on the mat.
- **Never let your elbows drift away from your ribs.** That is how the second hook goes in, how the body triangle closes, and how you end up in a [crucifix](../../jujitsu.md).
- **Never peel fingers.** Small-joint manipulation is banned everywhere (atlas Part XV.c), and it does not work anyway. Attack the wrist with two hands and break the grip with body rotation.
- **Never "wait it out" once the choke is locked with the second hand behind your head.** There is no leverage available and only a few seconds of consciousness. Tap.

## 3. The escapes

### Step zero — chin down and the hand fight (two hands on one)

**Diff 2/5 · Com 5/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.b: *chin down, hands fight*)

Not an escape. The thing that buys you the time to do one.

1. **Chin to your chest, turned toward the shoulder their choking arm crosses.** Your jaw is the wedge; keep it in the crook of their elbow.
2. **Both hands on their choking wrist**, four-finger grips, no thumbs wrapped. If their arm is not in yet, both hands live high on your own collarbones, palms out, blocking the gap.
3. **Peel down and across, toward your own hips** — never straight out to the side. Pulling outward fights their whole shoulder girdle; pulling down works against their fingers and their elbow angle, and your lats are stronger than their grip.
4. **Pin their wrist to your chest and keep it there.** Do not release to celebrate; they will switch sides immediately, and the switch is the moment most chokes actually land.
5. **Start the shoulder slide while you are still holding the arm.** The hand fight is a holding action — it does not improve your position by itself, and a hand fight with no escape attached is just a slow loss.

**The two details that matter:** *two hands on one, always*, and *peel toward your hips, never away from your body.*

### The shoulder slide (getting your back to the mat)

Part of the atlas entry **back escape — hand fight and slide** · **Diff 3/5 · Com 5/5**

1. Keep the hand fight. This whole escape happens with both hands still on their arm where possible.
2. **Make yourself long.** Slide your shoulders *down* toward your own hips until your head is lower than their chin and their chest is above your shoulder line. You are not trying to move them — you are sliding along the mat, which is a movement that works under any amount of weight.
3. **Turn your shoulders toward the side their choking arm comes over**, and drive that shoulder down into the mat behind you.
4. Keep sliding and rotating until both shoulder blades touch the floor. **Their choking arm is now trapped between your back and the mat.** That is what kills the choke — not your hands.
5. Do **not** let your hips travel with your shoulders. That is the next section, and it is where most people lose the escape.

**On direction.** Some gyms teach this to the other side. Rather than argue, use the test: **at the end of the movement, is their choking arm pinned underneath you, and are your hips outside theirs?** If yes, you went the right way. If their choking arm is free on top of you, you went the wrong way regardless of what anyone called it.

### The hip-to-hip principle

Part of the atlas entry **back escape — hip-to-hip** · **Diff 3/5 · Com 4/5**

The most commonly skipped step in the whole escape, and the reason people "escape the back" and end up right back on the back.

1. **Shoulders travel; hips stay.** Your outside hip stays pressed against their bottom hip through the entire escape — imagine a nail through both hips that your body rotates around.
2. If you slide your hips down and out while your shoulders stay upright, you have not escaped. You have sat up in front of them with your back exposed and given them a *better* angle than they started with.
3. **Your two ends go opposite ways.** Shoulders slide down and rotate toward the choking-arm side. Hips walk out the other way, toward their underhooking side. If both ends of you travel the same direction, you have simply rotated in place and they are still behind you.
4. When your shoulders reach the mat, **drive your hip into theirs** so they cannot re-align behind your spine.
5. Only now do you deal with the legs.

**The line to remember: the moment your hips separate from theirs, they re-take the back.** Everything in back defense is a fight to stay hip-to-hip while your shoulders escape.

### Clearing the first hook

1. **Do not touch a hook until your shoulders are past them.** Stripping hooks while your back is still exposed is wasted work — they re-insert faster than you remove.
2. **Use your legs, not your hands.** Cross your free leg over their hooking leg and extend both of your legs, dragging their hook out of the way with your whole limb. **Legs clear legs; hands defend the neck.**
3. If you must use a hand, take their **heel**, not their shin — the heel is the handle and the shin just rotates in your grip. Push it toward your own feet while straightening that leg.
4. **The instant one hook clears, turn that knee down toward the mat and bring the leg over their thigh.** You are not trying to clear both hooks; one hook plus your knee on the mat is already half guard.
5. Land in half guard, quarter guard, or bottom side control. All three are enormous upgrades from **−4** — the atlas prices bottom side control at **−2** and half guard bottom at roughly **−1**. Do not hold out for a better landing.

### Escaping the body triangle

The body triangle is rated **Diff 3 / Com 4** as a position. Escaping it is a genuinely different problem from escaping hooks, and the difference cuts both ways.

**Harder to escape:** a figure-four cannot be pushed off the way a hook can. It survives your shoulders reaching the mat, it squeezes your ribcage while you work, and it does not care how much you bridge.

**But slower for them to abandon:** the atlas notes it is "slightly slower to abandon if things go wrong," and that understates the tactical consequence. With hooks, they can strip, re-insert, switch sides and ride to mount instantly. Locked in a triangle, they cannot follow you to the other side, cannot take mount, and must *unlock a figure-four* before they can chase anything. **You have more time and they have fewer options — so the body triangle rewards a structured escape and punishes panic even more than hooks do.**

1. **Hand fight first, always.** The triangle does not choke you; the arm does. Do not touch their legs while an arm is live under your chin.
2. **Find the trapped ankle.** One of their feet is tucked behind their own opposite knee. **That is the direction you rotate.** Turning the other way winds the figure-four tighter around you; turning toward the trapped ankle unwinds it.
3. **Get your hips below the triangle.** Slide your shoulders and hips down the mat until the triangle sits on your lower ribs or waist rather than your hips. A triangle above your hip line has lost most of its holding power, and your hips are then free to move out from underneath it.
4. **Control the top foot and pin it to your hip** with one hand once your neck is genuinely safe, then hip-escape away from it.
5. **Step your outside leg over their bottom leg** as the lock loosens, and turn your knee to the mat. Half guard.
6. **If it is crushing your ribs, tap.** Body triangle squeeze is a legal submission in its own right; there is nothing brave about riding it out.

### Where you land, and why that's a win

1. Half guard, quarter guard or bottom side control is the normal exit. Take it.
2. Immediately establish a knee shield or a frame — a back escape that ends flat under side control is half an escape.
3. Expect a **guillotine or a front headlock** on the way out, especially against strong grippers who keep the seatbelt through the transition. Come out with your **head up and your posture set**.
4. Do not stand up mid-escape with someone attached to your back. See §8.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters**, and back control is where body type changes the problem most, because the position is built out of limb *length* rather than mass. A 60 kg opponent with long legs is a harder back control to escape than a 100 kg opponent with short ones. That is not true of any other position in this library.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Long legs** | Their hooks reach past your far thigh from an off-centre position, and the body triangle closes on a torso it has no business closing on | Deny the triangle early by keeping one hip on the mat and elbows down; strip hooks leg-on-leg, never hand-on-leg; escape *below* the triangle rather than out of it |
| **Much heavier than you** | You cannot lift their chest off your back, and the body triangle squeeze becomes genuinely dangerous | Use the shoulder slide — it is a sliding movement, not a lifting one, so it is weight-independent; stay on the mat once you get there; tap early to the squeeze |
| **Much lighter than you** | Their control is grips and speed, not weight; they will re-take rather than hold, and they get to the choke faster | Attack the seatbelt connection, not the hooks; escape in one continuous movement, not in stages; chin work is more urgent, not less |
| **Short, stocky, short legs** | Shallow hooks and no body triangle — but a very tight seatbelt and heavy chest instead | Fight the grip, not the legs; and out-*reach* them: slide your shoulders further down than they can follow, because their short arms run out of chest before you run out of mat |
| **Very strong grippers** | The seatbelt clasp will not break by pulling, and in the gi a collar grip means the choke is already half-on | Do not attack the grip — attack the structure that makes the grip useful. Slide the shoulders and let geometry break it. Strip collar grips *before* they get depth |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | Your weight advantage is worth less here than anywhere else — their limbs are holding you, not their mass | Don't shrug them off; use the same shoulder slide. Exploit that their hooks are shallow and their triangle probably won't close on your torso |

### Opponents with long legs

Read this one even if you skip the rest. Long legs are the single biggest modifier in back control.

- **Their hooks reach further, from further away.** A long-legged opponent can insert a hook while their body is still angled off to one side — a position from which a short-legged opponent has no hook at all. The practical consequence: **you will feel a hook arrive before you feel their chest arrive**, which is the reverse of the order you're used to. Do not use "their chest isn't on me yet" as evidence that you are safe.
- **The body triangle closes on torsos it shouldn't.** Long legs mean they can lock a triangle on a much bigger person than themselves. Prevention: keep **your elbows glued down** on the side they are threading, keep **your knees bent** so the thigh has no straight line to travel along, and **sit one hip to the mat** so there is no clear channel around your waist. A body triangle needs an unobstructed path around you; obstruct it early, because once it is locked you have moved from a minute-long problem to a five-minute one.
- **Once it is locked, go under it, not out of it.** With a long-legged triangle you cannot simply hip-escape sideways — their legs cover too much of you. Slide your shoulders and hips **down** the mat until the triangle rides up onto your lower ribs. It will feel worse to breathe and it will be substantially looser, because a triangle above the hip line has nothing to hold onto. From there, rotate toward the trapped ankle.
- **Strip long hooks with your legs, never with your hands.** Their hook is deep and their heel is far away; you will not out-reach it with an arm, and reaching for it costs you the neck. Cross your free ankle over their hooking ankle and extend. **Leg-on-leg beats hand-on-leg over distance**, and it keeps both your hands where they belong.
- **Keep your heels near your body.** A long hook needs a straight leg to ride. Bent knees give it nothing.

### Much heavier than you

- **The shoulder slide is the escape, because it is the only one that does not require lifting.** This is the key insight for heavy opponents and it is worth stating precisely: sliding your shoulders down the mat is a movement *along* the floor. It works under 70 kg and it works under 110 kg, because you are not displacing them at all — you are relocating yourself while they stay where they are. Any escape that requires you to lift their chest off your back is unavailable; any escape that slides is available. Choose accordingly.
- **Do not try to sit up or stand once your shoulders are down.** With a much heavier opponent attached, both are impossible and both re-expose your back. Once you are on the mat, stay on the mat and work the hips.
- **Their weight makes them slow to follow.** A heavy opponent cannot chase a rotating spine quickly. If you commit fully to the shoulder slide in one movement, you will usually get all the way through — the escape is more binary against heavy opponents than against light ones.
- **The choking arm is stronger, so change how you fight it.** A two-hands-on-one peel against a much stronger arm fails if you try to out-pull it. Instead, get both hands on the wrist, **lock your elbows to your ribs so the connection is skeletal**, and generate the break by rotating your whole torso. Your body turning is worth more than your arms pulling. And do not peel fingers — banned everywhere, and useless.
- **Tap early to the body triangle squeeze.** A much heavier opponent's triangle can compress ribs before you have any warning that it is dangerous. This is one of the places the atlas's advice about compressions applies directly: it can hurt before it is dangerous, but it can also be dangerous.
- **Honestly: prevention beats escape here.** Against someone much heavier, the time to solve the back is at the turtle and the technical-mount stage. See [turtle escapes](./turtle-escapes.md).

### Much lighter than you

- **Their back control is a grip problem, not a weight problem.** A lighter opponent cannot hold you with pressure, so the entire position is being held together by the seatbelt clasp and their hooks. **Break the seatbelt connection and a lighter opponent's back control collapses immediately** — far more completely than a heavier one's would. Attack the clasp, not the individual arms.
- **They will re-take rather than hold.** Expect them to switch sides, swap the choking arm, and re-insert hooks continuously. This means **your escape has to be one continuous movement, not a sequence of stages.** Every pause you take is a re-take opportunity for them. Against a heavy opponent you can escape in installments; against a light one you cannot.
- **The choke arrives sooner.** A lighter opponent will often skip the positional consolidation entirely and go straight for the neck, because it is their best route. So the chin and hand work is *more* urgent against a smaller opponent, not less — the opposite of most people's instincts.
- **Do not lazily flop your back to the mat.** Against a light, mobile opponent, a slow shoulder slide with your hips separated is an invitation to spin to the other side. Hip-to-hip discipline matters more here than anywhere.

### Short, stocky opponents with short legs

- **They probably cannot triangle you and their hooks will be shallow.** So they will hold you with a very tight seatbelt and a heavy chest instead. **Your escape is a grip fight and a shoulder slide, not a leg fight.** Do not spend energy hunting hooks that are barely in.
- **Out-reach them.** This is the specific and useful asymmetry: short arms mean their choking elbow sits close to their own body and is very hard to peel forward. So **stop trying to peel the arm away from your neck and instead take your neck away from the arm** — slide your shoulders further down the mat than they can follow. A short-armed opponent runs out of chest length before you run out of floor.
- **The reverse rule for long arms:** against long arms you trap; against short arms you use distance. Same escape, opposite emphasis.
- **They will be heavy on your spine.** Turn one hip to the mat early so their weight lands on your side rather than square on your spine.

### Very strong grippers

- **Do not attack the grip. Attack the structure.** A gable or S-grip seatbelt held by strong hands will not be pulled apart, and every second you spend trying is a second your neck is undefended. The seatbelt is only useful while their **chest is connected to your back** — so do the shoulder slide, and let the geometry break the grip for you. When their chest loses contact, the seatbelt has nothing to hold together.
- **In the gi, the collar grip is the emergency.** A strong hand in your collar means a [bow-and-arrow](../submissions/bow-and-arrow.md) or a sliding collar choke is already half-applied, and those do not need the arm under your chin at all. **Strip the collar hand before it gets depth**, with two hands and a hard chin-turn into it. This is the one grip you should fight directly, and immediately.
- **Never use your neck-protecting hand for grip fighting.** If one hand has to leave your collarbone, the other stays.
- **Expect the front headlock on the exit.** Strong grippers keep hold through the escape, so you will frequently come out of the back into a [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md) or a front headlock. Come out with your head up, your chin off your chest, and your posture already built — or you have traded **−4** for a submission.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **This is the position where being bigger helps least, and you should know that going in.** In mount and side control, their control comes from weight, so a size advantage transfers. In back control their control comes from *limbs*, and their limbs work fine regardless of your mass. You cannot shrug someone off your back.
- **The good news is structural.** Your bigger torso means their body triangle probably will not close, and their hooks will be comparatively shallow. **Attack the legs earlier and more aggressively than you would against a same-size opponent** — a shallow hook on a big thigh is genuinely fragile, whereas a deep hook on a small one is not.
- **Out-reach them with the shoulder slide.** A longer torso means that when you slide down the mat, their chest simply runs out of contact area. This is your primary escape and it works well.
- **Do not let a smaller opponent bait you into a strength contest at the wrist.** They will happily let you pull on their arm all day while their legs do the work.
- **Safety.** Do not sit back hard onto a smaller partner's trapped leg to strip a hook — that is a knee injury waiting to happen, and it is their knee, not yours. And do not stand up with a lighter partner clinging to your back; see §8.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Long torso.** Good news: a body triangle is much harder to lock on you, and you can slide your shoulders a long way before they run out of chest. Make the shoulder slide your primary escape and expect hooks rather than a triangle.
- **Short torso.** A body triangle closes on you easily and quickly, so your defence has to be preventative — elbows down, one hip to the mat, and treat any leg crossing your waist as an emergency rather than an inconvenience.
- **Thick neck.** It buys you real time against the [rear naked choke](../submissions/rear-naked-choke.md), and it makes people complacent. Time is not immunity: the atlas is clear that a thick neck changes the *grip* they need, not whether the choke works. Defend on the same schedule as anyone else.
- **Thin neck.** Your margin is smaller than most people's, so your chin work has to start earlier and you should never trade neck safety for positional progress. Chin tucked before the arm arrives, every time.
- **Short arms.** Peeling a choking arm is a longer reach than you have. Rely proportionally more on the shoulder slide and less on the hand fight — get your neck out of range rather than trying to remove the arm.
- **Older or less explosive.** There is no explosive back escape worth having, which is good news for you: this whole page is a structural, low-power sequence. Your real adjustment is upstream — do not get turtled, do not get technical-mounted, and do not roll away from side control.
- **Previous neck injury.** Back control is a neck-loading position even when you are winning the defence. Tap early, do not ride out chokes to prove a point, and consider telling training partners before the round (atlas, Tier 0).

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Their arm gets under my chin every time" | You are defending after it arrives instead of before | Chin down and turned into the crook *before* the arm moves; hands high on your own collarbones |
| "I peel the arm and it comes straight back" | You are pulling outward, using arm strength, and letting go too early | Peel down toward your hips with two hands, then pin the wrist to your chest and *keep* it |
| "I escape the hooks and they just re-take" | You cleared the legs while your shoulders were still in front of them | Shoulders to the mat first, legs last. Legs clear legs; hands defend the neck |
| "I slide down and end up sitting in front of them" | Your hips travelled with your shoulders | Hip-to-hip: shoulders rotate one way, hips walk the other. The hip contact is the pivot |
| "I get out of the back and straight into a guillotine" | You exited with your head down and their grip still on | Come out head up, posture built. Strong grippers keep the seatbelt through the transition |
| "The body triangle escape does nothing" | You are rotating the wrong way, or working above the triangle instead of below it | Rotate toward their trapped ankle; slide your hips down until the triangle is above your hip line |
| "I run out of air in thirty seconds" | You spent it on a panic burst, and a triangle is compressing your ribs | Nasal, shallow, front and sides. Escape structurally — there is no burst that works here |
| "One hand ends up on each of their arms" | You are matching them hand for hand, which you lose by arithmetic | Two hands on one. Always. Pick the choking arm |
| "I can't get the shoulder slide started under a heavy opponent" | You are trying to lift rather than to slide | Slide down the mat, not up off it. The floor takes their weight, not you |
| "Everything works until the last step and then I'm flat under side control" | You landed without a frame or a knee in | The escape ends when the shin is in front of their hip, not when the hooks come off |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from back control.** Start with the seatbelt set and hooks in. **Bottom player wins by getting both shoulder blades to the mat with their hips outside** — not by fully escaping. Top wins by finishing or holding. **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Defining the win as "shoulders down" rather than "escaped" is what makes this drill actually train the mechanism.

**2. Chin-and-hands, no escaping.** Partner does nothing but try to feed an arm under your chin. You may only use chin position and two-on-one hand fighting — no hip movement, no leg work. **60 seconds each, three rounds.** Unglamorous and worth more than any other drill on this page.

**3. Body triangle rounds.** Start already locked in a body triangle with the seatbelt on. **Two minutes**, then switch. Do this specifically, because the body triangle escape is mechanically different from the hooks escape and you will not learn it by accident.

**4. Long-legs round.** Deliberately take back-control rounds against the tallest, longest-legged person in the room. Then do the same against the shortest. Write down what changed — you should find hooks were the problem in one round and the seatbelt was the problem in the other.

**5. Solo shoulder slide.** From seated, slide your shoulders down to the mat while keeping one hip pressed against an imaginary opponent's hip, finishing with your shoulder blades flat and your hip still in contact. **10 per side.** Most people's first attempt slides the hips too — which is exactly the error the drill exists to catch.

## 8. Safety

**Chokes.** The [rear naked choke](../submissions/rear-naked-choke.md) is a blood choke and produces unconsciousness quickly with very little warning arc. There is no "hanging on" phase to be brave through. **Once the second hand is behind your head and the elbow is centred, tap.** The atlas rates the tap as a technique in its own right, and it is the reason you get to train tomorrow.

**The body triangle squeeze.** A legal compression that can crack ribs and can genuinely stop you breathing. Tap to it the same way you would tap to a joint lock. Do not treat rib compression as something to out-tough.

**Neck cranks.** A badly applied rear naked choke with the forearm on the jaw is a neck crank, banned by IBJJF at every belt. If your neck rather than your head is what hurts, say so and tap — riding out a crank buys you nothing and costs you cervical discs.

**Standing up.** Do not stand with someone on your back to escape. If they hold on, you can be dropped on your head; if they let go, they can land on theirs. Both outcomes are avoidable and neither is worth two points.

**Their safety.** If you are much bigger, do not slam your back down onto a smaller partner's leg or trapped ankle to strip a hook, and do not roll backwards onto them at speed. Knees and shoulders are what get hurt here.

**Legality.** Every escape on this page is legal at every belt in every ruleset.
