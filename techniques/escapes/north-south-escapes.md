# North–South Escapes

> The pin where your legs are irrelevant and there is no obvious direction to go — so you stop trying to make distance and change the angle instead.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom north–south (**−2** on the atlas ladder) |
| **Comes from** | Side control (they circle toward your head), a stuffed sprawl, a front headlock they abandoned, a passed guard that kept travelling |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 · [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a rates *frame and shrimp out* from north–south at Diff 3 |
| **How common** | 3/5 · north–south itself is rated Com 3 as a position |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** bottom north–south, 69, upside-down side control

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years. You cannot attack from a position you can't survive."* North–south is less common than side control or mount, but it is the one where an untrained bottom player has the fewest ideas — which makes the return on a few hours of study unusually high.

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Parts VII.a and VIII.

---

## 1. Why the position works

North–south is rated **Diff 2 / Com 3** as a top position and priced at **+2** on the ladder, alongside side control and knee-on-belly. They kneel or sprawl over you head-to-toe, chest on your chest, their hips somewhere above your head. The atlas summarises it exactly: *"Kills guard recovery, sets the north–south choke and kimura."*

What makes it awkward is not the pressure — side control is usually heavier — it is the **geometry**:

1. **Your legs are out of the fight entirely.** In every other bottom position, your legs are your best asset and they are at least pointed at the problem. Here they are a metre away from anything that matters. Bottom north–south is the only common pin where you have to escape with your arms and your hips alone.
2. **There is no obvious "away."** Under side control, "away" is a direction: backwards, out the bottom. Under north–south, moving toward your feet just drags you under them, and moving toward your head is into their hips. The escape is not a change of distance; **it is a change of angle.** You are trying to get back to being perpendicular to them, because perpendicular is bottom side control, and bottom side control is a position you have actually trained.
3. **Your arms are above your shoulder line, which is where arms go to die.** Their body sits on top of your shoulders, so your arms are naturally pushed up beside your head. That is exactly the position the [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md) needs, exactly the position the [north–south choke](../submissions/north-south-choke.md) needs, and one step from a [crucifix](../../jujitsu.md).

The single most useful thing to know about this position: **escaping to bottom side control is a success, not a failure.** They are the same number on the ladder, but one of them you know how to escape and the other one you do not.

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames go on their hips.** Not on their shoulders, not on their chest, not on their head. Your hands go on their hip bones with your **elbows bent and tucked to your own ribs**, and the frame's job is to hold about an inch of air between your chest and theirs — not to push them off. It is the only frame in this position with a working angle, because their hips are the part of them that is directly above you and the part you can load through your skeleton.

**Elbows in and *down*.** This is the position-specific rule and the one that matters most. Elbows drifting up beside your head is how every bad thing here happens. If your upper arm is above the level of your shoulder, it is available for a kimura and it is out of the fight for defending your neck. **Elbows below shoulder height, glued to your ribs, hands on their hips.**

**Elbow–knee connection.** It exists here, but only after the angle changes. You cannot bring an elbow to a knee while you are head-to-toe with them — the two are at opposite ends of your body. So the sequence is: **angle first, then elbow to knee.** Get to 45 degrees, then treat it as bottom side control and use the [side control escapes](./side-control-escapes.md).

**Breathing under pressure.** Their sternum or their hips will be sitting on your chest and possibly on your face. **Turn your head to one side and shrug that shoulder toward your ear** — this creates a small air pocket and simultaneously blocks the space the north–south choke needs. Then breathe nasally and shallowly, and take your air in the half-second after they adjust. The atlas is direct that panic is mostly oxygen debt; north–south is one of the positions where that shows up fastest, because the compression is on your upper chest rather than your belly.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never let both arms go above your head.** That is the fastest route to a kimura, a north–south choke or a crucifix. If one arm has to go up, the other stays down.
- **Never push straight-armed into their chest or shoulders.** It achieves nothing (you are pushing them into their own base) and it presents both arms for the kimura.
- **Never try to bridge them over your head.** There is nothing behind you to catch them and no lever to rotate them over. It is the most common instinct here and it is pure wasted energy.
- **Never turn belly-down casually.** If you go, go all the way to a proper turtle on knees and elbows — a half-turn face-down is a back take.
- **Never grip their gi lapels or reach for their belt from underneath.** You are extending an arm past your own head to do it.
- **Never wait.** North–south is a submission position more than a pin, and waiting is how you find out.

## 3. The escapes

### Frame and walk the hips out

**Diff 3/5 · Com 3/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a: *frame and shrimp out*)

The primary escape, and the one that works when nothing else does.

1. **Both hands on their hip bones**, thumbs pointing inward, elbows bent and pressed against your own ribs. If you can only reach one hip, put the other forearm across the front of their near thigh.
2. **Turn your head to one side and shrug that shoulder up.** Air first — everything else is easier once you can breathe, and this simultaneously blocks the choke.
3. **Extend the frames just enough to make an air gap** — one inch between their chest and yours. You are not pushing them off. You are separating two surfaces so that one of them can slide.
4. **Walk your hips out to one side using your feet.** Three or four small steps, not one big heave. This is the one gift the position gives you: your legs are free, and nobody is defending them.
5. **Keep walking until your body is roughly 45 degrees to theirs.** At that point you are effectively in bottom side control — get a frame in, get on your side, and switch to the [side control escapes](./side-control-escapes.md).
6. If they follow and re-square, keep walking. **A north–south player who has to chase your hips cannot also attack your arms or your neck**, so the walk is doing work even when it does not immediately escape.

**The two details people get wrong:** *hands on hips, never on shoulders* — and *you are escaping to side control, not to guard.* Anyone hunting for a route straight back to guard from north–south is going to spend a long time under north–south.

### Turn in and come up (the underhook escape)

Not separately rated in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md); mechanically it is the same come-up as **underhook to dogfight** from side control (Diff 3 / Com 4), and that is a fair estimate of its difficulty.

1. Pick a side and turn your head that way — head position leads.
2. **Thread your near arm under their arm on that side**, palm up, hand climbing to their shoulder blade or lat. Elbow stays low as it goes in.
3. **Small bridge into them** to unweight your shoulders.
4. Turn onto that shoulder and **come up onto your elbow**. Head up, not down — this is the same non-negotiable detail as the side-control dogfight, and for the same reason: a head-down come-up is a choke.
5. Run your hips toward their hips and come up to a knee. You will usually finish at their hip, in a scramble, or behind them.
6. This escape gets *better* the more committed their forward pressure is, which makes it the natural partner to the hip-walk against heavy opponents.

### Escaping backwards to turtle

Not separately rated as an escape; it is a deliberate positional trade.

1. Only if the angle change is genuinely unavailable — usually because they are far heavier than you and settled.
2. **Both elbows to the mat first, then turn.** Turning one elbow at a time gives up your back for free.
3. Post both hands, pull your knees underneath you and arrive in a **proper turtle**: knees under hips, elbows to knees, head up.
4. Deal with the seatbelt and the hooks immediately — see [turtle escapes](./turtle-escapes.md).
5. **Understand the trade.** You are exchanging a **−2** you cannot move in for a **−2** you can move in, at the price of some back-take risk. That is often a good deal, and it is never a free one. Only take it if your turtle is functional.

### Respecting the kimura

The [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md) is rated **Diff 2 / Com 5** and it is more available from north–south than from anywhere else on the mat, because your arms are already where it wants them.

1. **Prevention is the technique.** Elbows below shoulder height, glued to your ribs. A kimura needs your elbow away from your body and your arm bent behind you; deny the first condition and the second never arrives.
2. If they get the figure-four grip, **straighten the arm immediately** and grab something — your own belt, your shorts, your opposite thigh, their leg. The atlas rates *straighten the arm* at Diff 3 / Com 4 and is blunt about the timing: *"once it's bent behind you, you're mostly done."*
3. If the arm is already bending, **turn *into* the kimura side and come up onto your elbow.** Rotating your body in the direction they are rotating your arm relieves the torque. Rolling away from it tightens it.
4. **Tap early.** Shoulder injuries from kimuras are common and slow to heal, and the kimura gives less warning than most joint locks because the shoulder can rotate a long way before it complains.

### Respecting the north–south choke

The [north–south choke](../submissions/north-south-choke.md) is rated **Diff 4 / Com 3** and the atlas describes it as *"slow, inescapable when tight."* Both halves of that sentence matter.

1. **Because it is slow, early defense is nearly free.** Because it is inescapable when tight, late defense does not exist. This is the clearest early-versus-late gap of any choke in this library, and it is why the shrug in §2 is a rule rather than a tip.
2. The choke needs their arm under your neck on one side and their shoulder driving into the other. **Keep your chin tucked and your near shoulder shrugged up toward your ear** — a shrugged shoulder physically fills the space their arm needs.
3. If their arm gets under your neck: **do not wait to see how bad it is.** Turn your chin toward their elbow, get your near hand inside on their bicep, and start walking your hips toward that side immediately.
4. **Every degree of angle you gain kills the squeeze.** The north–south choke depends on you being exactly head-to-toe. It falls apart at about 45 degrees, which is the same 45 degrees your escape was heading for anyway — so against the choke, the escape *is* the defense.
5. If it is tight and your chin is trapped, tap. It works exactly as advertised.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** North–south exaggerates body-type differences more than most positions, because the entire pin is built on where their torso sits relative to your shoulders — and torso length varies enormously between builds.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Much heavier than you** | Your hip frames cannot straighten at all, and their weight is on your upper chest where you have the least leverage and the least air | Change the load path — turn 30 degrees so their weight lands on your shoulder and back rather than your sternum; walk with your feet, not your arms |
| **Much lighter than you** | The pin is easy to move under, which is the trap — they are using north–south as a submission hub, not a pin | Elbows to ribs before hips move; escape on their transitions; treat every north–south as a kimura and choke setup |
| **Very tall / long limbs** | Their arms reach past your hips to your belt while they stay square, and the north–south choke is far more available | Frame at their hip *and* across their near thigh; take the shorter diagonal route or the underhook come-up rather than the long hip-walk |
| **Short and stocky, low centre of gravity** | Their hips sit on your face rather than above your head, and there is no vertical space anywhere | Air first — head turned and shoulder shrugged; guard the arms, because short arms make the kimura easier and the choke harder; the angle change is *shorter* for you, so keep walking |
| **Very strong grippers** | A belt or lapel grip kills the hip-walk before it starts; a gripped wrist above your head is a kimura in progress | Break the hip grip first; never let a gripped wrist travel above your shoulder line — pull it straight down to your hip |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | Their north–south will be unstable and light, so you will try to bench-press them off, which offers up both arms | Hip-walk anyway; treat it as a submission position; watch for the switch to the back when you turn |

### Much heavier than you

- **Change the load path, don't try to reduce the load.** The most valuable adjustment in this position, and it costs nothing: **turn your body 20–30 degrees to one side so their weight lands on your shoulder and upper back rather than square on your sternum.** Weight on your back is survivable almost indefinitely; weight on your sternum shuts down your breathing in under a minute. Do this before you attempt anything else, because everything else requires air.
- **Frames change shape.** Against a much heavier opponent you will not be able to straighten both arms into their hips — and you should not try, because a straightening arm is a kimura offer. Instead, put **one hand on their near hip and one forearm blade across the front of their near thigh**, elbows bent. That combination stops them sprawling forward onto your head, which is the specific thing that makes heavy north–south unbearable.
- **Use your legs, because they are the one thing that is free.** Small steps with your heels, walking your hips sideways along the mat. Three centimetres at a time. This is the one position where a much heavier opponent cannot interfere with your legs at all, and most people never use them.
- **Do not attempt to make space between chests.** You will not create it. Move yourself instead. The distinction between "pushing them away" and "relocating yourself" is the difference between an escape system that works against big people and one that doesn't.
- **Do not turn belly-down under a much heavier opponent** unless you can get your knees under you in the same motion. A flattened face-down position under a heavy opponent is worse than where you started, and much harder to leave.
- **Prevention is cheaper.** North–south is almost always entered from side control, and the moment to stop it is when they begin to circle toward your head. Follow their movement with your hips — turn your body to stay perpendicular to them as they travel. Against a much heavier opponent, staying out of north–south is genuinely easier than getting out of it.

### Much lighter than you

- **The pressure will be manageable and that is exactly the danger.** A lighter opponent is not in north–south to hold you down. They are there because it is the best kimura and north–south choke position in the sport, and because it is a hub they can circle from to side control, knee-on-belly and the back.
- **Arm discipline before hip movement.** Against a heavy opponent you can sort out your air and your frames first. Against a light one, the [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md) arrives before the discomfort does. **Elbows to ribs, immediately, then everything else.**
- **Escape on the transition, not on the pin.** They will not stay still, which means you get an opening every few seconds — but it also means a slow, incremental hip-walk just hands them a new angle. Wait for them to move, then move once, decisively.
- **The hip-walk works easily, so give it a destination.** Walking to nowhere against a mobile opponent is not an escape; they simply circle to your other side and you have spent energy. Walk *with a frame in and a knee ready to insert.*

### Very tall opponents with long limbs

- **They can hold you without repositioning.** Long arms reach past your hips to your belt or your near thigh while their chest stays square on your chest. Against a shorter opponent, holding your hips means giving up chest pressure; against a tall one it does not.
- **Frame higher and wider.** One hand on the hip bone is not enough. Add a **forearm across the front of their near thigh** to stop them sprawling their hips forward over your head, which is the specific control a long-limbed player will use.
- **Their torso is long, so the sideways hip-walk is a longer journey.** Take the **diagonal** instead — walk your hips out and slightly toward their feet rather than straight sideways, which shortens the distance to 45 degrees. Or skip the walk and take the **underhook come-up**, which is a shorter route with a tall opponent because their chest sits high off you.
- **The [north–south choke](../submissions/north-south-choke.md) is a real threat, not a theoretical one.** Long arms wrap a neck from north–south without any repositioning at all. The shrugged shoulder and tucked chin are not optional against this build.
- **The one bit of good news:** long limbs are slow to reposition. When they have to move an arm or a leg, you get a bigger window than a compact opponent would give you.

### Short, stocky opponents with a low centre of gravity

- **Their hips end up on your face, not above your head.** Short torso means that when their chest is on your chest, their hips are much lower relative to you. Practically: the breathing problem is worse and the framing problem is different — their hips are close enough to reach easily, which is a genuine advantage.
- **Air is the first priority and it is achievable.** Head turned hard to one side, shoulder shrugged. Because their hips are close, your bent-elbow frame reaches them comfortably and can actually hold an air gap. Against a tall opponent that frame would be at full extension; against this build it is short and strong.
- **The kimura is easier for them; the north–south choke is harder.** Short arms mean a short, powerful lever on your shoulder — the kimura comes on fast and close. But short arms struggle to wrap all the way around a neck for the choke. **Guard the arms first against this build**, and worry about the neck second. Against a tall opponent, reverse that order.
- **Do not bridge.** A low centre of gravity plus a wide sprawled base means there is nothing to rotate them over. Every bridge is wasted.
- **The angle change pays off faster for you.** There is less of them to travel around, so 45 degrees arrives sooner than it would against a tall opponent. Keep walking; the reward is closer than it feels.

### Very strong grippers

- **The hip grip is the emergency.** In the gi, a hand on your belt or your far pant leg kills the hip-walk completely — and the hip-walk is the primary escape here. Break it before anything else, with **two hands on the wrist and a bridge or a body turn to create slack in the cloth first**. A taut grip does not peel; a slack one does.
- **A gripped wrist must never travel above your shoulder line.** If they have your wrist and start walking it up beside your head, that is a kimura or a crucifix in progress. **Pull it straight down to your own hip immediately** — down, not out to the side.
- **No-gi:** their strong grip will be a wrist tie or a clasped hand around your head and arm. Attack the clasp, not the individual arm. And keep your near elbow inside their hip so they cannot connect the two ends.
- **Never grip fight with the arm you need for your neck.** In this position that means: if one hand leaves its frame, the other stays.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **Their north–south will feel unstable, and you will want to bench-press them off.** Do not. Pushing two straight arms into a lighter opponent's chest from underneath is the single most reliable way to be kimura'd by a smaller person, and it is how most bigger grapplers first learn that the kimura exists.
- **Do the hip-walk anyway.** It works quickly against a lighter opponent and it costs almost nothing. The point is to build the habit while it is easy, so that it exists when you need it against someone your own size.
- **Treat it as a submission position, not a pin.** A smaller opponent in north–south is there for your arms and your neck. That is the entire reason they chose it.
- **Expect them to leave for your back.** When you turn to escape, a lighter opponent will happily abandon north–south to take the back rather than fight to re-square. Turn to your *side*, and do not roll past it.
- **Safety.** Do not drive a bridge up into a lighter partner's chin or sternum, and do not sit up hard into their face. You will connect harder than you intend.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your frames will not reach a tall opponent's hips. Compensate by turning your body 30 degrees earlier than you otherwise would — a turned body shortens the reach — and by using your **legs** more, since the hip-walk does not care about arm length.
- **Long arms.** You can frame their hips from further away and keep your elbows bent while doing it, which is the ideal. The cost is that long arms are more available for a kimura; be strict about keeping your elbows below shoulder height.
- **Long torso.** More distance to travel to reach 45 degrees. Use the diagonal walk and consider the underhook come-up as your primary rather than your backup.
- **Thick torso or restricted shoulder mobility.** The kimura threat is more dangerous for you, because a stiff shoulder reaches its end range earlier. Tap earlier than someone flexible would, and be more conservative about letting an arm drift up.
- **Older or less explosive.** Good news: nothing on this page requires explosiveness. The hip-walk is a patience escape and it is the one that works best. Skip the come-up if your shoulders complain.
- **Previous shoulder injury.** North–south is the highest-risk pin in this library for shoulders. Elbows down as a hard rule, tap early on the kimura, and tell your partner before the round (atlas, Tier 0).

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I can't breathe at all" | Their weight is square on your sternum and your head is straight | Turn your head, shrug the shoulder, and rotate your body 30 degrees so the load lands on your shoulder and back |
| "I push them off and they don't move" | You are pushing against their own base, with straight arms | Frames make an air gap; your *feet* do the escaping. Elbows bent, hips walking |
| "I keep getting kimura'd from here" | Your elbows are drifting above your shoulder line | Elbows below shoulder height and glued to your ribs, as a permanent rule in this position |
| "I walk my hips out and they just re-square" | You are walking without a frame or a destination | Frame in, walk to 45 degrees, then immediately switch to side control escapes — don't keep walking past the win |
| "I bridge and nothing happens" | There is nothing behind you to roll them over; north–south has no bridge | Stop bridging. This position is escaped sideways, never upwards |
| "The choke was on before I noticed" | The north–south choke is slow and quiet, and you were focused on the pressure | Chin tucked and shoulder shrugged as a default posture, not as a reaction |
| "I turn away and lose my back" | You turned belly-down one elbow at a time | Both elbows to the mat first, then all the way to a proper turtle — or don't go |
| "I escape into worse side control than I started with" | You arrived at 45 degrees flat, with no frame | The escape ends with a frame in and you on your side, not with the angle change alone |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from north–south.** Top player starts settled, chest on chest. **Bottom wins by reaching 45 degrees with a frame in** — not by recovering guard. **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Defining the win as "side control" rather than "escaped" is the whole point of the drill.

**2. Arm discipline, no escaping.** Top player does nothing but hunt for an arm above your shoulder line. You may not escape; you may only keep your elbows down and your hands on their hips. **60 seconds each, three rounds.** This is the drill that prevents the kimuras.

**3. The hip-walk, isolated.** Top player is completely passive and heavy. You do nothing but walk your hips from head-to-toe alignment to 45 degrees, using your feet. **Five reps each side**, slowly, focusing on taking small steps rather than one heave.

**4. Choke-defense positional round.** Top player may *only* attack the north–south choke. Bottom player's job is to never let it start. **Two minutes each.** You will discover that the shrugged shoulder does more work than anything you do with your hands.

**5. The transition round.** Top player must circulate continuously between side control, north–south and knee-on-belly. Bottom player may only move during a transition. **Three minutes each.** This is the habit that stops north–south ever settling on you in the first place.

## 8. Safety

**Shoulders.** This is the position most likely to cost you a shoulder in this library. The [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md) from north–south comes on fast, with a short warning arc, and the shoulder can rotate a long way before pain arrives. **Tap early**, and understand that shoulder injuries measure in months.

**Breathing.** If their weight or their hip is on your face and you genuinely cannot breathe, **say so out loud and reset.** Verbalising is the same skill family as tapping — the atlas is explicit that the tap is a technique, not a failure. Nothing about north–south is worth a panicked minute without air.

**Neck.** The [north–south choke](../submissions/north-south-choke.md) is a strangle, not a crank, when applied correctly — but a sloppy one loads the cervical spine. If your neck rather than your head is what hurts, tap and say why.

**Their safety.** If you are much bigger, do not drive bridges up into a smaller partner's face, and do not come up onto your knees through their chest. If you are the top player reading this for context: north–south is a position where people accidentally sit on faces. Watch where your hips are.

**Legality.** Every escape on this page is legal at every belt in every major ruleset.
