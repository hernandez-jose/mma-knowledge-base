# Knee-on-Belly Escapes

> The least stable pin in the sport and the most dangerous position to panic in — because knee-on-belly does not want to hold you down, it wants to make you flinch.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom knee-on-belly (**−2** on the atlas ladder) |
| **Comes from** | Side control (the most common route), a completed pass, a scramble, mount being escaped into a re-pass |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 · the atlas rates the *knee push* at 2; the bridge-and-roll is not separately rated (see §3) |
| **How common** | 4/5 · the *knee push* escape is rated Com 4 and knee-on-belly itself is rated Com 4 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** KOB, knee ride, knee-in-belly, joelho na barriga

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years."* Knee-on-belly is where that pays off fastest, because the escape window is short, obvious and repeatable — and because the alternative is an [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md).

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Parts VII.a and VIII.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Knee-on-belly is rated **Diff 2 / Com 4** and worth **+2** on the ladder — and under IBJJF rules it scores **2 points held for 3 seconds** (atlas Part XII). One shin across your belly or lower sternum, the other leg posted wide with the foot on the mat, their posture tall and their hands on your collar, your hip, or your far arm.

The atlas gets the essential character of it in one line: *"Uncomfortable, mobile, and worth 2 points. Their panic feeds the armbar."*

Understand the two things that make this position different from every other pin:

1. **It is the least stable pin in the sport.** Their base is high and narrow front-to-back — one shin and one posted foot. They are not glued to you; they are balanced on you. Almost nothing about knee-on-belly is designed to keep you there.
2. **It is the most dangerous pin in the sport.** Precisely because it is unstable, it is *mobile*: from here they can travel to mount, side control, north–south or the back faster than from anywhere else. And because it is uncomfortable, it produces reactions — and every panicked reaction you make offers up a limb. The [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md), rated Com 5 and roughly 20% of all finishes, is one step away and is specifically fed by the two things people instinctively do here.

So the position is a trap with a timer. **They are usually not trying to hold you. They are collecting two points and waiting for you to give them something.** Your job is to move before they settle and to move without offering a limb — in that order.

---

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames.** Both hands on the knee. **Never one hand.** One hand pushing a knee is a straight arm across your own body with the elbow flared, and that is an armbar with the setup already done. The standard shape is **near hand on their knee or shin, far hand on their far hip or their posted foot**, with both elbows bent and low. If you cannot reach both, put both hands on the knee itself, side by side.

**Turn your hip up.** This is the cheapest and most valuable adjustment in the position, and it takes half a second: turn your near hip toward them so their shin slides off your soft belly and onto your **hip bone**. Bone takes the pressure fine; your diaphragm and your floating ribs do not. Turning the hip up costs nothing, hurts them not at all, and changes the entire experience of the position.

**Elbow–knee connection.** Same win condition as everywhere else: get your **near elbow to your near knee** so a shin can come in front of their hip. The difference here is that the space you need already exists — their knee is up off the mat by definition. You are not creating a gap; you are occupying one before they fill it.

**Breathing under pressure.** A shin on the diaphragm makes inhaling almost impossible, so stop trying to inhale against it. **Exhale under the knee, and take your breath in the moment they shift.** Do not try to inflate your chest against a loaded shin; you will fail, and the failure feels like drowning, which is where the panic comes from. The atlas's line applies exactly: panic is mostly oxygen debt.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never push their knee with one straight arm.** Armbar. This is the single most common way people are submitted from knee-on-belly, and it is a self-inflicted wound.
- **Never reach up past their knee toward their collar, their head or their far shoulder.** Same problem, worse: now your arm is across your own centreline with your elbow exposed.
- **Never grab their lapel from underneath (gi).** That is [baseball bat choke](../../jujitsu.md) country — the atlas rates it Diff 4 / Com 2 and lists knee-on-belly as its most common launch point.
- **Never turn belly-down.** They are already high and mobile; turning away gives them the back for free.
- **Never sit up into them.** You will meet a front headlock or a [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md), and you will have given away your hips as well.
- **Never freeze and wait for it to pass.** Every other pin rewards patience. This one does not. **Waiting is the mistake.**

## 3. The escapes

### Catching it before it lands

Not separately rated; this is prevention, and it is the highest-percentage "escape" on the page.

1. Knee-on-belly almost always comes from side control. **The knee has to travel across your body to get there, and that transit is your window** — about one second wide.
2. As their knee comes over, **catch it with your near elbow and forearm**: elbow to your own hip, forearm on their shin, no straight arms.
3. **Turn your near hip up into them** at the same time.
4. **Insert your knee into the space their knee just left.** Their leg has vacated the mat next to your hip; that gap is yours if you take it immediately.
5. If you are late, do not chase it — go straight to two hands on the knee below.

**The line to remember: the best knee-on-belly escape happens before the knee lands.** Every second later costs you options.

### Two hands on the knee → shrimp and re-insert (the knee push)

**Diff 2/5 · Com 4/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a: *knee push — two hands on the knee, shrimp, and re-insert your legs before they settle*)

1. **Near hand on their knee or shin; far hand on their far hip or their posted foot.** Both elbows bent, both below your own chest line.
2. **Turn onto your near side, toward them.** Turning *into* them slides the knee onto your hip bone and puts your body on the angle you need. Turning away exposes your back and drives the knee deeper into your belly.
3. **Shrimp your hips away** — hips travel backwards, toward their feet — while your two hands hold the knee at a constant distance. The hands do not push; they hold the geometry while your hips do the work.
4. **Thread your near knee into the gap** the moment it appears, and turn the shin across their hip so bone holds the space.
5. Recover to half guard, or get a foot on their hip and build open guard. Get *something* attached before they re-settle.

**The two details that matter:** *turn into them, never away* — and *both hands on the knee before you move a single centimetre.* One hand is an armbar, and no amount of hip movement fixes that.

### Bridge-and-roll

Not separately rated from knee-on-belly in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md). The nearest atlas entry is **bridge-and-roll (ghost)** from side control at **Diff 3 / Com 4**, and that is a reasonable estimate here.

1. **Set the frame first.** Two hands on the knee, hip turned up. Never roll from a position where they can simply drop the knee back in.
2. **Time it.** You want the moment their weight commits over the knee — as they lean to attack a collar, reach across for your far arm, or begin to step to mount.
3. **Take the post away.** Reach your far arm around the outside of their **posted leg**, at the knee or the hamstring, and pull it toward you. Their base is a shin and a post; remove the post and there is only the shin.
4. Bring your near hand to their near hip or knee so they cannot simply sit back down on that side.
5. **Heels close to your bum, then bridge diagonally into them**, driving your hips up and toward the side you removed the post from.
6. Follow them over. You land in their guard, in top half, or in a scramble.
7. **If they sprawl the leg back out of your grip, abandon immediately.** Do not keep pulling on a leg you have lost — go straight back to the knee push. A committed roll against a re-based opponent is how you end up mounted.

### Why panicking here gives up the armbar

Not an escape. The reason the escapes above are written the way they are.

Knee-on-belly is *engineered* to produce a reaction. It is uncomfortable in a way side control is not, and discomfort produces two specific instinctive movements:

1. **Shoving the knee away with a straight, flared arm.**
2. **Reaching up toward their collar or their head to push them off.**

Both put your elbow past your own centreline with the arm extended and the shoulder unsupported. That is not "a mistake that could lead to an armbar" — **that is the armbar setup, performed by you, on yourself.** From knee-on-belly they simply trap the arm, step over your head, and sit down. It is one of the highest-percentage submissions in the sport (Com 5, and the atlas puts the armbar at roughly 20% of all finishes) and this is one of its easiest entries.

Three more panic reactions and what they cost:

| The instinct | What it gives them |
|---|---|
| Turning belly-down to get away from the pressure | The back (**−4**), for free |
| Sitting up into them | A front headlock or a [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md), plus your hips |
| Grabbing their lapel to pull them off (gi) | The baseball bat choke, which is *launched* from knee-on-belly |

**The rule that prevents all four: nothing goes above your own sternum, and nothing straightens.** Your hands live on their knee and their hip. If you need to push, push with two bent arms into one point. And escape with your **hips**, not your arms — the arms hold the shape, the hips do the moving.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** Knee-on-belly changes character more dramatically with body type than any other pin, because the whole position is one point of contact — and the amount of mass behind that point, and the height it comes from, are everything.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Much heavier than you** | Their knee alone can be more weight than you can hold off with your arms, and it lands on your diaphragm | Turn the hip up so the shin sits on bone, not soft tissue; hold the *angle* with your frames and move your hips out — never try to lift the knee |
| **Much lighter than you** | It does not hurt, so you relax — and the danger here was never the pressure | Treat it as a submission position; keep the hands low; escape on their movement, and fill the space with a knee as you make it |
| **Very tall / long limbs** | Their posted leg gives them a base with no edge to tip, and long arms control your collar and your far hip simultaneously | Forget the roll; attack the daylight under their posted leg, and use the fact that they must bend a long way down to attack you |
| **Short and stocky, low centre of gravity** | Compact, low, and impossible to bridge; the shin drives straight down instead of at an angle | Hip up and hip out — pure frames and hip movement, no bridging. But their narrow front-to-back base makes the roll better than against a tall opponent, *if* you take the post |
| **Very strong grippers** | A collar grip plus a hip grip pins your torso and sets both the baseball bat choke and the far-side armbar | Strip the collar hand first — it is the dangerous one — then the hip grip. Never grab back at their collar from underneath |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | It barely registers as pressure, so you will lie there and get armbarred | Treat it as a submission position; the roll genuinely works for you, but control it — a trapped leg plus your weight is a knee injury |

### Much heavier than you

- **Get the knee onto bone. This is the whole game.** A much heavier opponent's knee-on-belly is not a positional problem first, it is a breathing problem first — a shin loaded with 100 kg on your diaphragm shuts you down in seconds. **Turn your near hip up toward them so the shin slides from your solar plexus onto the front of your hip bone.** It takes half a second, requires no strength, and converts an emergency into an inconvenience. Do this before you think about escaping.
- **Never try to lift the knee.** You cannot, and every attempt straightens an arm. Your two hands are there to **hold the angle** — to stop the knee sliding back onto the soft target and to stop them driving it deeper — while your **hips** move out from underneath. Hands hold, hips escape. That division of labour is the escape.
- **The bridge-and-roll is low percentage and you should mostly skip it.** A much heavier opponent with a wide posted leg is not going over. Instead, use the bridge the same way you use it under [mount](./mount-escapes.md): **bridge only to make them post a hand or square their base**, then take the space with the knee push while they recover. Same pattern, same logic — the bridge is a question, not an answer.
- **You will get more time than you expect.** Heavier opponents tend to settle in knee-on-belly rather than travel from it, because settling is comfortable for them and travelling is work. Use that: set both hands, turn the hip, breathe once, *then* move. Against a lighter opponent you would not get that luxury.
- **Say something.** Knee-on-belly under a much heavier partner is one of the most common rib-injury positions in training, and the mechanism is exactly a loaded shin on a floating rib. "Too heavy" mid-round is an adult thing to say and the same skill family as tapping.

### Much lighter than you

- **The absence of pain is the trap.** A lighter opponent's knee-on-belly will feel like almost nothing, so you will lie there and let it happen. Meanwhile they are not there for the pressure at all — they are there because it is one of the best armbar and collar-choke positions in the sport, and because it is a hub they can travel from.
- **They will not settle; they will circulate.** Expect them to bounce between knee-on-belly, mount, north–south and the back, hunting an arm the whole time. Your escape has to happen *on their movement*, and it has to be one committed action rather than a slow negotiation.
- **They can ride your escape.** This is the specific failure against light opponents: you shrimp, you make a beautiful gap, and they simply step over into mount through the space you created. The fix is to **shrimp with the knee already threading**, so the space you make is occupied in the same motion. Never make space you are not immediately filling.
- **The roll needs a trap and a moment.** A lighter opponent will pull their posted leg back the instant you touch it, so the bridge-and-roll only works if you take the post *as* they commit their weight forward, not before.
- **Hands stay low anyway.** All the armbar and lapel discipline from §3 applies more against a lighter opponent, not less.

### Very tall opponents with long limbs

- **There is no edge to their base, so stop looking for one.** A long posted leg means their base is enormous front-to-back, and the bridge-and-roll has nothing to rotate them over. Spend that energy elsewhere.
- **Attack the daylight instead.** A tall opponent's knee-on-belly has a lot of air in it — the gap between their posted thigh and your body is much larger than a compact opponent's. **Get your far knee or your far arm into that gap**, use it as a lever against their posted leg, and turn in. This is the escape that exists specifically against tall opponents and almost nobody uses it.
- **Turning your hip up matters more, not less.** A long shin covers more of your torso, so more of your soft tissue is under load. Get onto the hip bone immediately.
- **Their reach is the problem upstairs.** Long arms let them control your collar and your far hip at the same time while staying tall and balanced. That is a real control and you will not out-position it — but it forces a compromise: **to actually attack you, a tall opponent has to bend a long way down.** That bend is your window. When their head comes toward you, their base shortens and their weight comes forward; move then.

### Short, stocky opponents with a low centre of gravity

- **Do not bridge.** Their mass sits low, their base is compact, and a bridge has no lever to work with. Everything here is frames and hip movement.
- **Expect them to convert rather than travel.** A short, heavy knee-on-belly player is usually passing through toward mount or back to side control rather than staying mobile and tall. So your window is the conversion, not the pin.
- **But the roll is genuinely more available than against a tall opponent — if you take the post.** This is the useful inversion: a short opponent's posted leg is *short*, which means their base is narrow front-to-back even though it is stable side-to-side. Remove the post and there is much less base left than a tall opponent would have. So: skip the roll against tall opponents, consider it against short ones.
- **Their short arms bring their weight to you.** To attack your collar or your far arm, a short-armed opponent has to come close, which brings their centre of mass over you and lightens their post. That is the moment to move — and it arrives more often against this build than any other.

### Very strong grippers

- **The collar grip is the emergency, not the hip grip.** This is the reverse of the priority in [side control](./side-control-escapes.md), and the reason is specific: a strong collar grip from knee-on-belly is a [baseball bat choke](../../jujitsu.md) or a cross-collar attack already half-built, and both are finishing threats rather than control problems. **Strip it first**, with two hands and a hip turn.
- **Then the hip grip**, which is what stops your shrimp.
- **Never grab their collar back.** From underneath knee-on-belly, reaching up to grip their lapel puts your arm exactly where the armbar and the choke both want it. If you want a gi grip from here, take their **sleeve at the wrist** or their **pant leg**, both of which keep your elbow low.
- **No-gi:** their strong grip will be a hand on your far hip or a cross-face wrist tie. Peel it with two hands as you turn into them — and note that a strong hand on your far hip is what makes the knee push hard, so it is worth the moment it costs.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **The pressure will be nothing and you will lie there.** This is the whole problem. A lighter opponent's knee-on-belly does not hurt, so your body never generates the urgency the position deserves — and then you get armbarred by someone 25 kg lighter and are genuinely surprised.
- **Treat it as a submission position from the first second.** Hands low, elbows in, nothing above your sternum. The discipline has to be intellectual because it will not be instinctive.
- **The roll actually works for you — with a caveat.** Your size advantage converts cleanly here, because knee-on-belly is a balance position and balance is exactly what mass beats. But **rolling a much lighter partner while their leg is trapped under your arm is a knee-injury mechanism.** Take the post, roll, and *release the leg as they go over*. Do not hold it and torque it.
- **Their leg is not a handle.** Do not yank a smaller partner's posted leg out from under them at speed.
- **Escape without strength on purpose.** If you cannot do the knee push against someone much lighter using only frames and hip movement, you do not have the escape.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Reaching their far hip or their posted foot may not be available. Put both hands on the knee itself, side by side, and do all the work with your hips. It is a smaller frame but a stronger one.
- **Long arms.** You can reach the far hip and the posted leg comfortably, which makes the bridge-and-roll a more realistic option for you than for most. The cost is that a long arm is easier to armbar — keep the elbows genuinely bent.
- **Long legs.** Knee insertion is easy and you can often get a foot on their hip from a long way out. Prioritise re-guarding rather than rolling.
- **Thick torso or a big midsection.** A shin sinks into soft tissue rather than sitting on it, which makes this position disproportionately unpleasant for you. **The hip turn is not optional** — make it the automatic first response before you even open your eyes.
- **Older or less explosive.** Skip the bridge-and-roll entirely. The knee push is a low-power escape and it is reliable; make it your only escape and get very good at the early version.
- **Previous rib or sternum injury.** This is the position most likely to aggravate it. Turn the hip up immediately, tell your partner before the round (atlas, Tier 0), and do not try to be tough about a loaded shin.

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I can't breathe at all" | The shin is on your diaphragm and you are trying to inhale against it | Turn the hip up so it lands on bone; exhale under the knee, breathe in the gaps |
| "I keep getting armbarred from here" | You are pushing with one straight arm, or reaching above your sternum | Two bent arms, both below your chest line, always. Hands hold, hips move |
| "I push their knee and they get heavier" | You are pushing rather than framing, and driving your own shoulders into the mat | The hands hold the angle; the escape comes from your hips going backwards |
| "I make space and they mount through it" | You shrimped first and threaded the knee second | Thread as you shrimp. Never make space you are not filling in the same motion |
| "I roll them and they land in mount" | You rolled without removing the post, or without trapping their near side | Take the posted leg first, and control their near hip so they can't sit back down |
| "I turn away to relieve the pressure and lose my back" | You turned away instead of into them | Always turn *into* the knee. Away is the back; into is the escape |
| "They choke me before I can do anything (gi)" | You grabbed their lapel, or let a collar grip get depth | Strip collar grips first; never take a lapel grip from underneath knee-on-belly |
| "I sit up to get out and end up in a guillotine" | Sitting up hands them your head and your hips at once | Stay on your back, turn onto your side, and move your hips out |
| "By the time I start escaping, they've moved on" | You waited. Knee-on-belly is the one pin where waiting loses | Catch it before it lands, or move within the first second or two |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from knee-on-belly.** Top starts with the knee on and posture set. **Bottom wins by getting a shin in front of their hip.** Top wins by holding for the round, advancing, or submitting. **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Per the atlas, positional rounds give roughly three times the reps of the relevant situation compared with open rolling.

**2. The transit drill.** Top player goes from side control to knee-on-belly and back, repeatedly and slowly. Bottom player does nothing but **catch the knee with a bent elbow and turn the hip up** each time. **Twenty repetitions.** This trains the escape that happens before the position exists.

**3. Hands-low discipline.** Top player attacks nothing but the armbar and, in the gi, the collar. Bottom player may not escape — the only job is to keep both hands on the knee and hip, below the chest line, for **60 seconds.** Two rounds each. Unglamorous, and it will stop you being armbarred by white belts.

**4. Hip-turn reps against a heavy partner.** Get the heaviest willing partner in the room to put a knee on your belly at about 60% weight. Practise nothing but **turning the hip so the shin lands on bone.** Ten reps each side. You will feel the difference immediately and remember it permanently.

**5. Post-removal reps.** Partner holds knee-on-belly with a wide post. You practise reaching the posted leg, pulling it in and bridging — **five reps each side**, with them resisting at about 50%. Learn how quickly a good opponent sprawls the leg back, so you know how narrow the timing is.

## 8. Safety

**Ribs and sternum.** A loaded shin on floating ribs is a genuine injury mechanism, and knee-on-belly under a much heavier partner is one of the most common places it happens. **Turn your hip up, and say something if it is too much.** Verbalising is not quitting; it is the same category of skill as tapping, which the atlas rates as a technique in its own right.

**The armbar.** Tap early. The [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) from knee-on-belly arrives fast because they are already above your arm line, and hyperextended elbows do not heal on a training-friendly schedule.

**Chokes.** In the gi, the baseball bat choke from knee-on-belly is fast and often arrives while you are focused on the knee. It is a legitimate strangle and it will put you out — tap when it is tight, do not ride it.

**Their safety, if you are the bigger one.** Do not rip a smaller partner's posted leg out from under them, and release the leg as they roll rather than holding it. Knees get hurt in exactly that moment.

**Legality.** Every escape on this page is legal at every belt in every major ruleset. Knee-on-belly itself scores 2 points under IBJJF after 3 seconds of control — worth knowing, because a competitor often has no intention of staying and is simply banking points on the way to mount.
