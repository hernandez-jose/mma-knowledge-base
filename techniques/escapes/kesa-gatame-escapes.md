# Kesa Gatame (Scarf Hold) Escapes

> The heaviest pin most people ever feel, and the most committed. Kesa is strong in exactly one direction — and it hands you their back if they lean the wrong way.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom kesa gatame (**−2** on the atlas ladder, a variant of side control) |
| **Comes from** | Side control (they sit through), a judo throw finished directly into the pin, a headlock on the ground, a failed guard retention |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 · [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a rates the *kesa escape* at Diff 3 |
| **How common** | 2/5 · the kesa escape is rated Com 2; kesa gatame itself is rated Com 3 as a position |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** scarf hold, headlock pin, 袈裟固, and — in most BJJ gyms — "the one the judo guy does"

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years."* Kesa is the lowest-frequency position in this library, which is exactly why untrained people get stuck in it for entire rounds. A couple of hours of specific work here permanently removes a position that otherwise beats you every time it appears.

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Parts II.a and VII.a.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Kesa gatame is rated **Diff 2 / Com 3** as a position, and the atlas describes it precisely: *"Old-school judo pin: sitting beside them, one arm wrapping their head, their near arm trapped under your armpit. Heavy and strong — but a fully committed position that skilled opponents can bridge out of."*

The mechanics, from underneath:

1. **They sit beside you with their hip on the mat**, chest crossing your chest diagonally, legs split wide — one leg extended toward your head, one bent or extended toward your feet. That split is a very wide base *toward their own legs*.
2. **One arm cradles your head or neck.** This does the same job the crossface does in side control: it steers your head, and your hips follow your head.
3. **Your near arm is trapped under their armpit.** This is the part people underestimate. You are not fighting from underneath with two arms and one problem — you are fighting with **one arm**, and the trapped one is simultaneously a submission target.
4. **Their weight lands on your ribs at an angle**, which usually means one lung is doing most of the work.

**And here is the trade they have made.** Their base is a "T": enormously strong toward their legs and their seat, and almost nonexistent **over their own shoulder line**. There is nothing behind them. That is why the classic escape direction is *toward their head*, not toward their legs — and why the atlas calls it "a fully committed position."

**The over-rotation gift.** Because they are sitting beside you facing your head, their **back is already turned toward your near side.** If they rotate their chest any further toward your head — chasing the head control, reaching for a submission, or simply leaning in as you bridge — they present their back to you directly. This is the only pin in this library where the escape and the back take are the *same movement*. It also means kesa is a poor choice against someone who knows it: a good top player will bail to side control or reverse kesa rather than over-commit, and if they don't, you go from **−2** to **+4** in one turn.

Their attacks from here: the [americana](../submissions/americana.md) and [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) on your trapped arm, a straight armlock across their thigh, head-and-arm pressure on your neck, and in the gi a collar attack. Note what is *missing*: from kesa proper they cannot easily mount you and cannot take your back. It is a pin that trades mobility for pressure.

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames.** Your one free arm is your only frame, so it has to be in the right place. **The far hand goes to the front of their neck or their near collarbone, forearm bladed, elbow low and bent.** This is the neck frame, and it is what stops them shifting their weight forward onto your head and tightening the cradle. Frame where you can keep your elbow bent — a straight-armed frame at full reach is not a frame, it is an offer.

**The trapped arm: bend it.** The single most important detail on this page. **A straight trapped arm is an armbar; a bent trapped arm is just an arm.** Walk your trapped hand up their back, or grab your own belt, your own waistband, their belt — anything that keeps the elbow flexed. You may never get that arm *free* against a good kesa, and that is fine. You must always get it *bent*.

**Elbow–knee connection.** It works the normal way here, and it is available: bring your **near knee up toward your near elbow** as you turn onto your near shoulder. A shin across their hip converts kesa into open guard. The near knee is the escape route, because their legs are on that side and nothing is defending it from underneath.

**Breathing under pressure.** Their chest crosses yours diagonally, so one side of your ribcage is loaded and the other is not. **Turn your far shoulder up slightly and breathe into your far side.** Nasally, shallowly, and take your air as they adjust. The atlas is direct: panic is mostly oxygen debt, and kesa produces more of it than the pressure alone justifies, because the head cradle also makes people feel trapped.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never let the trapped arm straighten.** Everything else on this page is negotiable; this is not.
- **Never lie flat with your legs extended.** Heels near your bum, knees live. Straight legs remove your bridge and put every kilogram they own onto your ribs.
- **Never try to escape toward their legs.** That is their base, it is enormous, and you will spend a round finding out.
- **Never push against their head or shoulder with a straight far arm.** It achieves nothing and it hands them your second arm.
- **Never yank your head out of the cradle.** Your head comes free because your hips moved. Pulling on it just tightens the cradle and tires your neck.
- **Never ignore a switch to reverse kesa.** If they turn to face your feet (atlas: reverse kesa, Diff 3 / Com 2), every threat changes — the armbar and leg entries become live and the head control disappears. Respond to the switch, don't finish the escape you started.

## 3. The escapes

### Freeing the trapped arm

Not separately rated; it is the prerequisite for everything below.

1. **Bend the elbow.** Drive your trapped hand toward your own hip or up toward their back. A bent arm cannot be armbarred and cannot be americana'd from this angle.
2. **Give the hand a home.** Grab your own belt or waistband, or reach across and grip their back. A hand with a job stops drifting back into danger.
3. If they have it straight and are leaning back for the armlock: **turn your thumb toward the ceiling and roll toward them onto your near side.** That is the atlas's *hide the thumb / bicep curl* defense (Diff 3 / Com 5) applied here — rotate the arm out of the plane they are levering in.
4. **Only then start bridging.** A bridge with a straight trapped arm feeds them the armlock with your own hips.

### Frame on the neck and bridge

**Diff 3/5 · Com 2/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a: *kesa escape — frame on the neck, bridge, and spin out to the far side or take the back*)

1. **Far hand frames the front of their neck or near collarbone**, elbow low and bent. Trapped arm bent, hand parked on their back or your own belt.
2. Both heels close to your bum, feet flat, knees up.
3. **Bridge over your far shoulder — toward their head, never toward their legs.** Drive your hips up and rotate them over their own shoulder line, into the direction where they have no base.
4. **Watch what they do.** If they cannot post, keep turning and roll them completely; you land on top.
5. **If they post their far hand to stop it — and against anyone competent, they will — that post is the escape.** Their weight has committed forward onto that hand, their hip has lifted off the mat, and the space you need has just appeared.
6. Go straight into the hip-out or the back take below. Do not bridge again on the same side.

**The two details that matter:** *bridge toward their head, never toward their legs* — and *the bridge's real job is to make them post; the post is what opens everything else.*

### Hip out and recover guard

**Diff 3/5 · Com 2/5**

1. Straight off the bridge, **turn onto your near shoulder** — the side they are sitting on — and bring your **near knee up toward your near elbow**.
2. **Frame with your far hand into their near shoulder or their hip** to hold the space the bridge just made.
3. **Shrimp your hips away from theirs**, travelling toward their feet. Small movements, held each time.
4. **Insert your near shin across their hip or belly** and turn the knee in. That shin is now doing the work your arm was doing.
5. Build a knee shield, get the far foot on their hip, and recover open guard — or come up to a knee if the scramble is there.

**The detail that matters:** *your head comes free because your hips moved.* People try to pull their head out of the cradle first and then wonder why they cannot move their hips. Hips first; the head follows for free.

### Escape to the back

**Diff 3/5 · Com 2/5** — the same atlas entry, and the reason kesa is a risk for them to hold.

1. Frame the neck, bridge, and **turn onto your near shoulder** — identical first steps.
2. As you turn in, notice where you are: **your chest is now facing their back.** That is not a coincidence; it is the structural cost of the position they chose.
3. **Reach your far arm over their far shoulder or under their far armpit** to start a seatbelt.
4. **Bring your near knee up behind their hip** and post your near hand on the mat.
5. Come up onto your knees behind them, secure the seatbelt properly, and either insert hooks or flatten them out.
6. You have gone from **−2** to **+4** in one movement. Under IBJJF that is four points and, per the atlas, the position with the highest finish rate in the sport.

**The detail that matters:** *the more they rotate their chest toward your head, the more of their back they hand you.* This escape is the punishment for over-commitment, so it becomes more available the harder they try to hold you.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** Kesa is unusual in that its weak direction is fixed by geometry — it is always over their shoulder line — but *whether you can get there* changes completely with their build. Against some builds you roll them; against others the roll is a fantasy and the bridge is only a decoy.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Much heavier than you** | You cannot roll them over their own head, and their angled chest shuts down one lung | Frame the neck first for air; bridge only to force the post; make the hip-out to knee shield your actual escape |
| **Much lighter than you** | The pressure is manageable, which hides the fact that they are there for your trapped arm, not to pin you | Bend the trapped arm first; the full roll genuinely works, so take it — and follow them if they bail to north–south |
| **Very tall / long limbs** | The head cradle is deep, they can reach your far arm too, and your neck frame ends up at full extension | Frame at their *elbow*, not their neck — keep your elbow bent. Their base is behind them regardless of height, so the roll stays available |
| **Short and stocky, low centre of gravity** | Extremely tight, extremely low, and there is no gap under their armpit at all | Do not try to free the arm — just bend it. Skip the roll entirely and go hip-out; their shallow head cradle is the weak link |
| **Very strong grippers** | A belt grip kills the shrimp and a collar grip kills the bridge; in no-gi the head-and-arm clasp locks everything | Break the hip grip first (it stops the escape), the collar second (it only stops the bridge). Create slack before peeling |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | It won't feel heavy, so you will sit up straight into the head control and get cranked or armbarred | Still frame, still bend the arm. The roll is genuinely yours — but land it controlled, and expect them to bail |

### Much heavier than you

- **Kesa from a much heavier opponent is a breathing problem before it is a positional problem.** Their chest crosses your ribcage diagonally, which loads one side and effectively takes a lung out of service. **Turn your far shoulder up a few degrees so their chest lands on your ribs rather than your sternum**, and breathe into the far side. Do this in the first two seconds, before you plan anything, because every subsequent decision depends on having air.
- **Set the neck frame first, and set it properly.** Against a heavy opponent the neck frame is not about creating space — it is about **stopping them shifting their weight forward onto your head**, which is what turns a bad pin into an unescapable one. Elbow low, forearm bladed, load running down the bone.
- **The roll is not going to happen, and planning around it wastes the round.** You cannot rotate a much heavier person over their own shoulder line from underneath. **Use the bridge purely to force the post**, exactly as with the [mount upa](./mount-escapes.md) and the [knee-on-belly](./knee-on-belly-escapes.md) bridge. Bridge, they post, their hip lifts, and you take three inches of space with the hip-out. That is your escape.
- **The hip-out to knee shield is the highest-percentage route**, and half guard is a legitimate finish line. Do not hold out for the back take against someone much heavier; take the shin frame and go.
- **Your trapped arm is at genuine risk.** A heavier opponent leaning back onto a straight arm generates a lot of torque very quickly, and the shoulder and elbow both take it. **Bend it or tap** — there is no third option worth having.
- **Say something if your ribs are going.** Kesa under a much heavier partner is one of the most common rib-injury positions in training, alongside bottom side control and knee-on-belly. Verbalising is the same skill family as tapping.

### Much lighter than you

- **They are using kesa as a submission hold, not as a pin.** A lighter opponent cannot crush you, so the reason they chose kesa is your trapped arm — the [americana](../submissions/americana.md), the [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) and the straight armlock over their thigh are all right there, and none of them care about weight.
- **Arm discipline before everything else.** Against a heavier opponent you sort out air first. Against a lighter one you sort out the trapped arm first, because the submission will arrive before the discomfort does.
- **The full roll is genuinely available.** This is one of the few places where the textbook finish actually finishes: a lighter opponent *can* be rotated over their own shoulder line. Bridge, follow them over, land on top. Do not throw away the escape by treating it as a decoy the way you would against someone bigger.
- **Expect them to bail rather than be rolled.** A lighter opponent will feel the bridge coming and switch to north–south, reverse kesa or side control instead. **Follow the escape into the next position rather than resetting** — the moment they leave kesa is the moment their control is weakest, and half of your escapes from [north–south](./north-south-escapes.md) and [side control](./side-control-escapes.md) start with exactly that transition.

### Very tall opponents with long limbs

- **Frame at their elbow, not their neck.** This is the specific adjustment for this build and it is worth more than anything else here. A long-armed opponent's neck is much further away than it looks from underneath, so reaching for it puts your far arm at **full extension** — the weakest position an arm can be in, and an open invitation to have it stripped or attacked. **Frame where your elbow can stay bent:** the crease of their cradling arm, or the point of their near shoulder. A short strong frame beats a long weak one every time.
- **Their long arms reach your far arm too.** A tall opponent in kesa can cradle your head and still get a hand on your far wrist, which removes your only free limb. Keep the far arm **inside**, close to your own chest, and do not let it wander out to the side looking for a grip.
- **The good news: height does not protect them.** Kesa's weakness is over the shoulder line, and that is true regardless of how long their legs are — the wide split base only defends the direction you were never going anyway. **The roll stays on the table against a tall opponent** in a way it does not against a short, heavy one. This is unusual: in most positions on this library, tall opponents are harder to roll. Here they are not.
- **Their long legs mean a fast switch to reverse kesa or to a leg entry.** Watch for it and respond to the switch rather than continuing your original escape.

### Short, stocky opponents with a low centre of gravity

- **Their kesa will be the tightest you ever feel.** Short arms mean a shallow but extremely close cradle, a thick torso means maximum contact, and a low seat means almost no daylight anywhere.
- **You will probably not free the trapped arm, so stop trying.** There is genuinely no gap under a short, thick opponent's armpit. **The requirement is a bent elbow, not a free arm** — get your hand onto their back or your own belt, accept that the arm stays there, and start the escape anyway. People lose whole rounds to kesa because they treat the arm as a prerequisite instead of a safety condition.
- **The roll is off. Completely.** A low centre of gravity plus a wide split base means there is no rotation available over their shoulder line from underneath. Do not spend energy on it, and do not use the bridge as anything but a way to make them post.
- **Their short arms are the weak link, and the weak link is the head cradle.** A short-armed cradle reaches your head but does not get past it — which means **turning your chin toward their body and shrugging your near shoulder can clear the head control**, and clearing the head control is what makes the hip-out possible. Against this build, that is the whole escape: clear the head, then walk the hips out in small increments, holding each gain with the shin.
- **Everything is horizontal.** No bridging, no rolling, no vertical anything. Small hip escapes along the mat, each one locked in.

### Very strong grippers

- **Break the hip grip first, not the collar grip.** In the gi they will typically grip your belt or your far pant leg with the trapping-side hand and your far collar or lapel with the cradling hand. **The hip grip is what stops your shrimp, and the shrimp is your escape.** The collar grip only stops the bridge, and against most builds the bridge was a decoy anyway. Fix the one that blocks the thing you actually intend to do.
- **Create slack, then peel.** A grip is hardest to break when the cloth is taut. **Bridge or move *toward* the grip to make it slack, then strip it in that instant** with two hands. Pulling on a taut grip held by a strong hand is a contest you lose and a habit that costs you your fingers over the years — the atlas is explicit that BJJ finger arthritis is real and near-universal.
- **They may hold their own lapel to lock the cradle.** If the cradling arm has gripped their own gi, the head control will not loosen no matter how you frame. Attack the *grip*, not the cradle — break the hand off the cloth and the whole structure opens at once.
- **No-gi:** the equivalent is a head-and-arm clasp. **Break the clasp, not the arm.** A gable or S-grip fails at the seam between the hands, not at anybody's wrist.

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **It will not feel heavy, and that is the entire problem.** Your instinct will be to sit straight up out of it. Sitting up drives your own head into their cradle, which is how bigger people meet the head-and-arm crank and the reverse-kesa armbar for the first time.
- **Do the technique anyway: frame the neck, bend the trapped arm, then move.** The order does not change just because you outweigh them.
- **The roll is genuinely yours.** Rotating a much lighter opponent over their own shoulder line is well within your means, and it is the fastest exit available. **But land it controlled** — do not dump a smaller partner on their head or neck, and do not follow them over with your full weight landing on their ribs.
- **Expect them to bail early.** A smaller opponent will abandon kesa at the first sign of a real bridge and go to north–south, the back or a leg entry. **Be ready to follow the escape into the next position** rather than expecting one movement to finish it.
- **Watch the trapped arm anyway.** A lighter opponent chose kesa because of that arm. Your size does not protect an elbow.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your far-hand frame will not reach their neck at all against most opponents. **Frame at their near shoulder or their cradling elbow instead** and keep it bent. Get your near knee involved sooner — your shin has more reach than your arm and does not tire.
- **Long arms.** You can reach the neck frame comfortably, which is an advantage; the risk is that a long arm out at full extension is easy for them to strip or attack. Keep the elbow bent even when you do not have to.
- **Long legs.** The knee shield arrives easily once you have space, and the shin frame will reach across their hip from further away than most people's. Prioritise the hip-out.
- **Thick torso or limited spinal extension.** Your bridge is shallow, so treat it as a decoy from the start rather than discovering that mid-round. Live on the hip-out and the shin frame.
- **Previous shoulder or elbow injury.** The trapped arm is exactly what is at risk. Bend it immediately, tap early on the straight armlock, and tell your partner before the round (atlas, Tier 0). This is not a position to be stoic in.
- **Older or less explosive.** The bridge-heavy version of this escape ages badly. The hip-out to knee shield does not — make it your only plan and get good at the neck frame that enables it.
- **A judo background.** You already know this pin from the top, which means you know its weak direction better than most. The BJJ-specific addition is the **back take** in §3: in judo you would settle for turning out, but here the over-rotation is worth **+4**.

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "My shoulder is about to go" | The trapped arm is straight and they are leaning back on it | Bend the elbow and park the hand on their back or your belt — before anything else, every time |
| "I bridge and they don't move at all" | You are bridging toward their legs, which is their base | Bridge over your far shoulder, toward their head, where they have no base |
| "I bridge, they post, and I've got nothing" | You treated the post as a failure instead of as the opening | The post *is* the escape. Drop, hip out, shin in — immediately, on the same beat |
| "I can't get my head out of the cradle" | You are pulling on your head instead of moving your hips | Hips first. The head comes free as a consequence, never as a cause |
| "I can only breathe on one side" | Their chest is loading your sternum square-on | Turn your far shoulder up a few degrees so the weight lands on ribs, and breathe into the far side |
| "My far arm gets stripped or attacked" | You are framing at full extension, reaching for their neck | Frame closer — their shoulder or their cradling elbow — and keep the elbow bent |
| "I escape and end up in north–south, which is worse" | They bailed rather than be rolled, and you reset instead of following | Follow the transition. Their weakest moment is the moment they leave kesa |
| "I sit up and get cranked" | Sitting up drives your own head deeper into the cradle | Turn onto your near shoulder instead of sitting up. Rotate, don't rise |
| "They switch to facing my feet and everything changes" | That is reverse kesa, and it has different threats | Respond to the switch: the head control is gone, but the armbar and leg entries are live |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from kesa.** Top starts fully settled with the head cradle and the trapped arm. **Bottom wins by getting a shin in front of their hip, or by taking the back.** **90 seconds, reset, switch.** Per the atlas, positional rounds produce roughly three times the reps of the relevant situation compared with open rolling — which matters more here than anywhere, because kesa appears rarely in open rolling.

**2. Trapped-arm discipline, no escaping.** Top player attacks nothing but the trapped arm: americana, straight armlock, armbar. Bottom player may not escape — the only job is to keep the elbow bent and the hand parked. **60 seconds each, three rounds.**

**3. Bridge → post → hip-out chain.** Partner's only job is to post their far hand when you bridge. Every time they post, you drop and take the space with the hip-out. **Ten reps.** This builds the reflex that makes kesa escapable against heavy opponents, and it is exactly the same reflex as the [mount hip heist](./mount-escapes.md).

**4. The back-take rep.** Partner deliberately over-rotates their chest toward your head. You turn in, seatbelt, and come up behind them. **Five reps.** Do it slowly and notice how little rotation it takes before their back is available — that is the fact that makes kesa a risk for them.

**5. Build comparison.** Run the same kesa round against the heaviest and the lightest partner you can find. You should discover, without being told, that the roll finishes against one and only ever produces a post against the other.

## 8. Safety

**The trapped arm.** This is the injury to worry about. A straight arm with someone leaning back onto it loads the elbow and the shoulder at the same time, and the [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) from kesa arrives with a short warning arc because the position already has your arm isolated. **Bend it, or tap.** Elbow hyperextensions do not heal on a training-friendly schedule.

**Ribs.** Kesa under a much heavier partner is a genuine rib-injury position — the angled chest concentrates load on one side of your ribcage. Turn the far shoulder up, and **say so out loud** if it is too much. The atlas is explicit that the tap is a technique rather than a failure, and verbalising discomfort belongs in the same category.

**Neck.** Kesa's head control shades into a neck crank easily, especially when the top player is frustrated. IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt (atlas Part XII). If your neck rather than your head is what hurts, tap and say why — do not ride it out to prove the pin is escapable.

**Their safety.** If you complete the roll, control the landing. Rolling someone over their own shoulder line puts their head near the mat, and a smaller partner can arrive there faster than you intend. Bring them over, don't launch them.

**Legality.** Every escape on this page is legal at every belt in every major ruleset. Kesa gatame itself is a legal, scoring pin — under IBJJF it is scored as side control, not as a separate position.
