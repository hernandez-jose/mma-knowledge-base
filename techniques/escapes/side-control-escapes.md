# Side Control Escapes

> The most-visited bad position in jiu-jitsu, and the one where the gap between "surviving" and "escaping" is decided entirely by whether you can still turn your head.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Positional escape — bottom side control (**−2** on the atlas ladder) |
| **Comes from** | Any completed guard pass, a stuffed takedown, a failed sweep, mount or knee-on-belly being escaped badly |
| **Difficulty** | 2–3/5 · per escape, see §3 |
| **How common** | 5/5 · side control is rated Com 5 in the atlas and is "the default landing spot after a pass" |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** bottom side control, under side mount, cross-side bottom, 100 kilos (kesa's cousin — see [kesa gatame escapes](./kesa-gatame-escapes.md))

> [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII: *"escapes are the single highest-return skill in jiu-jitsu for the first two years. You cannot attack from a position you can't survive."* You will spend more time in bottom side control than in any other losing position, because every pass in the sport ends here.

Ratings on this page are taken unchanged from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part VII.a.

---

## 1. Why the position works

Side control is worth **+2** and rated **Diff 1 / Com 5** — the easiest dominant position in the sport to hold and the one everybody lands in. They are chest-to-chest across your torso, perpendicular to you, hips low and wide. Their legs are nowhere near yours, which is the point: **your legs, the best tools you own, have been taken out of the conversation.**

Two control points hold the position together, and everything on this page is an attack on one or the other:

1. **The crossface.** Their shoulder or forearm driven across your jawline, turning your head away from them. This is the single most important control in the position, and it is not about pain — it is about steering. **Your hips follow your head.** If your head is turned away from the direction you need to move, your hips physically cannot get there. A side control without a crossface leaks in five seconds; a side control with a deep crossface can hold a much better grappler for a minute.
2. **The near-hip block.** Their far arm under your head or their near knee/hand jammed against your near hip, stopping your hips from sliding backwards out the bottom. Kill this and the frame-and-shrimp becomes almost free.

Their weight goes through your sternum, which compresses your breathing the same way mount does. From here they have a menu: mount (**+3**), knee-on-belly (**+2**, two points at three seconds), north–south, and the submissions that make side control genuinely dangerous rather than merely uncomfortable — [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md), [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md), [americana](../submissions/americana.md), [darce](../submissions/darce.md), paper cutter, and the far-side [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md).

**The thing to internalise:** side control is not one position. It is a hub. A good top player will circulate between side control, knee-on-belly, north–south and mount, and each transition is a moment where one of their control points is temporarily off. **You escape on the transitions, not on the pin.**

## 2. The rules of survival here

**Frames — the two-frame sandwich.** Your near forearm goes across their neck or collarbone (never their face — it is bad manners and it fails anyway), and your near elbow drops inside their near hip with the forearm on the hip bone. Those two frames make a wedge that holds their chest and their hips apart. The critical detail is angle: **a frame at 90 degrees to the mat holds; a frame at 45 degrees collapses.** Your elbow should be more or less directly under your own shoulder, so the load runs down your skeleton into the floor instead of into your triceps.

**Get on your side.** Flat is dead. In bottom side control, lying square with both shoulder blades on the mat means their crossface works, their weight is maximal, and mount is one knee-slide away. Both of your knees should be pointed at them, your bottom shoulder should have a gap under it, and you should be looking at their chest. The whole position changes character the moment you turn.

**The elbow–knee connection.** Same win condition as under mount: get your **near elbow to your near knee**. When they touch, a shin can come in front of their hip, and a shin in front of the hip is half guard. Half guard is a legitimate destination and a huge upgrade — do not hold out for closed guard while a good knee shield is available.

**Breathing under pressure.** Their weight is across your chest at an angle, which usually means one lung is more compressed than the other. Turn your head *toward* them, shrug your near shoulder toward your ear, and breathe into your far side. Nasal, shallow, and **exhale as they settle** — take your air in the half-second after they adjust. The atlas is direct about this: panic is mostly oxygen debt, and fixing your breathing under a heavy top player beats a year of conditioning.

**Never, in this position:**

- **Never lie flat with your arms straight, pushing on their shoulders.** You are making yourself heavier, exhausting your arms, and offering both the kimura and the arm triangle in a single gesture.
- **Never let your near arm end up trapped under their body with your head.** That is the arm triangle setup and the reason so many people get choked from a position they thought was only uncomfortable. Near elbow lives against your own ribs.
- **Never reach across their back for a far underhook while you are flat.** From flat, that arm crosses your own throat — it is the [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md) and the far-side armbar. Get on your side first, then reach.
- **Never turn belly-down without committing to turtle properly.** A half-turn face-down is a back take. If you go, go all the way to knees and elbows (see [turtle escapes](./turtle-escapes.md)).
- **Never let them settle a crossface without responding.** Every second you leave the crossface unaddressed makes every escape on this page harder.
- **Never freeze when they go to knee-on-belly.** It is the best escape window in the position, not a worse position.

## 3. The escapes

### Frame and shrimp to guard

**Diff 2/5 · Com 5/5** ([`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) VII.a: *"the universal side-control escape"*)

1. **Set both frames.** Near forearm blade across the front of their shoulder/collarbone; near elbow inside their near hip, forearm on the hip bone. Elbows bent.
2. **Turn onto your near side**, both knees toward them, near shoulder off the mat. You are now in a position where your hips can actually move.
3. **Small bridge into them** — enough to lift their weight off your near hip and unweight the crossface for a moment. Not a big bridge; a hip lift.
4. As you come down, **shrimp your hips away**. The frames hold their body still while *you* move; you are not pushing them anywhere.
5. **Thread the near knee into the gap** the instant it appears, and turn the shin across their near hip. The shin now holds the space with bone.
6. Build a knee shield, get your far foot to their hip if you can, and recover from there.

**The two details people get wrong:** *you shrimp away from the frames, you do not push them away with the frames* — the frame is a strut, not a piston. And *the escape ends at half guard*; if you have a good knee shield, take it and stop negotiating.

### Underhook to dogfight

**Diff 3/5 · Com 4/5**

Trades a bad, static position for a scramble — and scrambles are where matches are decided (atlas Part X).

1. Get on your side, near elbow tight to your ribs, and pick your moment: they adjust, they reach for a grip, or they lift to move.
2. **Thread your bottom arm — the one nearest your own hips — under their far armpit**, palm up, hand climbing high onto their shoulder blade or lat. Shallow underhooks get whizzered; deep ones don't.
3. **Get your head up off the mat**, ear glued to their chest or shoulder. This is the step that makes or breaks it. **An underhook with your head on the mat is not an escape, it is an [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md) donation.**
4. Bridge into them to unweight, then post your top elbow and come up onto it.
5. Post your near hand, get a knee under you, and drive into them. You are now in the **dogfight** (atlas: Diff 3, Com 3).
6. From the dogfight: come up and take the sweep if their base is behind them, take the back if they whizzer hard and turn away, or sit back into a knee-shield half guard if the scramble goes wrong.

### Bridge-and-roll (ghost escape)

**Diff 3/5 · Com 4/5**

Two escapes that the atlas groups as one entry, because they are the same bridge with two different endings.

1. **Trap something.** Either overhook and pin their crossfacing arm to your body, or pin their near arm against your own ribs. A bridge with nothing trapped just repositions them.
2. Heels close to your bum, both feet flat.
3. **Bridge into them over your near shoulder**, driving your hips up and toward their head — not straight up, and never away from them.
4. **The bridge is not meant to roll them.** Its job is to force a post and, in doing so, lift their hips off yours.
5. **Roll finish:** if their arm is trapped and they cannot post, keep turning and come up on top.
6. **Ghost finish (the more common one):** as they post to stop the roll, snap your hips back out along the mat and slip your head and shoulders out the "back door", spinning to face them. Come up on top, or take the back if they have turned away from you.

### Granby roll

**Diff 3/5 · Com 3/5**

1. **Prerequisite check: your neck must be clear.** No crossface, no arm under your head, and your near frame in. If they have a crossface, this escape breaks rather than works.
2. On your side, tuck your chin hard and load your near shoulder.
3. **Roll over the shoulder — never over the head or the neck.**
4. Kick your top leg over as you invert, spinning your hips underneath them.
5. Come out facing them with your legs between you: seated guard, open guard, or out the back door to their hip.

If you cannot do a controlled shoulder roll in the warm-up on your own, do not do this one in a live round. The atlas rates inversion as a neck-loading movement to build up slowly.

### Dealing with the crossface

Not an escape — the sub-skill without which none of the escapes work.

1. **Turn your chin *into* them, not away.** Hide your face against their chest or shoulder. A head turned away is a head they can steer; a head buried into them has nowhere left to be pushed.
2. **Shrug your near shoulder up toward your ear.** This shortens the target and takes the crossface off your jaw and onto your deltoid, where it does nothing.
3. **Get your near hand inside**, thumb up, onto their collarbone or the front of their shoulder — a short frame with a bent elbow, not a push.
4. **In the gi:** grip their far lapel with your near hand and pull it down across their body. It kills the crossface arm's ability to reset and gives you a handle to shrimp against.
5. **In no-gi:** frame at the *elbow crease* of their crossfacing arm rather than at their shoulder. The elbow is the joint that has to bend for the crossface to work.

**The rule: you cannot shrimp away from a crossface you have not cleared.** Order of operations is always head, then hips, then legs. People who "can't shrimp" in side control almost always have a head problem, not a hip problem.

### Dealing with knee-on-belly transitions

1. **Recognise it as an opportunity.** When they come up to knee-on-belly, their weight goes from spread across your whole torso onto one point, and their base goes from low and wide to high and narrow. That is the best moment in the entire position.
2. **Get both hands on the knee immediately** — one on the knee or shin, one on their far hip or posted foot. Never one hand; one hand is an [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md).
3. **Turn your near hip up toward them** so the knee slides off your soft belly onto your hip bone.
4. **Shrimp away and re-insert the knee** before they settle their posture.
5. If they are travelling from side control *toward* mount rather than stopping at knee-on-belly, catch the knee with your far elbow as it crosses your belt line — that is the window, and it is roughly one second wide.

Full detail lives in [knee-on-belly escapes](./knee-on-belly-escapes.md). The one-line version: **never freeze under knee-on-belly.** Freezing is what turns a two-point position into an armbar.

## 4. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** Bottom side control against a 60 kg opponent and a 110 kg opponent are different problems, not the same problem with a different amount of effort. The single sentence that captures the whole section:

> **Against a much heavier opponent you escape with patience. Against a much lighter one you escape with commitment. They are not the same skill, and practising one does not give you the other.**

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Much heavier than you** | Frames collapse under load, "bridge into them" produces no lift, and the come-up escapes require you to move a mass you cannot move | Frames at 90 degrees loaded through bone; get on your side and *stay* there; convert every escape into a hip-movement escape; the bridge only makes them post |
| **Much lighter than you** | Their side control isn't heavy, so space appears easily — but they simply travel to north–south, knee-on-belly or the far side and you have gained nothing | Trap a limb before you move, escape on one committed line rather than in increments, and expect the submission before the pass |
| **Very tall / long limbs** | They lock a crossface and a far underhook simultaneously without moving, and their long posted legs leave no edge to roll them over | Attack the crossfacing *elbow* rather than their far hand; get the shin in early, because their chest is far from their base; the granby works unusually well |
| **Short and stocky, low centre of gravity** | Their whole mass sits on your sternum through a very short lever, their hips are already low, and there is nothing to bridge | Abandon vertical entirely; walk your hips out horizontally in small increments; their shallow crossface is the weak link — clear it first |
| **Very strong grippers** | A lapel-and-belt grip pins your shoulders and hips independently; you cannot shrimp against a pinned belt | Break the *lower* grip first — the hip grip stops the shrimp, the collar grip only stops the bridge. Bridge to create slack, then peel |
| **You are the bigger one** (size gap reversed) | Their pin is light but mobile and technical; strength escapes work and teach you nothing; and they will take your back the instant you turn | Set frames earlier because you have more body to move; underhook-to-dogfight is your best route; keep the far shoulder down |

### Much heavier than you

**Why the standard version fails.** Every step of the textbook escape assumes you can create a gap. Against 30 kg more than you, gaps do not get created — they get *found*. Their crossface has more mass behind it, their chest flattens your frames rather than resting on them, and "bridge into them" produces about two centimetres of nothing.

Six concrete changes:

1. **Fix the frame angle before anything else.** This is the highest-leverage detail on the page. A frame collapses when the load has a sideways component; it holds when the load runs straight down the bone into the floor. **Your near elbow should be directly beneath your near shoulder, forearm vertical-ish, wrist stacked over elbow.** People frame with their elbow flared out to the side, which turns their triceps into the only thing holding a heavy human off their chest. It will not last thirty seconds. Fix the angle and the same frame holds indefinitely.
2. **Stop trying to make space. Start taking space.** You will never push a much heavier opponent away. What you *can* do is move yourself along the mat — small, repeated hip escapes of two or three centimetres, each one immediately locked in with the shin. The heavier they are, the slower they are to chase your hips, and that slowness is the entire opening you get.
3. **Get on your side and never come off it.** Flat-on-your-back side control under a much heavier opponent is where the round goes to die. If you are on your side with a frame in, you can hold that shape for minutes with almost no energy. If you are flat, you are one knee-slide from mount and you have no escapes at all. **If you can only do one thing in the position, do this one.**
4. **Change what the bridge is for.** Do not bridge to lift them — bridge to make them *post*, exactly as with the [mount upa](./mount-escapes.md). A heavy opponent who posts a hand to stop your bridge has just narrowed their base and lifted their hips off yours for half a second. Take the shrimp in that half-second. The bridge is a question, not an answer.
5. **Bridge as a rotation, not a lift.** If you must bridge, do not drive straight into their chest — you will simply wedge yourself under them. Bridge *and simultaneously turn your near hip out*, so the movement is a corkscrew. That converts a lifting problem, which you lose, into a rotating problem, which you can sometimes win.
6. **Re-order your escapes.** Against a much heavier opponent, the **underhook-to-dogfight drops down the list** — coming up to your elbow requires you to lift their weight off your shoulder, and you cannot. **Frame-and-shrimp to half guard moves to the top.** Get a shin across their hip; a shin frame is stronger than any arm frame you own and will hold weight your arms cannot.

**Timing.** Escape when their weight is somewhere else. When they load toward your head to crossface harder, your hips are light. When they load toward your hips to block, your head is free. When they step over toward mount or circle toward north–south, everything is light for about one second. **Do not attempt an escape while a heavy opponent is settled and balanced — you are just donating energy.**

**Say something.** Bottom side control under a much heavier partner is one of the two most common rib-injury positions in training. If you cannot breathe, say so out loud. That is a skill, not a concession — see §8.

### Much lighter than you

- **The trap is that space is easy to find and worthless.** You will shrimp, create a gap, feel good about it — and they will simply be at north–south or on the other side before your knee arrives. Against a lighter opponent, incremental escaping *feeds* them transitions, because every gap you make is a gap they can travel through.
- **Trap before you move.** Overhook their crossfacing arm, pin their near arm to your ribs, or get a grip on their far hip. Something must be attached to you, or your movement just gives them a free repositioning.
- **Escape on one committed line.** The **underhook-to-dogfight** and the **bridge-and-roll** become your primaries because they force a decision — they cannot travel and defend a come-up at the same time. Pick one, commit fully, and accept the scramble.
- **The danger is the neck, not the pass.** A lighter opponent in side control is usually there to attack: [darce](../submissions/darce.md), [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md), [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md), and the far-side armbar all arrive earlier than they would against a heavier player who is content to be heavy. Keep your near elbow to your ribs and your head off the mat.
- **They cannot hold you, so they will hold your grips instead.** Break the grip and the pin is over. Grip fighting is the escape.

### Very tall opponents with long limbs

- **They can lock you down without moving.** Long arms mean the crossface reaches past your head *and* the far arm reaches under your far armpit at the same time, so they get a full chest-lock with no repositioning. Against a shorter opponent one of those two is always available; against a tall one, neither is.
- **Attack the elbow, not the hand.** A long arm's power is nowhere near its hand. Put your frame in at their **crossfacing elbow crease** and walk it up. Trying to reach their shoulder or their head means framing at full extension, which is the weakest place your arm can be.
- **Get the shin in early, even at an ugly angle.** A tall opponent's chest sits far from their base, which means their chest-to-hip connection is long and easy to split. A shin across their hip separates the two halves of a tall opponent much more effectively than it does a compact one.
- **The granby is unusually good here.** Their base is high and long, which leaves room to slip underneath. This is one of the few builds where inverting is a percentage play rather than a gamble.
- **The bridge-and-roll is not.** Long legs post wide; there is no edge of their base to tip them over. Do not spend energy there.

### Short, stocky opponents with a low centre of gravity

The build that makes people believe pressure is magic. It isn't — it's geometry.

- **Bridging is nearly pointless and you should stop.** A bridge is a rotation, and rotation needs height. A short, thick opponent's mass sits so low and so close to your midline that there is no lever to rotate them over. Every bridge you throw is a rep of cardio for you and a rest for them.
- **Work horizontally instead.** Tiny repeated hip escapes along the mat, each one held by the shin frame. You are not creating space; you are *relocating yourself*, two centimetres at a time. Against this build that is the whole escape, and it works — it just does not look like anything.
- **Their crossface is the weak link.** Short arms mean a shallow crossface — it reaches your jaw but not past it. Turning your chin into them and shrugging clears it far more easily than it would against a tall opponent, and once the crossface is gone, the hip walk is available. **Clear the head first, always, but against this build especially.**
- **Take the back door, not the top.** Their short legs mean their base is narrower front-to-back than a tall opponent's. The **ghost escape — slipping your hips out backwards toward their legs** — is the highest-percentage escape against a short, stocky opponent, precisely because it does not require lifting anything.
- **Get the underhook lower.** With short arms, their armpit sits lower relative to your body than you're used to. Aim your underhook at their ribs, not their shoulder.

### Very strong grippers

- **Break the lower grip first.** This is the non-obvious priority. A belt, pant or hip grip is what kills the shrimp; a collar or lapel grip only kills the bridge. If you can only break one, break the one nearer your hips — the shrimp is the escape you actually need.
- **Create slack before you peel.** A grip is hardest to break when the cloth is taut. **Bridge or move toward the grip to make it slack, then strip it in that instant.** Trying to peel a taut, strong grip by pulling is a losing physical contest that also costs you your fingers over the years.
- **Two hands on one, thumb side.** Always. And generate the break with a body rotation, not an arm pull.
- **No-gi:** their strong grip is a wrist tie, a head-and-arm clasp, or an over-under body lock. Attack the *connection* — the clasped hands — not the individual hand on you. A gable grip breaks at the seam, not at the wrist.
- **Expect the follow-up.** A strong gripper who loses the pin will keep the grip through the transition, which usually means you escape directly into a guillotine or a front headlock. Come out with your head up and your posture set (see [guillotine](../submissions/guillotine.md)).

### When you are the bigger one (the size gap reversed)

- **Their side control will feel like nothing, and it is not nothing.** A lighter, technical top player will hold you with a crossface and an angle rather than with weight, and will be hunting submissions rather than the pass. Treat every light side control as a submission position.
- **You have more body to move, so start earlier.** A bigger frame is genuinely slower to shrimp — more mass, longer levers, more distance for your knee to travel. Your frames need to be set *before* they consolidate, not after, because your recovery is slower than a smaller person's.
- **The underhook-to-dogfight is your best escape.** It is the one where a strength and size advantage converts cleanly into a real come-up, and unlike the frame-and-shrimp it does not depend on out-moving someone quicker than you.
- **Keep the far shoulder down.** A smaller opponent will abandon side control for your back at the first sign of your shoulders turning, because your back is worth far more to them than the pin. Turn to your side, not past it.
- **Escape without strength on purpose.** If you cannot escape a much lighter opponent using only frames, angle and hip movement, you do not have the escape — you have a size advantage that expires the first time you meet someone your own size who trains.
- **Safety.** Do not bridge a much lighter partner into the air, and do not come up onto your knees driving your shoulder through their ribcage. You will hurt people without noticing, and they will stop wanting to train with you.

## 5. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your frames run out of length in a supine position. Get on your side sooner (a side-lying frame is a shorter reach), keep the elbow bent, and convert to a **shin frame** at the first opportunity. Your shin is longer than your forearm and it never gets tired.
- **Long legs.** Knee insertion and the granby are both easier for you. You can often reach a foot to their far hip from further away than most people, which makes the frame-and-shrimp reliable even against good pressure.
- **Long torso.** More distance for your near elbow to travel to your near knee, so the elbow–knee connection needs a bigger hip escape. Compensate by turning further onto your side rather than shrimping further.
- **Thick torso or limited hip mobility.** Skip the granby entirely. Live on the frame-and-shrimp and the underhook, and be strict about the frame angle, since you have less range to recover with when a frame collapses.
- **Older or less explosive.** The bridge-and-roll and the dogfight come-up both degrade with age. Rebuild around the frame-and-shrimp, the shin frame and knee-shield half guard, and put your effort into *not getting passed* — guard retention is the older grappler's real side-control escape.
- **Previous rib or shoulder injury.** Bottom side control loads exactly those structures. Tell your partner before the round (atlas, Tier 0), avoid the underhook come-up on a bad shoulder, and use position, not toughness, to protect the ribs.

## 6. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "My frames collapse instantly" | The elbow is flared out, so your triceps are holding the load instead of your skeleton | Elbow directly under your shoulder, forearm near-vertical, load through bone into the mat |
| "I can't move my hips at all" | It's a head problem, not a hip problem — the crossface is still on | Chin into them, shrug the near shoulder, clear the crossface *first*, then move |
| "I shrimp and they just re-close the gap" | You are shrimping into empty space with nothing to hold it | The shin goes in with the hip escape, not after it. Space you don't fill is space you gave back |
| "I get the underhook and get arm-triangled" | Your head stayed on the mat while your arm went across | Head up and glued to their chest *before* the underhook goes deep |
| "The granby just gets me flattened worse" | You inverted with a crossface on, or with their arm under your head | Granby needs a clear neck. If they have your head, do a different escape |
| "I bridge and nothing happens" | Nothing is trapped, or you're bridging away from them | Trap an arm first, bridge *into* them and over your near shoulder |
| "I escape and end up in a guillotine" | You came out with your head down and their grip still attached | Head up, posture on the way out. Coming out of side control is a posture problem |
| "They keep going to knee-on-belly and I lose ground" | You're treating the transition as a threat rather than as the window | Two hands on the knee, hip up, shrimp and re-insert — see the KOB page |
| "I'm exhausted after ninety seconds" | You're pushing rather than framing, and holding your breath | Bones not muscles; nasal breathing; exhale as they settle |
| "It works in drilling and never live" | You're escaping against a settled, balanced pin | Escape on their transitions — the pin is the one moment you should *not* pick |

## 7. Drills

**1. Positional sparring from side control.** Start with the top player settled, crossface in. Bottom wins by getting a **shin in front of their hip** — not by reaching guard. Top wins by holding or advancing. **90-second rounds, reset, switch.** Per the atlas, positional rounds produce roughly three times the reps of the relevant situation compared with open rolling.

**2. Frame-integrity drill (no escaping allowed).** Top player applies as much pressure as they can and tries to flatten you. Your only job is to keep one frame at a working angle and stay on your side for **60 seconds**. No escapes permitted. Two rounds each. This is the drill that fixes bottom side control against heavy people, and almost nobody does it.

**3. Crossface clearing, isolated.** Partner does nothing but crossface and re-crossface. You do nothing but clear it — chin in, shrug, hand inside, lapel grip. **Two minutes each.** Then do the frame-and-shrimp and notice how much easier it has become.

**4. The transition-hunt round.** Top player must move continuously between side control, knee-on-belly and north–south, changing every five seconds. Bottom player may only attempt an escape *during* a transition. **Three minutes each.** This trains the single most transferable habit on the page.

**5. The weight-differential pair.** Run three minutes of bottom side control against the heaviest person in the room and three against the lightest, back to back, then write three lines on what changed. Patience against one, commitment against the other — you should be able to feel the difference by the end of the second round.

## 8. Safety

**Ribs.** Bottom side control under a much heavier partner is one of the most common places to injure ribs in training, and rib injuries measure in months. **Verbalise it.** "Too heavy" or "can't breathe" mid-round is a legitimate, adult thing to say, and the same skill family as tapping — the atlas is explicit that the tap is a technique, not a failure.

**Neck.** The granby and any inverted escape load the cervical spine. Roll over the **shoulder**, never the head, and build inversion capacity slowly in warm-ups before you use it live.

**Shoulders.** The underhook come-up puts your shoulder in a loaded, externally rotated position under someone's weight. If you have a history of dislocations or labrum problems, use the frame-and-shrimp instead — this one is not worth a re-injury.

**Submissions to respect.** The [arm triangle](../submissions/arm-triangle.md) is the specific one to fear here: it arrives out of your own escape attempt, and it gives very little warning between "annoying" and "out". Tap early. The [kimura](../submissions/kimura.md) and the far-side [armbar](../submissions/armbar.md) both punish a straightened arm — and the atlas is blunt that once a kimura is bent behind your back, "you're mostly done", so fight the grip early.

**Legality.** Nothing on this page is restricted at any belt or in any ruleset.
