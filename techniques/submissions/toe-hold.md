# Toe Hold

> A figure-four grip on the foot, rotated inward — an americana for the ankle. It attacks the ankle first and the knee second, which is exactly why a flexible opponent may feel almost nothing until it is too late.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Leg lock — rotational ankle attack that loads the knee |
| **Comes from** | Saddle / inside sankaku, outside ashi garami, 50/50, single-leg X, top half guard and passing positions |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | IBJJF: brown and black belts only, gi and no-gi. Illegal at white, blue and purple. |

**Also known as:** ashi dori garami, americana for the foot, figure-four foot lock

*Ratings match [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).*

---

## 1. The mechanism

Your two hands form a figure-four around their foot: one hand on top of the foot
near the base of the toes, the other arm threaded under the ankle and gripping
your own wrist. That gives you a steering wheel with a long handle. Rotating the
foot inward — toes turning toward their own midline — drives the ankle into a
combination of inversion and plantarflexion, well past the range the joint
normally travels.

**Two structures, in sequence.** First the ankle: the lateral ligament complex on
the outside of the ankle and the tissue around the joint take the load, and on
most people that is where the tap comes from. But an ankle only has so much
rotation available. Once it runs out, the rotation does not stop — it travels up
the tibia. And a rotating tibia with a pinned femur is a knee attack, exactly as
described on the [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) page.

**That sequence is the whole safety story of this submission.** On an average
ankle, the joint complains early and loudly, and the toe hold is a comparatively
civilised lock. On a hypermobile ankle, the first stage produces almost no
signal at all — the joint absorbs 30 or 40 degrees of rotation quietly — and then
the load arrives at the knee suddenly and without warning. Which is why the
phrase you will hear from experienced leg lockers, *"they didn't tap to the toe
hold,"* is a description of a knee injury and not of a tough opponent.

**Direction matters.** The atlas describes the toe hold as twisting the foot
inward, and that is the version to learn: rotation toward their midline. IBJJF
require the inward direction, and an outward rotation is treated as an illegal
knee attack rather than a foot lock. Check the current ruleset for your event —
this is one of the details that varies between organisations.

## 2. The standard application

From the saddle (inside sankaku), which is the most controlling home for this
submission. The mechanics are the same from outside ashi and 50/50, with less
control over the femur.

1. **Establish the entanglement and the femur.** Their leg across your hips, your
   legs figure-foured, their thigh pinned. **The toe hold's torque escapes at the
   hip more easily than any other leg lock's — if their femur can rotate, you are
   twisting their whole leg rather than their ankle.**
2. **Bring the heel to your chest.** Do not attack a straight leg at range. Pull
   their heel in toward your own chest or hip so the leg is short and their knee
   is bent — this both stops them ripping the foot free and keeps the eventual
   rotation local.
3. **Take the top hand.** The hand nearest their toes goes over the top of their
   foot, palm across the instep near the base of the toes, fingers wrapping the
   little-toe edge. **Grip near the toes, not mid-foot — the further out you are,
   the longer the handle and the less force you need.**
4. **Thread the bottom arm.** Your other forearm goes under their ankle, right in
   the narrow spot immediately above the heel, and that hand grips your own
   wrist. Close the figure-four tight so there is no slack in the frame.
5. **Take the slack out of the joint.** Before you rotate, plantarflex their foot
   slightly — press the toes away — so the ankle is already at the top of its
   range. Now the rotation you add goes into the joint rather than into the
   available slack.
6. **Rotate like a steering wheel, slowly.** The top hand drives the toes inward
   and down toward their opposite hip; the bottom forearm lifts the ankle. Both
   at once, over three or four seconds. **Do not crank with the top hand alone —
   that is fast, uncontrolled and it is how partners get hurt.**
7. **Release on the tap, by opening the figure-four**, and lower the leg rather
   than letting it drop.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The toe hold's problem is that you are trying to build a rigid mechanical frame
out of two hands around an object — the human foot — that varies enormously in
size, shape and mobility. Almost every adjustment below is about restoring the
frame, and the first one is about knowing when not to.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypermobile ankles** | The joint eats 30–40° of rotation silently; they may never tap, and then the load hits the knee all at once | Take the slack out first (plantarflex, then rotate), clamp the heel so the foot cannot slide in your grip — and if there is still no reaction, stop and change locks. Do not out-rotate a flexible ankle |
| **Very stiff ankles** | End range arrives almost immediately, with no room for error | Rotate at half speed; expect the tap in the first few degrees |
| **Big feet** | You cannot close the figure-four — your bottom hand will not reach your own wrist | Grip your own forearm or a fistful of your own sleeve instead of the wrist; move the bottom arm's contact right into the ankle crease, the narrowest point on the leg |
| **Small feet** | The foot rotates inside your top hand — the grip slides off the toes | Cup the ball of the foot with your palm and hook your fingers hard around the little-toe edge; wedge the foot against your shoulder or chest to add a second contact |
| **Thick calves** | Your bottom arm cannot get under the shin — the calf belly is in the way | Thread under the ankle joint itself, immediately above the heel, where the leg is at its narrowest; do not try to burrow through the calf |
| **Thick thighs** | Your knee pinch will not close, the femur rotates, and the torque never reaches the ankle | Figure-four your own legs around the thigh rather than pinching; or take the saddle, where the femur is trapped across your hips |
| **Long legs** | The knee stays far away and the hip absorbs the rotation | Pull the heel to your chest first so the leg folds; get your inside knee up into their hip crease |
| **Much heavier than you** | They straighten the leg and rip it out of a two-hand grip you cannot maintain | Fold the leg and pin the heel before gripping; if they straighten and stand, release rather than hold on |
| **Much lighter than you** | They spin out easily — and their ankle reaches end range far sooner | Control the femur first, then apply at a third of your normal speed |

### Hypermobile ankles — the build this submission is genuinely dangerous against

If you read one subsection on this page, read this one.

A hypermobile ankle can absorb a large amount of inversion and plantarflexion
without pain. You apply what feels like a firm toe hold, get no reaction, apply
more, still get no reaction — and the whole time, the ankle is quietly running
out of range. When it does run out, the rotation transfers to the tibia, and at
that point you are applying a rotational knee attack with a two-handed lever and
no idea how close you are to the end. **The atlas's warning about leg locks
applies here in its strongest form: there is no reliable warning, so the tap
cannot be your only stopping signal.**

What to do mechanically:

1. **Remove the slack before you rotate.** With the figure-four closed, press the
   toes away from you (plantarflexion) until you feel the joint firm up, *then*
   begin the inward rotation. This means the rotation starts at the joint's end
   range rather than spending itself getting there — you get feedback from the
   first degree instead of the fortieth.
2. **Clamp the heel so the foot cannot travel.** A surprising amount of what
   looks like ankle flexibility is actually the whole foot sliding inside a loose
   grip. Pull their heel into your armpit or hard against your chest and hold it
   there with your body, so the calcaneus is fixed. Now every degree of rotation
   you apply happens at the joint.
3. **Shorten the handle deliberately.** This is counter-intuitive — a longer
   handle is more powerful — but power is not your problem here, feedback is.
   Moving your top hand slightly back from the toes toward the mid-foot costs you
   leverage and buys you feel, and against this build feel is what keeps their
   knee attached.
4. **Read resistance, not reaction.** Apply until you feel the joint stop giving,
   then stop, whether or not they have made a sound.
5. **Then change locks.** If you have taken out the slack, fixed the heel and
   rotated to the end of the ankle's range and they are still comfortable, the
   toe hold is not there. Do not go looking for the knee with it. Where your belt
   and ruleset permit, the [kneebar](./kneebar.md) or the
   [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) attacks the knee deliberately, from a position
   built to control it — which is a completely different proposition from
   arriving at the knee by accident at the end of a foot lock.

### Very stiff ankles

Heavy lifters, ex-runners, and anyone with old ankle injuries frequently have
very little rotation available. On them the toe hold arrives in a few degrees.
Everything about the setup stays the same; the only change is speed. Set the
figure-four, take the slack out, and then move at half your normal rate,
expecting the tap almost immediately. If you apply a toe hold to a stiff ankle at
the speed that works on a flexible one, you will hurt someone.

### Big feet

The mechanical failure here is specific: you cannot close the figure-four,
because your bottom hand cannot reach your own wrist around a large foot and a
thick ankle. An open figure-four is not a frame — it is two hands pulling in
different directions, and it will slip under load.

- **Change what the bottom hand grips.** Take your own forearm rather than your
  wrist, or in the gi take a fistful of your own sleeve. Both close the loop.
  A closed loop on the forearm is far stronger than an "almost" grip on the
  wrist.
- **Move the bottom arm to the narrowest point.** Thread it into the crease right
  above the heel rather than across the mid-foot. The circumference there is the
  smallest on the whole lower leg, and it is often the two inches you need.
- **Use the length.** Once the frame is closed, a large foot is a long handle and
  the lock produces a lot of rotation for very little effort. Rotate less than
  you think, and rotate slowly.

### Small feet

The opposite failure: there is not enough foot for your top hand to hold, so the
foot rotates inside your grip and your rotation disappears.

- **Change the top grip from a flat palm to a cup and hook.** Put the heel of
  your palm on the ball of their foot and hook your fingers firmly around the
  little-toe edge, so you are gripping a shape rather than resting on a surface.
- **Add a second contact.** Wedge the outside of their foot against your shoulder
  or your chest so that the frame is hand-plus-body rather than hand-alone.
- If it still slips, a small foot is a very good target for the
  [straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) instead — the tendon does not
  care how small the foot is.

### Thick calves

Your bottom arm has to get under the leg, and a big calf belly makes that
impossible mid-shin. People respond by burrowing, which just pushes the arm up
the leg and destroys the angle.

Do not burrow. Thread the bottom arm into the narrow crease immediately above the
heel — the "bracelet" spot where even a very large lower leg is comparatively
slim. From there the figure-four closes and the rotation still works. It is worth
being deliberate about this on entry, because trying to correct arm placement
after the grip is closed generally means losing the foot.

### Thick thighs

Same problem as every other leg lock: your knees will not close around the thigh,
the femur rotates, and your carefully built ankle lock becomes a leg twist that
moves their whole body instead of their joint.

- Replace the pinch with a wrap: figure-four your own legs around the thigh so
  the clamp is a closed loop rather than a squeeze.
- Better, take the saddle. With their thigh across your hips and your legs
  figure-foured, the femur is trapped structurally and the toe hold has something
  to work against.
- Get your inside knee high, into the hip crease, so you are controlling near the
  pelvis rather than at the widest part of the leg.

### Long legs

A long leg lets the rotation escape at the hip, and it also keeps their knee far
enough from your body that you have no secondary control over it.

The single most useful change is to **fold the leg before you grip**: pull the
heel to your chest so the knee bends and their shin comes close to their own
thigh. A folded leg cannot dissipate rotation through the hip nearly as well as a
straight one, and it also removes their ability to straighten and rip the foot
out. Then drive your inside knee up into their hip crease so you are controlling
the femur near the pelvis.

### Much heavier than you

Two hands around a foot is not much of a grip against a large, strong person who
decides to straighten their leg and stand up. You will not win that contest.

- **Fold the leg and pin the heel before you grip anything.** If the heel is
  pinned to your chest and their knee is bent, they cannot generate the straight
  line they need to rip it free.
- **Do not hold on while they rip.** This is the same warning as on the heel hook
  page and it applies for the same reason — the injury in that scenario happens
  during their escape, at their speed, with no control from you. Open your hands.
- Prefer the saddle over outside ashi against much heavier opponents, because the
  saddle traps the femur with your whole body rather than with your legs alone.

### Much lighter than you

They will spin out of a loose entanglement almost effortlessly, so all the
control work has to be done first: femur, then heel, then grip, then rotate.

More importantly, everything about a lighter opponent's ankle is smaller — less
tissue, shorter ligaments, less tolerance. The rotation that produces a firm
"that's tight" on a 100 kg training partner can be an injury on a 55 kg one.
Apply at a third of your normal speed, stop at the first sign of resistance, and
do not chase a tap.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

- **Small hands.** The figure-four is a reach problem for you on anything but a
  small foot. Default to gripping your own forearm rather than your wrist, and
  thread the bottom arm at the ankle crease rather than the mid-foot.
- **Large hands.** You can hold the foot itself rather than relying purely on the
  frame, which is a real advantage — but it tempts you into cranking with the top
  hand. Keep the rotation coming from both arms turning together.
- **Short arms.** Bring the leg to you rather than reaching to it. Fold the heel
  into your chest as the first action of every entry.
- **Weak grip or sore fingers** (common, and the atlas notes that BJJ finger
  arthritis is near-universal). A correctly framed toe hold needs very little
  finger strength — the frame does the work. If your fingers hurt after toe
  holds, your figure-four is open and you are compensating with grip.
- **Lighter than most of your partners.** Prioritise the saddle. From outside
  ashi your control is a pinch, and against bigger opponents a pinch is not
  control.
- **Stiff hips.** If the saddle is not comfortable for you, 50/50 gives you a
  reasonable toe hold platform with less demand on hip rotation, at the cost of
  the femur control.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You rotate and their whole leg turns | The femur is free — the torque is escaping at the hip | Figure-four your legs around the thigh, or move to the saddle; fold the heel to your chest |
| The grip pops open under load | Your figure-four never closed — usually a big foot | Grip your own forearm instead of your wrist; thread the bottom arm at the ankle crease |
| The foot rotates inside your hands | Small foot, or the top hand is flat on the instep | Cup the ball of the foot and hook the little-toe edge; wedge the foot against your shoulder |
| You cannot get your bottom arm under the leg | You are trying to thread across a thick calf | Thread immediately above the heel, the narrowest point |
| Lots of rotation, no reaction at all | Hypermobile ankle — the joint is absorbing it, and the knee is next | Take out the slack first, clamp the heel, read resistance not reaction; if still nothing, change locks |
| They straighten the leg and drag you along | You attacked at range instead of folding the leg | Fold the leg and pin the heel before gripping |
| They tapped instantly and complained afterwards | Stiff ankle, or you rotated with the top hand alone | Both arms, together, over three to four seconds |
| Referee penalises you mid-match | Wrong belt, or you rotated outward | Brown/black only in IBJJF, and the rotation must be inward. Know the ruleset before the match |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They point the toes hard and pull the foot straight | [Straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) — the toes-pointed position is what it wants (legal at every belt) |
| They rotate their whole leg to follow the twist and expose the heel | [Heel hook](./heel-hook.md) (adult brown/black, no-gi only) |
| They straighten the leg violently to rip it out | [Kneebar](./kneebar.md) — the straightened leg is the target (brown/black) |
| They fold the knee hard and drive it to the mat | [Calf slicer](./calf-slicer.md) from the folded position (brown/black) |
| They sit up and hand-fight the figure-four | Release and sweep — single-leg X or the saddle sweep; take the position instead |
| They cross the free leg over to pry you open | Catch it and take 50/50 or step through to the other saddle |
| Nothing is available and you are stalling | Let the leg go and pass or sweep. The atlas's rule still applies: position before submission |

**What feeds into it:** the saddle from a back step or an imanari roll; outside
ashi from a failed pass; 50/50; a defended
[straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) where they boot the foot; and
top half guard when their bottom foot ends up exposed.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is a grip fight. Late defense is a rotation you cannot stop.**

**Early — before the figure-four closes.** The toe hold needs two of your
opponent's hands and a closed frame, which means it is the leg lock with the
longest, most visible build-up.

- **Straighten the leg and point the toes.** A straight leg with a pointed foot
  keeps the heel and instep tight against their forearm and makes the top grip
  hard to find.
- **Hide the foot behind your own body or under their armpit** — get the foot off
  the open plane where they can wrap it.
- **Hand-fight the top grip specifically.** It is the harder of their two grips to
  establish and the easier to strip. Peel the hand off the instep before the
  bottom arm threads through.
- Best of all: **get your knee to the mat and free the hip**, which kills the
  entanglement and the lock together.

**Middle — figure-four closed, rotation not yet applied.**

1. **Rotate toward the attack.** The universal leg lock principle in the atlas:
   turn your body the same direction they are turning your foot, so the rotation
   is absorbed by your hip and your body rather than your ankle and knee. On a
   toe hold this is unusually effective, because the lock has a short range.
2. **Come up on your elbow and reach for their grip**, then hip escape backwards
   along the line of your own leg.
3. **Kick the free leg over their head or across their body** to break their
   posture and open the entanglement.

**Late — the rotation has started.** Tap immediately. Two reasons, and the second
is the one people underestimate. First, the ankle can be sprained badly enough
here to keep you off the mat for a month. Second, and worse: if you are a
flexible person, **the ankle may not hurt at all**, and the next thing to receive
the rotation is your knee, without warning. If you are hypermobile, do not use
your own comfort as a gauge of how much trouble you are in — tap on position, not
on pain.

## 8. Drills

1. **Frame-closing drill.** Partner lies passive. Close the figure-four on their
   foot ten times per side, aiming for a closed loop every rep. Repeat with the
   biggest-footed and smallest-footed partners in the room and note which grip
   variation you needed for each. 3 rounds. This is the drill that stops the
   grip popping open in a live round.
2. **Slack-then-rotate.** With the frame closed, plantarflex the foot until you
   feel the ankle firm up, hold two seconds, then rotate 10° and stop. 8 reps per
   side, no finishing. The point is to learn what "the joint is now loaded" feels
   like in your hands, so you are not relying on your partner's reaction.
3. **Flexibility survey.** Take three partners of visibly different builds. Apply
   a slow toe hold to each and stop at the first sign of resistance. Ask each one
   what they felt and when. You will find the range of "when it starts to hurt"
   is far wider than you expected, and that is the whole argument for section 3.
4. **Positional sparring — saddle, no submissions.** One player starts in the
   saddle, the other must escape. Attacker scores by achieving a closed
   figure-four on the foot and stopping there. 90-second rounds. This drills the
   dangerous part safely.

## 9. Safety

- **Apply slowly.** Three to four seconds minimum, always, with both arms turning
  together. A toe hold cranked with the top hand alone is a fast rotational load
  on a small joint, and it can arrive faster than a hand can tap.
- **Take the slack out of the ankle before you rotate.** This is a safety
  technique as much as a mechanical one: it means you get feedback from the first
  degree of rotation instead of discovering the joint's limit by passing it.
- **Understand the two-stage risk.** The ankle is what you are attacking; the knee
  is what is behind it. On a flexible opponent the first stage can be silent, and
  the second stage is a rotational knee injury of exactly the kind the
  [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) page warns about. **A partner who does not tap is
  not necessarily safe.**
- **Read resistance, not reaction.** Stop when the joint stops giving, regardless
  of whether they have made a sound.
- **Tap early. Knee ligaments do not recover like muscle** — the atlas's rule, and
  it applies here because a toe hold that runs out of ankle becomes a knee attack.
- **Release by opening the figure-four and lowering the leg.** Never let the leg
  drop, and never roll away while still holding the foot.
- **Ask about ankle and knee history before the round.** Both joints are in play.

**Legality.** IBJJF permit the toe hold for **adult brown and black belts only**,
gi and no-gi; it is illegal at white, blue and purple. The rotation must be
**inward** — twisting the foot outward is treated as an illegal attack on the
knee. Many toe hold entries also pass through leg entanglements where **knee
reaping** rules apply: reaping is illegal in the gi at every belt and legal in
no-gi only for adult brown and black belts, so a legal submission from an illegal
entanglement is still a penalty. Rules change; check the current official ruleset
for your event.
