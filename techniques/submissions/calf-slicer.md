# Calf Slicer

> Your shin wedged in the back of their knee, their own leg folded over it, and the fold closed until the calf has nowhere to go. Not a joint lock — a compression, and it is beaten by muscle mass more than by technique.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Leg lock — compression (calf against your shin, plus levering of the knee) |
| **Comes from** | The truck, turtle top, back control with a leg hooked, 50/50 and the saddle, a failed kneebar, lockdown and the electric-chair family |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 2/5 |
| **Legal** | IBJJF: brown and black belts only, gi and no-gi. Illegal at white, blue and purple. |

**Also known as:** calf crush, calf cutter, leg slicer, panturrilha

*Ratings match [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md). The 2/5 for frequency is fair —
it needs positions (the truck is rated 5/5 difficulty and 1/5 frequency in the
atlas) that most people never enter.*

---

## 1. The mechanism

A slicer is not a lock on a joint. It is a lever that crushes soft tissue against
a hard edge.

Your shin — or your forearm — is the wedge. It sits in the popliteal fossa, the
hollow at the back of their knee. Their own lower leg is then folded over the
wedge, and you close the fold. The calf muscle has nowhere to go: it is being
compressed between their own femur and the sharp anterior border of your tibia.
That produces a deep, unpleasant, muscular pain that arrives well before any
structural damage — which is the honest and useful difference between a
compression lock and a rotational one.

There is a second effect, and it is the reason this submission is regulated as a
leg attack rather than treated as harmless. The wedge in the back of the knee
acts as a fulcrum that levers the joint surfaces apart while the fold loads the
whole leg. **The deeper into the knee crease your wedge sits, the less this is a
calf crush and the more it becomes a distraction force on the knee joint
itself.** That is why the calf slicer is banned below brown belt in IBJJF
alongside the kneebar and toe hold, and it is why "just squeeze harder" is the
wrong answer to a difficult body type.

Understanding those two effects — muscle compression versus knee distraction — is
what section 3 is about. On a lean leg, the compression does the work. On a
heavily muscled leg, the compression is unavailable and you have to move the
wedge toward the joint, which is a different and more serious submission.

## 2. The standard application

The figure-four version, from a leg entanglement or the truck. Same-size partner.

1. **Get the wedge in.** Thread one leg through so your shin sits across the back
   of their knee. **Aim for the crease itself, not the belly of the calf — every
   inch lower makes it a worse slicer.**
2. **Turn the shin.** Rotate your leg so the sharp front edge of your tibia is
   what points into the crease, not the flat inside surface. **This one rotation
   is worth more than any amount of squeezing** — it turns a contact patch the
   width of a hand into a line.
3. **Fold their leg over the wedge.** Their heel has to travel toward their own
   backside. Drive it there with your other leg crossed over their shin, or hug
   the foot with both arms and pull.
4. **Close the frame.** Figure-four your own legs — hook your top foot behind
   your bottom knee — so the sandwich is a closed loop rather than a squeeze you
   have to maintain.
5. **Control the hip.** They escape by rotating the femur and straightening the
   leg. Pin the thigh, or keep an arm or a hook on the hip so the femur cannot
   turn.
6. **Finish.** Straighten your bottom (wedge) leg while your top leg drives their
   foot down toward their hip, and arch your body back. Three vectors, applied
   together and slowly.
7. **Release on the tap by opening the figure-four**, not by kicking the leg away.

The arm version is identical in principle: your forearm replaces the shin as the
wedge, and both hands pull the foot toward their backside. It is weaker but it
fits into far less space — which, as section 3 explains, is exactly what you need
against some builds.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The calf slicer is the most body-type-dependent submission in this library, and
it is the only one where the correct answer against a particular build is
sometimes *"this person cannot be calf sliced; go and do something else."* The
whole technique depends on a fold closing, and some legs do not fold.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick, muscular calves** | Muscle is a shock absorber — your shin sits on the belly, the fold never closes, and the pain never arrives | Move the wedge up into the knee crease itself so the target is the joint rather than the muscle; turn the shin to its sharp edge; grip the **foot** rather than the shin for more angle per pull |
| **Skinny calves** | It bites instantly — but the wedge slides down toward the Achilles under load | Hook their foot to lock the fold, keep the wedge anchored in the crease, and apply over 3–4 seconds |
| **Big hamstrings and thick thighs** | Their own muscle stops the leg folding past roughly 45°. Some people are functionally immune | Swap the shin wedge for a forearm wedge — a much smaller diameter that fits the range they do have — or change locks entirely |
| **Long legs** | The foot has a long way to travel to reach their backside; you run out of arm before the fold closes | Drive the foot with your leg, not your arms, and grip at the foot rather than the shin for the longest lever |
| **Short legs** | The fold closes quickly and the lock arrives with almost no build-up | Set everything, then apply at half speed — the warning period you are used to is not there |
| **Very flexible knees and hips** | The leg folds fully and comfortably, and they rotate the femur to spin out | Move the wedge to the joint line and add the knee-distraction vector; control the femur before you close the fold; be willing to abandon |
| **Much heavier than you** | Their leg is heavy and strong enough to resist folding; they post and stand out of it | Fold the leg before you finish the frame, use both your legs to close it, and take the position over the finish if they start standing |
| **Much lighter than you** | Less muscle to compress, so it goes on very fast | Half speed, and stop at the first sign of resistance rather than waiting for a reaction |

### Thick, muscular calves

The defining problem of this submission, and the reason it has a reputation for
working brilliantly on some people and not at all on others.

A large gastrocnemius belly does three things to you at once. It pads your shin
so the contact pressure never gets high enough to hurt. It physically fills the
space, so their leg cannot fold as far — their own calf hits their own hamstring
early. And it deforms under load, absorbing travel that should have gone into
compression. You end up with a position that feels like a vice to you and merely
uncomfortable to them.

Four adjustments, in order:

1. **Move the wedge up into the popliteal crease itself.** On a lean leg you can
   afford to sit an inch or two down the calf. On a heavily muscled one you
   cannot — slide your shin up until it is in the hollow right behind the knee,
   against the joint. This changes the mechanism: you are no longer crushing
   muscle, you are levering the knee joint open with the fold. It works where the
   compression version does not, and it is a more serious submission, so apply it
   more slowly, not less.
2. **Turn your leg so the sharp edge of your tibia is the contact.** A quarter
   turn of your own leg takes the contact area from roughly a palm's width down
   to a line. Same principle as putting the radius edge on an Achilles in the
   [straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md): pressure is force divided by
   area, and on a padded limb the only variable you can meaningfully change is
   area.
3. **Grip the foot, not the shin.** Every inch further out along their leg that
   you apply the closing force produces more fold for the same effort. When part
   of your available range is being eaten by muscle bulk, you need every degree
   you can get.
4. **Close the frame with your legs, not your arms.** Figure-four your own legs
   around the folded limb. On a big leg your arms will not out-pull the hamstring
   and calf; your legs will.

**And the honest fifth option.** If the leg simply will not fold — if you have
the wedge in the crease, the edge turned, the foot gripped, and their heel still
sits a hand's width from their backside — the calf slicer is not there and it is
not going to be. Muscle mass protects against compression far better than it
protects against rotation or extension. Take the [toe hold](./toe-hold.md), the
[kneebar](./kneebar.md) or the [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) instead, all of which
are indifferent to how big the calf is.

### Skinny calves

Everything is easy, and that produces two errors.

The first is technical: with nothing filling the space, your shin slides *down*
their leg under load — out of the crease, down the calf, and eventually onto the
Achilles, where it does nothing. Anchor it: hook their foot with your top leg or
your arm so the fold is locked, and keep a small amount of upward pressure into
the crease as you finish.

The second is a pacing error. A lean calf compresses almost immediately, so the
tap arrives in the first second. That is not a licence to go quickly — it is a
reason to go slowly, because you have far less margin between "uncomfortable" and
"torn calf muscle." Three to four seconds, as with everything else on this shelf.

### Big hamstrings and thick thighs

This is the build where the correct answer is often "no."

The calf slicer needs the fold to close, and the fold closes by the calf
approaching the hamstring. If both of those muscles are large, the leg reaches a
hard mechanical stop at 45° or so of knee flexion — muscle meeting muscle — with
nothing you can do about it. Push harder and you are not compressing anything;
you are just loading their knee joint through a mass of tissue.

Two real adjustments and one exit:

- **Swap the shin for a forearm.** A forearm is a much smaller-diameter wedge than
  a shin, so it fits into the shorter range of motion that is actually available.
  You lose force but you gain fit, and fit is the binding constraint here. Slide
  the forearm into the crease, grip your own wrist or their shin, and close the
  fold with your body rather than your arm strength.
- **Move the wedge to the joint line and change the target.** With the fold
  limited, compression is off the table; a wedge in the crease combined with a
  fold still levers the knee. Apply this version markedly more slowly, because
  the pain-before-damage margin that makes slicers comparatively forgiving comes
  from the muscle, and you have just removed the muscle from the equation.
- **Or leave.** A heavy-legged opponent is a poor calf slicer target and a good
  kneebar or heel hook target. The atlas's principle — position before
  submission — applies to submission *selection* too.

### Long legs

A long shin means the foot has a long way to travel before the fold closes, and
you will typically run out of arm length halfway there. The lock feels like it is
almost on for the entire time it is not working.

- **Drive the foot with your leg, not your arms.** Cross your top leg over their
  shin as high toward the ankle as you can reach and use hip and hamstring
  strength to close the fold. Your arms simply do not have the range.
- **Grip at the foot** if you are using your hands, never mid-shin. The lever
  arm is the only thing working in your favour here.
- **Keep the femur pinned.** On a long leg there is a lot of limb between your
  wedge and their hip, and every bit of that is somewhere the force can escape by
  rotating. Trap the thigh before you start closing.

### Short legs

The opposite: the heel is already close to the backside, so the fold closes
almost as soon as you begin. The lock arrives quickly and with very little
build-up.

The adjustment is entirely about pace. Set the wedge, turn the edge, close the
frame — and then apply at half your normal speed, because the ramp you are used
to feeling on longer legs is not there. On short, compact legs a slicer can go
from "not on" to "fully on" inside one second.

### Very flexible knees and hips

Some people fold up completely and comfortably. Their heel touches their backside
without effort, and the compression that makes most people tap barely registers.
They will also rotate the femur and spin out, because flexible hips make the
standard escape easy.

- **Control the femur first.** Pin the thigh with your hip or a hook before you
  close anything. Against this build the escape usually beats the submission
  unless you have removed rotation entirely.
- **Move the wedge to the joint line** and finish with the knee-distraction
  vector rather than the muscle compression, since the compression is not doing
  it.
- **Be willing to abandon.** A flexible person who folds comfortably around your
  shin is telling you the mechanism is not available. Chasing it by adding force
  turns a compression lock into a knee lever applied blind, which is precisely
  the thing this submission's belt restriction exists to prevent.

### Much heavier than you

Their leg is heavier, their hamstring is stronger, and folding it is a real
physical task rather than a formality.

- **Fold first, frame second.** Get the leg folded while you still have position
  and leverage, then close the figure-four around it. Trying to fold a heavy leg
  after your frame is already set rarely works.
- **Use both legs.** Arms are not going to do this. The bottom leg supplies the
  wedge, the top leg supplies the fold, your body supplies the arch.
- **Watch for the stand-up.** A much heavier opponent will often simply post a
  hand, stand, and walk out, dragging you. That is a position problem, not a
  submission problem — take the back or the sweep instead of holding a slicer
  that is being carried around the mat.

### Much lighter than you

There is less muscle between your shin and their knee, so the same wedge produces
much more compression, and the lock goes on far faster than you expect. Halve
your speed and stop at the first sign of resistance rather than waiting for a
reaction. Lighter partners also fold more easily, which means you can reach the
knee-distraction range without meaning to.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

- **Thin or bony shins.** A genuine advantage — your tibia is already a sharp
  edge, and slicers will work for you against builds that defeat other people.
  The corresponding duty is to go slower, because you generate more pressure per
  unit of effort than your partners realise.
- **Heavily muscled calves of your own.** Your shin is padded on the working
  surface, which blunts the wedge. Rotate your leg further so the bare front edge
  of the tibia leads, and be deliberate about the crease placement — you have
  less margin for a sloppy wedge.
- **Short legs.** Threading a shin behind a tall opponent's knee is a reach
  problem. The forearm wedge is a better default for you, and the truck and back
  positions suit you better than long entanglements do.
- **Long legs.** You can wedge and fold in one movement from positions others
  cannot reach. Beware of the corresponding sloppiness — a long leg lands in the
  calf belly as easily as in the crease.
- **Weak grip or sore fingers.** Good news: the figure-four leg version needs no
  grip at all. If you are straining your hands on a calf slicer, close the frame
  with your legs instead.
- **Lighter than most of your partners.** You will lose the folding contest
  against big legs. Prioritise positions where their leg arrives already folded —
  the truck, and the moment they fold a leg to defend a kneebar.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Very tight, they are unbothered | Your shin is on the calf belly, not in the knee crease | Slide the wedge up into the crease and turn the shin to its sharp edge |
| Your shin slides down as you finish | Nothing is anchoring the wedge — common on lean legs | Hook their foot to lock the fold; keep upward pressure into the crease |
| Their heel will not come near their backside | Big hamstring and calf meeting — a hard mechanical stop | Swap to a forearm wedge, or change locks; this leg may not be slice-able |
| You run out of arms halfway through the fold | Long shin, and you are closing with your hands | Close with your top leg from as high on their shin as you can reach |
| They rotate their thigh and spin out | You never controlled the femur | Pin the thigh or hook the hip before closing the fold |
| They post a hand and stand up with you attached | Weight difference, and a position problem rather than a submission one | Abandon the slicer and take the back or the sweep |
| It goes on instantly and they are angry afterwards | Light or lean partner, applied at your normal speed | Half speed on lean legs; stop at resistance, not at reaction |
| It only ever works on the same two training partners | You have one version — muscle compression | Learn the crease-wedge, joint-line version separately, and learn when to walk away |
| A referee stops you and penalises | Wrong belt | Brown and black only in IBJJF, gi and no-gi |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They straighten the leg hard to escape the fold | [Kneebar](./kneebar.md) — a straightened leg is what it wants (brown/black) |
| They rotate the femur to spin out of the wedge | [Heel hook](./heel-hook.md) from the saddle if the heel is exposed (adult brown/black, no-gi) |
| They pull the foot away from you to unfold | [Toe hold](./toe-hold.md) or [straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) — the foot has come to you |
| They turn away and give up their back defending | Take the back; hooks and the [rear naked choke](./rear-naked-choke.md). This is usually the better outcome |
| They flatten and post to stand | Take the sweep or the position; do not hold the slicer while they walk |
| Nothing is closing and you are stalling in the truck | Move to a back take. The truck is worth more as a position than as a finish |

**What feeds into it:** the truck (from turtle — see
[turtle escapes](../escapes/turtle-escapes.md) for the other side of that);
back control with a leg hooked through; a
[kneebar](./kneebar.md) attempt they defend by folding the leg — which builds the
slicer for you; 50/50 and the saddle; and the lockdown/electric-chair family from
half guard.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is a foot. Late defense is a torn calf.**

**Early — before the fold closes.** The tell is unmistakable: something hard has
appeared behind your knee.

- **Straighten the leg immediately and keep it straight.** A straight leg cannot
  be sliced, full stop. This is the whole defense, and it is available for
  several seconds if you react to the wedge rather than to the pain.
- **Hook their wedge leg with your free foot** and push it out of the crease
  before they can close anything.
- **Turn your hips toward the wedge** so your femur rotates and the fold cannot
  close cleanly.

**Middle — the fold has started.** Two things, together:

1. **Grab your own foot or shin and pull it away from your backside.** Ugly and
   very effective. Your arms working against their fold buy you the seconds you
   need for the next part.
2. **Hip escape away from the wedge and rotate**, which is the atlas's universal
   leg lock principle applied here — turn in the direction they are folding you
   so the load goes into rotation you can survive rather than compression you
   cannot.

**Late — the fold is closed and the pressure is on.** Tap.

There is a real and commonly repeated nuance here — the atlas mentions it in its
gap analysis — that compressions can hurt well before they are dangerous, unlike
a heel hook. That is true, and it is why some experienced grapplers will ride out
a slicer for a few seconds looking for an escape. **Do not treat it as
permission.** Two things are being loaded, not one: the calf can tear, and if the
wedge is up in the crease your knee joint is being levered as well. Riding out a
slicer to prove a point is how people end up limping for a month. If you are
newer than brown belt — meaning you are meeting this submission outside
competition, in an open mat — just tap. The atlas is blunt about it: tapping
early is a technique, not a failure.

## 8. Drills

1. **Wedge placement, no finish.** Partner lies passive. Place your shin in three
   positions — mid-calf, upper calf, and in the knee crease — and apply a small
   amount of fold at each. Have them report what they feel and where. 6 reps per
   side. Then repeat with a lean-legged partner and a heavy-legged one; the
   contrast is the entire lesson of section 3.
2. **Fold-closing drill.** From the entanglement, practise closing the fold ten
   times per side with your legs only — no hands, no finishing. The goal is to
   feel the point where the leg stops folding, which is different on every
   partner and is the thing that decides whether this submission is available.
3. **Edge-rotation drill.** With the wedge set and the fold light, rotate your
   own leg from flat-surface contact to sharp-edge contact and back, five times,
   while your partner reports the difference. Two minutes of this permanently
   changes how you set slicers.
4. **Positional sparring — truck and back, no finishes.** One player starts in the
   truck; the other must escape. Attacker scores for a back take or for
   establishing a closed slicer frame, then stops. 90-second rounds. Brown belt
   and above if you are going to allow the finish at all.

## 9. Safety

- **Apply slowly.** The atlas's leg lock rules apply here as everywhere: apply
  slowly, tap early. Three to four seconds from "frame closed" to "they should be
  tapping."
- **Compressions give more warning than rotations — but not much, and not
  always.** It is true that muscle compression hurts before it damages, and that
  is a genuine difference from a heel hook. It is not a reason to hold one on.
  A calf slicer can tear calf muscle, and a wedge sitting up in the knee crease
  is levering a joint, not just squeezing a muscle.
- **The deeper the wedge, the more serious the submission.** Sliding your shin up
  into the crease to beat a muscular calf is the right technical answer and it
  makes the lock more dangerous at the same time. Go slower when you do it.
- **Never finish with a jerk or a snap.** The closing action uses your legs and
  your whole body, which means it can generate a lot of force very quickly.
- **Release by opening the frame** and letting their leg unfold under control.
  Kicking it away on the tap is an easy way to hurt someone after the fact.
- **Ask about knee and calf history before the round.** A previously torn calf or
  a repaired knee changes the risk substantially.
- **Tap early. Knee ligaments do not recover like muscle**, and neither does a
  significant calf tear — which will keep you off the mat for weeks.

**Legality.** IBJJF permit the calf slicer for **adult brown and black belts
only**, in both gi and no-gi. It is illegal at white, blue and purple, in the same
group as the bicep slicer, the kneebar and the toe hold. Several common entries —
the truck, the saddle, 50/50 — also involve leg configurations where **knee
reaping** rules apply: reaping is illegal in the gi at every belt and legal in
no-gi only for adult brown and black belts. Rules change; check the current
official ruleset for your event.
