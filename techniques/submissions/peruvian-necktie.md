# Peruvian Necktie

> The front headlock's most committed finish: you sit, you roll them over your own hip, and their neck ends up compressed between your forearm and your thigh.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle with a compression/crank component — gi and no-gi |
| **Comes from** | Front headlock, a stuffed shot, turtle top, a snap-down, a failed guillotine |
| **Difficulty** | 4/5 |
| **How common** | 2/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts as a strangle. Finished as a neck crank it falls under the neck-crank/spinal-lock ban, which IBJJF applies at **every** belt — see §9 |

**Also known as:** Peruvian tie, PNT

---

## 1. The mechanism

Two surfaces, one neck. Your **forearm** lies across the front of their throat, threaded under their chin from the front headlock. Your **thigh or shin** lies across the back of their neck, at the base of the skull. You draw the two together: hands pull toward your chest, leg drives down toward the mat.

Done well, the blade of your forearm sits on the side of the neck and the compression closes a carotid while the leg supplies the head flexion that shortens the neck and brings the artery forward — a strangle. Done badly, the forearm sits on the windpipe and the leg drives the chin into the chest, and it becomes a straight neck crank: spinal flexion plus a bar across the throat. **The line between those two outcomes is where your forearm is aimed, and it is a line you have to stay on deliberately.** More on that in §9.

The roll exists for a reason beyond drama. From a standing or kneeling front headlock, they have a base and you do not have an angle — you cannot get a leg over the back of someone's neck while they are on their hands and knees pushing back into you. Sitting and rolling takes their base away, puts them on their back or their side, and delivers your leg to the back of their neck in the same motion. The roll is not a flourish; it is the only way to arrive at the finishing position.

---

## 2. The standard application

From a front headlock with them bent over or on all fours. Sides mirror, so this is described by **role**: the two legs have jobs, and which of your legs does which depends on which side you took the headlock from.

1. **Own the front headlock first.** Choking arm threaded under their chin so your forearm crosses the throat and your hand comes out the far side of their neck. Your chest is heavy on their upper back and their head is below the level of their hips. If they can lift their head, you do not have a front headlock and none of this works.
2. **Lock the grip palm-to-palm.** Your free hand meets the choking hand in a gable grip. **Do not grip your own wrist** — a gable grip lets you pull along the line of the choke; a wrist grip pulls at an angle and costs you depth.
3. **Take the slack out now, before you move.** Pull the grip toward your own sternum and pinch your elbows in. Everything after this point is harder to tighten than it is to pre-tighten.
4. **Step the leg nearer their hips over their back** — the "back leg" — so your shin lies across their shoulder blades and your foot plants on the far side of their body. **This is the leg you will roll over, and it is what stops them standing up or somersaulting out.**
5. **Sit down tight against their near shoulder.** No gap between your hip and their body. Any daylight here becomes space they escape into during the roll.
6. **Roll over the back leg's hip**, taking them with you. They turn onto their side or their back; you land on your side, still holding the grip. **Roll them over, do not throw them** — see §9.
7. **Bring the other leg — the "neck leg" — over the back of their neck** as you land, so your hamstring or shin sits at the base of their skull. Cross your ankles or pinch your knees to lock the head in place.
8. **Finish.** Pull the gable grip toward your chest and drive the neck leg down toward the mat. Aim the blade of your forearm at the **side** of their neck, not the middle of their throat.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The Peruvian necktie has three separate points of failure — getting the arm under a chin, getting a leg over a neck, and getting a roll to happen — and different builds break different ones. The front headlock itself is a position the atlas rates 3/5 and 4/5 for frequency; most of these adjustments are really about holding it long enough to finish.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Thick neck, heavy traps | The arm cannot get under the chin; you finish on the jaw | Chin-strap first and lift their chin *up and across* before threading; then let the **leg**, not the arm, be the primary compressing surface |
| Thin neck | The arm goes very deep and the finish becomes a pure crank | Rotate the forearm blade onto the side of the neck and drive the leg **less**; it is already a strangle without the extra flexion |
| Broad shoulders / thick upper back | Your neck leg lands on their shoulder ridge, not their neck | Sit closer to their ear — no gap at all — and drive their head across their own centreline first so the leg lands in the notch behind the ear |
| Long arms | They post far out and stand out of the front headlock before you sit | Kill the post: over-hook the near arm before you commit, or hit the roll while their arm is still extended |
| Long torso | Your back leg cannot reach across their back to plant on the far side | Hook the shin behind their far armpit instead of planting the foot; roll off a shorter lever |
| Much heavier than you | You cannot invert a heavier body over your own hip | Make them travel forward first (snap-down or drag), then sit — use their momentum; or skip the roll and finish with the "sit and stretch" version |
| Much lighter than you | They roll straight through and out, or spin to your back | Block the far hip with the back leg before you commit; expect to land in a scramble and be ready for it |
| Very flexible | They somersault forward through the front headlock | Take the necktie *on* the somersault — the roll was what you wanted; block the roll by driving their head down **and out**, not straight down |
| Very stiff / muscular back | Their spine will not flex, so the load lands on the jaw and shoulders | Get the leg lower — base of the skull, not mid-neck — get the grip higher under the jaw, and turn their head to one side so the compression is lateral rather than straight flexion |
| Gi | Extra grips available; the roll is easier | Grip their far lapel with the choking hand to lock depth; use the belt or pants to steer the roll |
| No gi / heavy sweat | The arm slides out under load exactly when you roll | Gable grip only, re-grip rather than re-squeeze, and pre-tighten before the roll |

---

### Thick neck and heavy traps

The front headlock arm has to travel from in front of their face, under their chin, and out the far side of their neck. On a thick-necked opponent with heavy traps, it does not get there — the arm runs out of length across the front of the throat and you end up with the crook of your elbow on their jaw and your hand nowhere useful. Rolling from there produces a face crank and nothing else.

**Use a chin strap first, then thread.** Do not attack the neck directly. Cup the point of their chin with your fingers, pull it **up and across** toward their far shoulder, and hold it there. This does two things: it lifts their head enough to expose several centimetres of throat that was previously buried against their chest, and it rotates their head so the near side of their neck is presented to you rather than the widest part of it. *Then* thread your arm into the space you have made.

**Let the leg be the primary compressing surface.** This is the important reframe. On a normal neck the arm and the leg share the work. On a thick neck the arm cannot get deep enough to be the main event, so change your expectation: get whatever arm depth you can, and make the **neck leg** the thing that finishes it. A thick neck actually helps here — a thicker neck raises your thigh higher off their spine and lengthens the lever your leg is working with. Aim the leg at the base of the skull and drive it hard while your arm merely holds the front.

**Go earlier.** You cannot out-muscle a deep wrap on a thick neck while they are still based and pushing back into you. Take the sit-and-roll a beat sooner than you would against a thin-necked opponent — the position on the ground is more forgiving than the position on the feet.

### Thin neck

The opposite risk, and it is a safety risk more than a technical one. On a slim neck your arm goes very deep very easily, your leg loads the back of the head at a steep angle, and what you end up with is not a strangle — it is a full-force spinal flexion with a bar under the jaw. It hurts a great deal, it will make them tap, and it is a crank.

**Rotate the blade onto the side of the neck** — turn your gripping forearm so the ulnar edge is on their carotid rather than flat across the windpipe — and **drive the leg noticeably less.** On a thin neck the arm alone is close to sufficient; the leg's job shrinks to holding the head still. If you find yourself needing the leg hard to make it finish, your forearm is in the wrong place.

### Broad shoulders and a thick upper back

The neck leg is the problem. On a wide-shouldered opponent your shin lands on the ridge of their trapezius, two or three inches lateral of the neck, and all your drive goes into a shoulder that is built to absorb it.

**Close the gap between your hip and their ear.** The single biggest cause of a leg landing on the shoulder is sitting too far away — from further out, the leg arrives at a shallow angle and skids off the top of the shoulder. Sitting flush against their ear means the leg comes over **downhill** and drops into the neck instead of across the shoulder.

**Push their head across their own centreline before you sit.** With the chin strap or your chest, drive their head toward their far shoulder. That opens up the notch behind the ear on the near side and gives your leg a target that is neck rather than trapezius.

If it still will not land, the Japanese necktie — the same family with a different trapping-arm configuration — accommodates a wide shoulder girdle better and is the honest alternative.

### Long-armed opponents

Long arms mean long posts. From a front headlock, a tall opponent puts a hand far out in front of themselves and simply stands up out of the position while you are still setting your grip.

**Kill the post before you commit to sitting.** Over-hook their near arm with your free arm — the classic "head and arm" front headlock rather than the pure chin-strap version — so they have one post instead of two. Or take the roll *while* their arm is extended out in front of them and their weight is over it: an extended post is a lever you can roll them over, and it collapses forward the moment you sit.

### Long torso

Your back leg has to reach across the width of their back and plant on the far side. On a long, deep torso it will not get there, and a half-committed leg across their spine is what they escape through.

Do not reach — **hook.** Bring the shin over their back and hook the foot behind their far armpit rather than planting it on the mat. That gives you the same rolling lever over a much shorter distance and it is more secure against a scramble. Roll over that hook.

### Much heavier than you

The roll is the failure point. You are attempting to invert a heavier body over your own hip, and if you simply sit down under someone significantly bigger, you end up on your back with them on top of you and your neck attack gone.

**Make them travel first.** Snap them down, or cross-face and drag them forward, so their weight is already moving toward your side before you sit. Then sit into the momentum you created. You are redirecting a moving body, not lifting a static one.

**Or skip the roll entirely.** The "sit and stretch" version is the honest answer against a much bigger opponent: sit close to their shoulder, get the back leg over their back, and instead of rolling them, **extend** — straighten your body and drive them flat onto their face while the neck leg pins the back of their head. You never complete the inversion, they never end up on their back, and the strangle still closes because the two compressing surfaces are already in place. It scores nothing and finishes plenty.

### Much lighter than you

Smaller, quicker opponents do not resist the roll — they go *with* it, roll straight through, and come up on the other side, often on your back. The technique that finishes a same-size partner hands a lightweight a free scramble.

Block the exit first: place the back leg so it also blocks their far hip, not just their back, before you commit to sitting. And plan the landing — expect the scramble, expect to end up back in a front headlock or in a race for their back, and make peace with that before you start. Against a much lighter opponent the front headlock itself, held patiently, is often worth more than the necktie.

### Very flexible

A flexible opponent's answer to a front headlock is to somersault forward through it. This is the build where the technique gets counter-intuitive: **their escape is your entry.** The roll is what you wanted anyway. When they start the somersault, do not fight it — take the necktie on it. Ride the rotation, sit, and let their own tumble deliver them to the finishing position.

If you would rather they stayed put, do not drive their head straight down — drive it **down and out**, away from their own hips. Downward pressure feeds the somersault; forward-and-away pressure stalls it.

### Very stiff or very muscular back

The finish depends partly on head flexion, and a rigid, heavily muscled thoracic spine simply does not flex. Your leg drives, their spine stays stacked, and the load ends up on their jaw and the tops of their shoulders — which is painful, ineffective and closer to a crank than a strangle.

Three changes. **Get the leg lower** — the base of the skull, not the middle of the neck; the lower the contact, the less spinal flexion is needed. **Get the grip higher** — the forearm right under the jawline rather than down on the collarbones. And **turn their head to one side** before you drive, so what you are producing is lateral compression on a carotid rather than a straight forward crank on the spine. On a stiff opponent, the rotated version is both more effective and much safer.

### Gi versus no-gi

The atlas rates this as working in both, and it does — but they are noticeably different techniques.

**In the gi**, once your choking hand is through, grip **their far lapel**. That locks your depth permanently: sweat, bridging and shrugging stop mattering, and the arm cannot be stretched back out. You can also steer the roll with a belt or pant grip, which makes the inversion much more reliable against a bigger opponent. The gi turns the least reliable part of this technique into a fixed structure.

**In no-gi**, sweat is the enemy and the roll is when you lose the grip — that is the exact moment the arm is loaded hardest and is least supported. Use a gable grip only, **pre-tighten before you sit** rather than planning to tighten after you land, and if the grip slips, re-grip rather than squeezing harder. Also expect their head to slide: a sweaty head is a slippery thing to hold with a thigh, so the ankle cross or knee pinch in step 7 matters much more without a gi.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Getting under a chin is your bottleneck on anyone larger. Make the chin strap your default entry rather than the direct thread, and build your version around the leg being the primary compressing surface. Take the roll earlier.

**Long arms.** Depth is easy and it will tempt you into a wrap that goes all the way around the throat — which is where the crank risk lives. Check that your forearm blade is on the side of the neck and that you are not simply levering their head into their chest.

**Short legs.** Reaching a shin across a wide back to plant on the far side may not be available. Hook behind the far armpit instead, and sit flush against their ear so the neck leg does not have to travel far.

**Long legs.** Both leg placements are easy and you will over-commit — a leg thrown loosely over the back or the neck is a leg they can duck under. Place them deliberately rather than throwing them.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** The front headlock scales well across a weight gap; the roll does not. Learn the "sit and stretch" version first and treat the full inversion as the version you use on people your own size or smaller.

**Heavier than most of your partners.** Your problem is not finishing, it is finishing too fast and too hard. This technique lands your bodyweight and a leg on a neck. Go slower than feels necessary, and see §9.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Your arm is on their jaw, not their throat | You never got under the chin | Chin strap first — lift the chin up and across, then thread into the gap |
| They stand up out of the front headlock | You did not control an arm; they had two posts | Over-hook the near arm, or roll while their post is extended |
| You sit and end up flat under them | You tried to invert a heavier person from a standstill | Snap or drag them forward first, or use the sit-and-stretch version instead |
| Your leg is on their shoulder, not their neck | You sat too far from their ear, or their head was square | Sit flush to their ear; drive their head across their centreline first |
| They somersault out | You drove their head straight down | Drive down **and out** — or take the necktie on the somersault |
| Grip pops open during the roll | You planned to tighten after landing | Pre-tighten before you sit; gable grip, elbows pinched |
| They tap but it was all jaw and spine | Forearm across the windpipe, leg driving pure flexion | Rotate the blade onto the side of the neck; get the leg lower and turn their head |
| It finishes but their spine is loading first | Stiff opponent, straight-line flexion | Turn their head to one side and make the compression lateral |
| Grip slides apart mid-roll in no-gi | Sweat, and no cloth to anchor | Gable grip only; re-grip rather than squeeze; in the gi, take the far lapel |
| They roll through and take your back | No block on the far hip | Position the back leg to block the hip before committing to sit |

## 6. Chains

The front headlock is a hub, not a destination — the atlas rates it 4/5 for frequency for exactly that reason. If the necktie is not there, something else is.

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Hands to the mat, driving forward to stand | Go behind — the classic front-headlock back take |
| They pull their head out backwards | Guillotine, or re-attack the collar tie |
| They defend the arm under the chin | D'Arce or anaconda — the arm is already in the right neighbourhood |
| They roll through the necktie | Follow to their back, or re-take the front headlock |
| Broad shoulders block the neck leg | Japanese necktie — the same family with a different arm configuration |
| They turtle up tight and wait | Clock choke or [loop choke](./loop-choke.md) in the gi; back take in no-gi |
| They flatten out onto their stomach | Ride to the back and hunt the [bow and arrow](./bow-and-arrow.md) or an RNC |
| They roll to their back to escape the front headlock | [North–south choke](./north-south-choke.md) from the position you just landed in |

Feeding in: the necktie is the standard reward for a stuffed shot, a snap-down, a failed guillotine attempt on their part, and any turtle where you have their head. It is also the reason the front headlock is worth drilling on its own — everything above starts from the same grip.

## 7. Defending it

**Early — and early is nearly everything here.** Do not be in a front headlock with your head below your hips. When you feel an arm coming under your chin, tuck your chin hard to your chest immediately — a chin welded down is very hard to thread under — and get your head **outside** their hip on one side rather than centred between their legs. Post both hands and start standing before their grip closes. Hand-fight the wrist of the arm coming under your chin with both of your hands, and strip it before the gable grip forms.

**Middle — grip locked, they are sitting down.** The moment you feel them sit, **do not stay on your knees.** Two options and you must pick one instantly. Either drive your hips forward and up to stand, running your head out of the wrap while their base is momentarily gone; or turn hard toward them and come up on top of their sitting leg, so the roll never happens. Get a hand between your neck and their forearm if you can — a wedged hand keeps the arm off the artery and turns a strangle into a stalemate. Do not simply pull backwards with your neck; that feeds the wrap.

**Late — rolled, leg on the back of your head, grip drawing in.** There is essentially nothing. Your head is trapped between a forearm and a thigh, you are on your side or your back with no base, and the two surfaces do not fatigue. **Late defense is far worse than early defense here, and worse than it is for most chokes**, because the compression has a crank component: waiting it out risks your neck rather than just your consciousness. Tap immediately, tap loudly, and tap with a foot on the mat if your hands are trapped.

## 8. Drills

**1. Front headlock holding (3 × 2 minutes).** No submissions. You hold the front headlock, they try to stand up, roll out or go behind. This is the actual bottleneck — most failed neckties are failed front headlocks.

**2. Sit-and-roll reps, cooperative (10 per side).** Slow, no squeeze at all. The only goals are the two leg placements and landing flush. Say them out loud as you go: *back leg over the back — sit tight — roll — neck leg over the neck.* Grip stays loose throughout so nobody's neck is loaded while you are learning the footwork.

**3. Sit-and-stretch reps (10 per side).** The no-roll version, against a partner heavier than you if one is available. This is the version that will actually work in a hard round, and almost nobody drills it.

**4. Positional round — front headlock, 90 seconds.** Start with them on all fours and you in a front headlock, grip already set. You attack the necktie and its chains; they escape or stand. Reset every 90 seconds and swap.

---

## 9. Safety

**This is the least safe technique in this folder and it needs to be treated that way.**

Two specific hazards. First, **the crank component.** Applied correctly it is a strangle: forearm blade on a carotid, leg supplying flexion. Applied even slightly wrong it is a spinal-flexion crank with a bar under the jaw — and it will still make people tap, which means you can practise the wrong version for years and think it is working. Check every rep: is your forearm on the *side* of their neck? If it is flat across the front of the throat and your leg is driving their chin into their chest, you are cranking their neck, not choking them. Fix the aim, do not add force.

Second, **the roll.** You are taking someone over while their head is trapped and their hands are usually not free to post. Roll them, do not throw them, and never drive their head into the mat as part of the takeover — a spiked head is a catastrophic injury and slams are banned in almost every ruleset. Take the roll along the mat, not down into it.

Apply the finish slowly. Their hands are frequently trapped by your legs and their voice is muffled against your body, so agree in advance that a foot tapping the mat or any audible noise counts as a tap. Then listen. Release the leg first and the grip second, so their head unloads before their throat does.

**Legality.** As a strangle, the Peruvian necktie is legal at all belts in the major rulesets. As a neck crank it is not: the atlas's IBJJF table lists neck cranks and spinal locks as prohibited at **every** belt, white through black. That is not a technicality — a referee watching a necktie is watching to see whether their head is being flexed or their neck is being strangled. Some gyms and some events treat the move as borderline for exactly this reason. If you compete, know your event's position on it. If you train, do the strangle version and nothing else.
