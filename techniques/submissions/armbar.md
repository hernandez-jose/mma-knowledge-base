# Armbar

> Their elbow hyperextended over your hips with their thumb pointing up — the most common joint lock in the sport, and the only submission available from all five dominant positions.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Joint lock (elbow hyperextension) |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, mount / S-mount, side control, knee-on-belly, back control |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** juji gatame, cross armlock, straight armlock, juji

Per [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) the armbar accounts for roughly 20% of all
finishes — the highest of any joint lock. Chokes still finish more often and
break less, so build the neck game first; but if you learn one arm attack, learn
this one, because every position on the ladder feeds it.

---

## 1. The mechanism

The elbow is a hinge. It flexes to roughly 145 degrees and stops at straight —
give or take a few degrees of natural hyperextension. The armbar drives it past
that stop. Three forces do it: your legs pin their shoulder and torso **down**,
your hips push **up** against their upper arm, and your hands pull their
wrist **down**. The humerus is held still; the forearm keeps travelling; the
joint capsule and the collateral ligaments at the front of the elbow take the
load.

Two details decide whether that actually happens.

**Their thumb points up.** With the thumb toward the ceiling, the hinge axis of
the elbow is square to your hip line and the point of the elbow (the olecranon)
aims straight at you. Rotate their thumb toward the floor and the whole humerus
internally rotates: the hinge axis swings away from your hip, your pull stops
hyperextending the elbow and starts rotating the shoulder instead. That rotation
is not a submission — it is their [hitchhiker escape](#7-defending-it).

**The fulcrum is your hip, not your groin.** The last point of contact between
your body and their humerus is the pivot. It should be the front of your hip and
the top of your thigh, buried into their armpit, so their elbow hangs just off
the far edge of it with nothing underneath. If their arm slides down onto your
pubic bone or your belly, three things go wrong at once: your hips can no longer
be tight to their shoulder, the elbow is now resting *on* you and supported from
both sides so it cannot open, and elevating your hips lifts their whole body
instead of bending their arm. Groin fulcrum is the single most common reason a
"tight-looking" armbar does nothing.

Everything in section 3 is a variation on one question: **where does the edge of
your hip sit relative to their elbow?**

---

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard, against someone your size.

1. **Break their posture** — pull their head down with a collar grip, a neck tie
   or a two-on-one on the arm. An armbar attempted against upright posture is a
   sweep at best.
2. Isolate the arm: grip above their elbow and **drag it across your
   centreline** so their trapped arm is on the far side of your sternum. Their
   elbow crossing your midline is the moment the armbar becomes real.
3. Open your guard and **angle out** — shrimp your hips 30–45 degrees toward the
   trapped-arm side so your spine is closing on perpendicular to theirs. If you
   stay square, you will finish nothing.
4. Put your non-trapped-side foot on their hip, swing your other leg high and
   over their head, and clamp your **knees pinched together** on their upper
   arm. The pinch is not decoration — it is what holds the humerus still.
5. Take the wrist with both hands, **thumb up**, and pull their forearm flat
   across your sternum, blade of the forearm on your chest. Take the slack out
   *before* you commit your hips.
6. Curl the heel of your top leg toward your own backside so your calf, not your
   hamstring, presses their head down. A bent leg is far stronger here than an
   extended one.
7. Roll slightly onto the hip nearest their head so your hip crest sits in their
   armpit, and elevate — **hips up, shoulders down** — until they tap.

Steps 4 and 5 together are the answer to the bicep curl. They cannot fold the
arm if the humerus cannot rotate (knees) and the wrist cannot travel (hands). If
either one is loose, they will curl out and you will lose it.

### From the other four positions

| Position | What changes | The detail people miss |
|---|---|---|
| **Mount / S-mount** | Ride to high mount, shape one leg into an S under their armpit, isolate the far arm, then step over the face and sit back. | Get your knee **under their armpit** before you rotate; from flat mount they just curl the arm and bump you. |
| **Side control** | Feed the far arm across, drive your knee over their belly, then step over the head as if mounting and sit through. | Kill the near arm first or they frame and turn in as you swing over. |
| **Knee-on-belly** | Their panic push is the invitation: they extend an arm into your knee or hip, you trap it, step over the head and sit back. | Keep the knee on them until your leg has cleared their head, or you land in their guard. |
| **Back control** | When both their hands defend the collar, take the far arm off their neck, throw the same-side leg over their head and roll them belly-down or sit back to the mat. | You are giving up the back — take it only when the arm is already isolated, and expect them to spin. |

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section the library exists for.** The armbar is the clearest case
in jiu-jitsu of a technique whose geometry changes completely with limb length.
The steps above are written for an arm about as long as yours. Almost nobody's
is.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Short arms** | Their elbow never clears the far edge of your hip, so the joint is supported on both sides and cannot open. You extend and nothing happens. | Slide your hips 5 cm (two inches) *toward their shoulder*, deeper into the armpit, so the elbow hangs off the edge. Pinch the knees hard to stop the humerus sliding back. |
| **Long arms** | You get extension easily, but there is a long span of unsupported humerus for them to fold, and a long forearm to grab things with. They bend the arm and stack you. | Take the wrist earlier and higher — forearm across your sternum pointing over your opposite shoulder — and elevate the hips further through a bigger arc. Get the angle before they get their feet under them. |
| **Thick, muscular arms** | The arm won't seat between your knees, and their curl beats your pull in a straight strength contest. Bulk on the back of the arm also eats your last few degrees. | Attack the grip, not the arm: two hands on one of theirs, peel at the thumb side, or wedge a shin over the top of their clasped hands. Then finish with rotation, not just extension. |
| **Thin, slippery arms** | Nothing for the knee pinch to hold. The arm slides straight through and out. | Stop relying on the pinch. Trap the *forearm* with a hug — one arm over, one under — or a sleeve/cuff grip in the gi, and keep the far leg heavy so the shoulder can't travel. |
| **Hypermobile elbows** | Their elbow goes 10–15 degrees past straight without pain and they do not tap where a normal arm does. | Take the slack out fully, then add rotation: turn belly-down toward their thumb so internal rotation runs out before extension does. Know when to swap to the wrist or the shoulder. |
| **Stiff shoulders** | The arm won't come across your centreline at all — the block is at their shoulder, not their elbow, and they tap from shoulder pain before the elbow is loaded. | Stop dragging the arm; move *your* body around them instead. Take the angle with your hips and let the arm stay where it is. |
| **Much heavier than you** | They stack you flat and drive their weight down the arm. Your top leg gets crushed and you lose the head. | Finish on your side, top shoulder off the mat, heel curled to your backside. If the stack starts, turn belly-down with it instead of resisting it vertically. |
| **Much lighter than you** | They spin, invert and pull the arm out of a gap you didn't notice, and their elbow reaches end range fast. | Control the far side (far armpit or lapel), keep a foot driving their hip, and apply at half your usual speed. |
| **Long torso** | Your bottom leg lands across their belly instead of their chest, so they sit up and posture out. | Slide the bottom leg two hand-widths up so your knee is near their sternum, or hook the far shoulder with your instep. |

### Short arms — the canonical problem

This is the one that convinces people the library is necessary. You have a
textbook armbar: knees pinched, wrist controlled, thumb up. You elevate. Nothing
happens. They look mildly uncomfortable and start curling.

Here is why. Your hip is a blunt fulcrum roughly a hand's width across. If their
upper arm is short, the whole distance from shoulder to elbow may be barely wider
than that. With your hips at the normal distance, their shoulder is at one edge
of your hip and their elbow is at the *other edge* — resting on you. A joint
supported on both sides cannot hyperextend. You are trying to snap a stick that
is lying flat on a table.

The fix is not more force. It is to move the table.

1. **Move your hips up the arm, toward their shoulder.** Bridge slightly and
   walk your hips 5 cm (two inches) further into their armpit, so your hip crest
   is against their deltoid rather than their mid-humerus. Their elbow now hangs
   past the far edge of your hip with air underneath it. That air is the whole
   technique.
2. **Pinch the knees harder than feels necessary.** Having moved the contact
   point toward their shoulder, the only thing stopping the humerus sliding back
   across your hip is your thighs. Knees glued, ankles crossed or feet pointed
   down.
3. **Claim every centimetre of forearm.** A short arm gives you a short handle.
   Pull their wrist to your *sternum or the far side of your chest*, not to your
   belly — hugging it low wastes the little lever you have.
4. **Elevate diagonally, not vertically.** Drive your hips up and toward their
   head-side shoulder rather than straight to the ceiling. That keeps the humerus
   trapped against your ribs while the elbow opens.
5. **Add a few degrees of rotation.** Pull the thumb-up wrist very slightly
   across toward your own opposite ear. A short elbow needs less linear travel
   if some of the travel is rotational.

One warning: short-armed opponents are usually strong pullers — a short lever
favours the curler. Break the grip *before* you lie back, not after.

### Long arms — the opposite problem

Long-armed opponents give you the extension for free and then punish you for it.
Two things go wrong.

First, there is a long, unsupported span of humerus between the edge of your hip
and their elbow. That span is a lever *they* own: they can fold the arm at the
elbow much more easily than a short-armed opponent can, because the fold happens
out in space where you aren't touching them.

Second, a long forearm reaches. They can grab their own belt, their own thigh,
your leg, or the mat, and use it to stack.

- **Own the wrist before you fall back, not after.** With a long arm the slack
  lives at the wrist. Grip the forearm blade just above the wrist with both
  hands and pull it across your sternum so their hand points over your opposite
  shoulder. That takes the slack out and pre-loads the thumb-up rotation in one
  movement.
- **Hug, don't grip.** Put your top arm *over* their forearm and clamp it to your
  chest with your elbow, instead of gripping the wrist with fingers. A long
  forearm has too much leverage against finger strength.
- **Elevate higher.** A longer arm needs a longer arc before the elbow reaches
  end range. Where a short arm needs your hips 5 cm off the mat, a long arm may
  need 15 cm. Bridge from your feet, don't crunch from your back.
- **Kill the free hand early.** Post your bottom foot on their near hip or hook
  their far shoulder with your calf so the free arm can't come across to help
  them fold the trapped one.
- **Pre-empt the stack.** Take the angle to a full 90 degrees between your spine
  and theirs before you commit. A stack needs them to drive forward over their
  own toes; if you are perpendicular, forward for them is sideways for you and
  the drive goes nowhere.
- **If the stack lands anyway, go with it.** Do not push back vertically — you
  will lose. Turn belly-down toward their thumb and finish face-down, or bail to
  a [triangle](./triangle-choke.md) or [omoplata](./omoplata.md) as your leg
  comes free.

### Thick, muscular arms — beating the grip, not the person

A heavy-armed opponent defends by clasping their hands (gable or S-grip) and
curling. Two problems: the arm barely fits between your knees, and their curl is
genuinely strong.

- **Set the knee pinch higher.** A big bicep means the arm's widest point is
  mid-humerus. Pinch above the bulk, closer to their shoulder, or your knees ride
  down the taper and the arm squirts out.
- **Break the connection before you break the arm.** In order of preference:
  two hands on *one* of their hands and peel at the thumb; step your top leg's
  foot over their clasped hands and use your shin as a wedge to lever them apart;
  or drive your heel into their far shoulder to lengthen the gap between their
  hands.
- **Never pull straight against a curl.** Their bicep beats your arms. Take the
  slack, then extend your *hips* — your posterior chain against their bicep is a
  fight you win.
- **Expect the tissue to bottom out.** On a very thick arm the mass on the back
  of the upper arm and the forearm meet before the joint reaches end range, so
  pure extension may simply run out of room. Convert: turn belly-down to add
  internal rotation, take the [wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md) you already have the
  grip for, or switch to a [kimura](./kimura.md) on the same arm.

### Thin, slippery arms

The opposite failure: everything is tight and then the arm is simply gone.

- **The pinch is not enough.** There is not enough limb to clamp. Make your
  *hands and chest* the primary retention: their forearm blade flat on your
  sternum, one of your arms over it and one under, hands joined.
- **In the gi, take cloth.** A cuff or sleeve grip on a skinny arm does what the
  knee pinch does on a thick one.
- **Trap the shoulder, not the arm.** A thin arm only slides out when the
  shoulder is free to move. Keep the far leg heavy across their chest and your
  hips glued to their armpit — no gap, no slide.
- **Slow down.** Less muscle mass around the joint means less padding and a
  shorter distance between tight and injured.

### Hypermobile elbows — when they don't tap

Some people — often generally hypermobile — carry 10 to 15 degrees of natural
hyperextension. Their arm looks alarming when it's straight and they feel
nothing. Cranking harder on an arm that has already given you everything is how
people get hurt, on both sides.

- **Take out every millimetre of slack first.** Wrist to your chest, hips glued,
  knees pinched, *then* elevate. Hypermobile joints hide slack everywhere.
- **Add rotation.** Turn belly-down toward their thumb. Rotation reaches end
  range long before extension does on a hypermobile elbow, and the combination is
  what finally registers.
- **Change joints.** You already hold their wrist — bend it
  ([wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md), blue belt and up under IBJJF). Or release the
  extension, re-bend the arm and take the [kimura](./kimura.md); a hypermobile
  elbow does not imply a hypermobile shoulder.
- **Safety note that runs the other way:** a hyperextending elbow gives less
  warning, not more. When it fails, it fails suddenly. Apply slower against these
  arms, not faster.

### Stiff shoulders

Some opponents cannot get their arm across their own centreline at all. You pull
and the whole torso comes with it, and they wince from the shoulder while the
elbow is still slack.

- **Stop pulling the arm to your centreline. Move your centreline to the arm.**
  Shrimp your hips further out and rotate your body around their arm instead of
  hauling their arm around their body.
- **Attack the near arm, not the far one.** Stiff-shouldered opponents defend
  the far-arm drag well and the near arm badly.
- **Watch the shoulder tap.** If they tap while your armbar isn't even on, they
  tapped for the shoulder. That is a legitimate tap. Release immediately.

### Much heavier than you

Weight beats structure only if you let them apply it in a straight line.

- **Break posture before you commit, every time.** A heavy opponent who is still
  upright when your leg goes over the head will simply stand and shake you off,
  or lift and slam.
- **Curl the top heel to your own backside.** Extended, your hamstring holds
  their head and their weight wins. Bent, your calf and hamstring together are
  much stronger and their head is pulled *down*, which is where you need it.
- **Never finish flat.** Roll onto the hip nearest their head with your top
  shoulder off the mat. Flat on your back you are a platform for their weight;
  on your side, their weight drives past you.
- **Choose the belly-down finish.** Turning face-down converts their downward
  pressure into your finishing force instead of fighting it.
- **From the top, take the far arm.** A heavy opponent's near arm is defended by
  their whole body. From S-mount, isolating the far arm makes their size work
  against them — they cannot easily bench you and rescue the far side at once.

### Much lighter than you

- **They will spin, not stack.** A light opponent's escape is to invert, roll or
  simply thread the arm out through a gap. Close the gaps: control their far
  armpit or lapel with your bottom arm so their upper body cannot rotate.
- **Keep a foot driving their near hip** so they cannot shrimp back to guard as
  the arm slips.
- **They reach end range fast.** Less limb mass, less muscular protection.
  Elevate at a crawl and expect the tap earlier than your reflexes want it.
- **Do not chase.** If a light opponent frees the arm, take the position they
  gave you rather than diving after the arm — the scramble is where they live.

### Long torso, short torso

Torso length changes where your legs land, which changes whether they can sit up.

- **Long torso:** your bottom leg lands across their belly rather than their
  chest and they crunch up into you. Slide that leg two hand-widths higher so
  the knee is near their sternum, or hook their far shoulder with your instep.
- **Long torso plus short arms** is the worst combination and worth recognising
  by feel: you cannot simultaneously get your leg across their chest and your hip
  under their elbow. Pick the arm. Sacrifice chest control, get your hips into
  the armpit, and use your top leg's calf on their head to stop them sitting up.
- **Short torso:** control is easy, but their knees are close to their chest and
  they get to a stack posture quickly. Take the angle sooner and finish sooner.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**If your arms are short:** you cannot reach their wrist comfortably once you
lie back, and reaching costs you the chest connection. Bring the arm to you
instead of going to it — pull with your legs (knees pinched, heel down on their
head) until the forearm is on your sternum, then clamp it with your elbow rather
than gripping with your hands. In the gi, grip your own lapel over their arm.

**If your arms are long:** you will grip the wrist too early and too far away,
which lets the arm sit off your chest with slack in it. Deliberately shorten:
take the grip, then pull the whole arm in until their forearm touches your
sternum before you elevate.

**If your hands are small or your grip is weak:** the palm-to-palm and S-grip
finishes fail. Use the harness instead — one arm over the forearm, one under,
gripping your own wrist or forearm — and let your chest do the holding.

**If you are much lighter than most of your partners:** the belly-down armbar is
your default finish, not your backup. It removes their weight advantage from the
equation and gives you a full body-rotation lever instead of a hip bridge. Also
prefer entries where you start with the angle already made — knee-on-belly and
S-mount — over spinning from closed guard, where a heavy opponent can pin you
mid-spin.

**If your hips are stiff or your back is long:** throwing a leg over the head
from closed guard is a hip-mobility skill and you may not have it yet. Use the
top-position entries: from mount, *step over the face* rather than spinning; from
knee-on-belly, the leg travels over the head with your weight already on them.
Drill the spin cold, slowly, on your own — it improves fast.

**If you are much heavier than your partners:** your risk is that your hip
elevation is far more force than their elbow needs. Elevate a fraction of what
you think, and never bridge explosively.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Arm is straight, hips are up, they look fine | Fulcrum is on your groin or belly — the elbow is supported on both sides | Walk your hips into their armpit until their elbow hangs off the edge of your hip |
| They fold the arm and you can't stop it | Knees are open, or their hands are joined | Pinch the knees, then break the grip with a two-on-one or a shin wedge — don't pull harder |
| Arm slides out as you elevate | Gap between your hips and their shoulder | Close the gap first, elevate second; hug the forearm rather than gripping the wrist |
| They stack you and your neck is loading | You never got the angle; you're square to them | Turn belly-down with the stack, or bail to triangle/omoplata; never fight a stack vertically |
| Their thumb ends up pointing at the floor and it loosens | They've started the hitchhiker | Follow their rotation and re-take the arm on the other side, or switch to the back |
| You finish it but their shoulder hurts, not their elbow | Fulcrum too high, near their armpit, or the arm is bent | Move the contact 2–3 cm toward their elbow and re-straighten the arm |
| You lose it every time they bridge | Top leg is extended, so their head escapes | Curl the heel to your backside; calf on the neck, not hamstring |
| It works in drilling, never in rolling | You're entering without breaking posture | Posture first. Always. No exceptions |

## 6. Chains

### Their defense → your next attack

| Their defense | Your next attack | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Bicep curl, hands clasped | [Wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md) on the top hand, or [kimura](./kimura.md) on the bent arm | Their curl hands you a bent arm and a wrist |
| Stack and drive | [Triangle](./triangle-choke.md) or [omoplata](./omoplata.md) | Their head is already between your legs and their posture is broken forward |
| Hitchhiker (thumb to the floor) | Follow the rotation to their back, or re-armbar the other side | They must turn away from you to unwind |
| Pull the arm out and posture | [Omoplata](./omoplata.md) or sweep to top | Their arm leaving the gap is a shoulder you can attack with the leg |
| Grab their own belt / lapel | Break the grip, or switch to the far arm | Two-on-one beats one grip; the far arm is now undefended |
| Stand up to escape | Release and come up — [wrestle-up](../../jujitsu.md) or leg entanglement | Never get lifted; take the position instead |

### What feeds into it

| From | The trigger |
|---|---|
| Triangle attempt | They posture and pull the arm out — armbar the free arm |
| [Kimura](./kimura.md) grip | They straighten the arm to defend — that's an armbar |
| [Omoplata](./omoplata.md) | They roll forward — follow and take the arm |
| Back control | Both hands defend the collar — take the far arm off the neck |
| Knee-on-belly | They push the knee with a straight arm |
| Mount, high | They frame on your hips with a straight arm |

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is enormously better than late defense.** By the time your arm
is straight and their hips are elevated, you are relying on their mistake. The
atlas rates both the bicep curl and the hitchhiker at [3/5
difficulty](../../jujitsu.md) — they are not beginner skills, and neither is a
substitute for not being there.

**Early — before the arm is isolated.** Elbows in, hands on your own centreline,
never leave an arm across your body. When someone breaks your posture in guard,
your first job is your elbows, not your head. If they start to angle out, square
back up — the angle *is* the armbar.

**Middle — the arm is trapped, the leg is over.** Turn your thumb toward the
ceiling immediately; that is the direction that preserves your elbow. Clasp your
hands (palm-to-palm is stronger than an S-grip against a pull) and curl the arm.
Then, and only then, pick a direction: drive your near shoulder toward their far
hip and **stack**, walking your feet toward their head, keeping your head up.
Stacking with your head down turns their armbar into a neck problem.

**Late — arm straight, hips elevated.** Your options are the hitchhiker escape —
rotate your thumb toward the floor and walk your body around to unwind the arm —
or the tap. Be honest about which one is available. The hitchhiker works when
they have let the arm rotate; against a tight thumb-up armbar it does not, and
the difference between the two is about half a second. **Tap for the elbow.**
Elbow ligaments do not forgive gambling, and the atlas is blunt about it: the tap
is a technique, not a failure.

One more: if they have you belly-down, the escape window has usually closed.
Tap.

## 8. Drills

**1. Fulcrum feel (5 minutes, no reps to failure).**
Partner lies on their back, arm extended. Set up the armbar and elevate two
centimetres — first with their arm across your groin, then with your hip crest
in their armpit. Swap and feel both. This single comparison teaches more than
fifty repetitions of the entry.

**2. The spin (20 per side, solo or with a passive partner).**
From closed guard: break posture, drag the arm across, hip out, leg over,
knees pinched, elevate one inch, release. Slow. The goal is that hip-out step
before the leg swings — most people swing first and then try to fix the angle.

**3. Grip-break circuit (3 rounds x 10 each).**
Partner locks a gable grip and curls at 70%. Break it three ways: two-on-one at
the thumb; shin wedge over the clasped hands; heel into the far shoulder. Ten
of each. Reset every time they win.

**4. Positional sparring — armbar finish (90-second rounds).**
Start with the armbar already on: your legs over, their arm bent and hands
clasped. Their job is to escape, yours is to finish. This is the round that
exposes whether you actually understand section 3 — do it with a short-armed
partner and a long-armed partner in the same session and notice how differently
you have to place your hips.

## 9. Safety

The elbow gives very little warning. Unlike a choke, where the person losing has
several seconds of clear feedback, an armbar goes from tight to damaged in a
fraction of a second — and hyperextension injuries take months, not weeks.

- **Apply at a speed that allows a tap.** Elevate your hips over roughly a
  second, not explosively. Never throw your hips.
- **Never jerk, never bounce.** Take the slack out, then apply steadily.
- **Release instantly on a tap** — hand, foot, voice, or any sound that might be
  a tap. Do not "hold it a second longer" to make a point.
- **Hypermobile partners need more caution, not less.** The joint fails suddenly
  after giving you no feedback.
- **Do not drill flying armbars** in a normal class. The atlas rates them 5/5
  difficulty and 1/5 frequency for good reason, and they are rough on the
  receiving partner's elbow and neck.
- **If you're defending:** tap for the elbow. Every time. Stacking is a real
  defense; gambling on it once your arm is straight is not.
- **Legality:** legal at every belt in IBJJF gi and no-gi, and in ADCC, EBI and
  every mainstream ruleset. See the belt legality table in
  [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).
