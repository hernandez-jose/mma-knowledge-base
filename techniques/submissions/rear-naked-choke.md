# Rear Naked Choke

> A two-sided carotid strangle applied from behind, where they cannot see it coming and cannot reach you — the highest-percentage finish in the sport and the reason back control is worth more than mount.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) |
| **Comes from** | Back control, turtle, technical mount |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** mata leão, sleeper, RNC

---

## 1. The mechanism

The rear naked choke closes both **common carotid arteries** at once. Your forearm
crosses the front of their neck and the crook of your elbow sits centred on the
trachea, so the windpipe drops into the V and takes almost no pressure. What
does take pressure is the soft tissue either side of it: your **radius/forearm**
presses one carotid, your **bicep** presses the other, and your second arm
behind their head stops the head escaping backwards out of the loop. When both
sides shut, cerebral blood flow stops and consciousness goes with it — fast, and
without the long, panicky struggle of an air choke.

That is why the position of the elbow matters more than how hard you squeeze. An
elbow that drifts off-centre turns the choke into a one-sided strangle plus a
face crank: they get uncomfortable, they get angry, and they defend for another
two minutes. The squeeze itself is not a bicep curl. It is a **shoulders-back,
elbows-together, chest-expanding** motion — you shorten the loop by rotating
your whole arm around their neck, while the rear hand drives their skull forward
into the gap. [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) rates it around 45% of finishes
at a recent IBJJF Worlds; it earns that by being a mechanical inevitability once
the arm is in, not by being subtle.

## 2. The standard application

Assume a same-size opponent and established back control with hooks or a body
triangle.

1. Secure the **seatbelt**: one arm over the shoulder (this becomes the choking
   arm), one under the opposite armpit. Chest glued to their spine, your head on
   the choking-arm side of their head.
2. Win the hand fight first. Use the under-armpit hand to pull their far
   shoulder down and back, and use your chest to flatten them toward the mat on
   the choking side.
3. Feed the choking hand under the chin **like a blade — palm down, thumb-side
   leading, fingers straight**. A hooked hand catches on the jaw; a flat hand
   slides.
4. Keep feeding until the **crook of your elbow is centred on their trachea, in
   line with their sternum**. Stop chasing depth after that — deeper is not
   tighter, it is off-centre.
5. Bring the choking hand to the bicep of your other arm. That second hand
   slides behind their head, palm on the back of the skull, fingers pointing at
   their far ear. Do not lace fingers behind the head.
6. Take out slack: pull your choking-side elbow down toward your own hip, drop
   your shoulder blades together, and press your own head against theirs on the
   choking side.
7. Finish: **expand your chest and bring your elbows toward each other while the
   rear hand drives their head forward.** No curling, no yanking.
8. Keep the legs working the whole time. If your hooks or body triangle come off,
   they will spin and the choke evaporates.
9. Release the instant they tap, go limp, or stop defending purposefully.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The RNC has one fixed constraint you
cannot argue with: the loop around their neck is made of your arm, and your arm
is a fixed length. Every adjustment below is a way of either shortening the loop
or making the neck effectively smaller.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | Your arm runs out of length before the hand reaches your bicep; the choke slides up onto the jaw and becomes a face crank | Ride 5–8 cm higher on their back, turn their head away from the choking arm, and switch to a palm-to-shoulder or hand-in-armpit grip to shorten the loop |
| **Thin / long neck** | You over-insert; the elbow travels past centre and the V lands on the side of the neck, closing one carotid only | Stop when the elbow is on the sternum line; use the shortest grip you own and finish with head drive, not depth |
| **Deep chest / barrel torso** | The seatbelt can't close, your chest won't glue, and they turn inside your arms | Grip the seatbelt higher — over-shoulder hand catching your own under-arm forearm at the collarbone, not the sternum |
| **Long arms** | They get two hands on your choking wrist early and peel with real leverage | Hide the choking hand: no exposed fingers or wrist, palm-to-shoulder grip, and attack the hand fight with your legs (body triangle) so both your arms stay free |
| **Short arms** | They can't peel — but they can turtle up tight and hide the chin in a small gap | Use the gift wrap: trap one of their arms across their own body first, which opens the chin gap without any hand fighting |
| **Much heavier than you** | Hooks get stripped, they stand up or sit back onto you, and you lose the position before the choke arrives | Body triangle over hooks, keep your back off the mat and stay hip-to-hip on one side; take the arm before the neck |
| **Much lighter than you** | You over-insert past the neck and your own bulk stops your chest reaching their back | Shorten deliberately: hand to your own opposite shoulder, elbow high, and go slower — it closes faster than you expect |
| **Very flexible / strong chin tuck** | They bury the chin and shrug the shoulder to their ear, filling the gap with their own trapezius | Attack the head, not the gap: rear hand turns their face away first, then insert on the exposed side |
| **Very stiff / heavily muscled** | The neck is armoured, the shoulders shrug up, and there is genuinely no slot under the chin | Convert: trap their near arm up and finish an arm-triangle-style strangle from the back, or hold the body triangle and harvest the arms |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

This is the build that beats most people's RNC, and it beats it for a purely
arithmetic reason: the loop your arm has to make is neck circumference plus
trapezius mass, and by the time your hand should be arriving at your own bicep
it is still somewhere near their far collarbone. What people do instead is slide
the arm up onto the jaw, where there is less circumference — and that is a face
crank, not a strangle.

Five concrete changes, in the order you should try them:

1. **Climb.** Slide your chest up their back until your choking-side armpit is
   above the line of their shoulder — roughly 5–8 cm higher than feels normal.
   From there your arm comes down over the collarbone at a downward angle
   instead of running horizontally into the trapezius shelf. The trap is a wall
   when you attack it sideways and a ramp when you attack it from above.
2. **Turn the head first.** Before the choking hand goes anywhere, put your other
   hand on their forehead or far cheek and rotate their face *away* from the
   choking arm. A turned head lengthens the near side of the neck, opens a real
   gap under the jaw, and puts the far carotid closer to the surface. On a thick
   neck this is worth more than any grip change.
3. **Shorten the grip.** Do not fight for the classic hand-to-bicep. Land your
   choking hand flat on your own **opposite deltoid** (palm-to-shoulder), or
   tuck it into your own **opposite armpit**. Either removes several centimetres
   of required reach, and both still let the second hand get behind the head.
4. **Close from the back, not the front.** Instead of pulling the choking hand
   tighter, pull your **choking-side elbow down toward your own hip** and squeeze
   your shoulder blades together. That rotates the entire arm around the neck
   and shortens the loop from the far end, where there is slack, rather than the
   near end, where there is bone.
5. **Change the wedge.** If the arm will not get under the chin at all, stop
   grinding. Trap their near arm across their own throat (a gift wrap, or simply
   pulling their wrist to their far shoulder) and choke with their own deltoid
   filling one side — the back-control version of the
   [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md). On this build that is usually the faster
   finish anyway.

### Thin or long neck

The failure here is the opposite one and it is much less obvious, because
everything *feels* right. Your arm sinks in easily, you get the grip you were
taught, and then nothing happens for ninety seconds. What has happened is
over-insertion: the crook of your elbow has travelled past the midline and is
sitting on the far side of the neck. One carotid is compressed, the other is in
open air, and they can defend indefinitely.

- Stop the insertion **when the elbow is over the sternum**, not when the arm
  stops moving. Sight down their chest; the point of your elbow should be on
  their centreline.
- Use the shortest grip you have (palm-to-shoulder, or hand behind your own
  head) so that the loop cinches rather than dangles. A long neck inside a big
  loop is slack, and slack is what they escape through.
- Finish with the **rear hand driving the head forward** rather than with a
  squeeze. A long neck has more flexion range, so head drive buys real
  compression that a knee-jerk squeeze does not.
- Expect it to arrive quickly once it is centred. Long, thin necks go out fast.
  Have your release plan ready before you start squeezing.

### Deep chest / barrel torso

Broad, deep ribcages do not stop the choke — they stop the **position** that
feeds the choke. Your seatbelt has a longer path to travel, your under-armpit
hand ends up gripping nothing, and their turn is easier because your chest never
truly glues.

- Take the seatbelt **higher**: the over-shoulder hand catches your own
  under-armpit forearm at the level of their **collarbone**, not the sternum.
  The circumference at the collarbone line is smaller than at the ribs, and the
  higher grip also blocks their chin from dropping.
- Prefer a **body triangle** to hooks. A deep chest usually comes with a deep
  torso, and hooks on a wide body get stripped one at a time.
- Ride the hip: keep one of their shoulders pinned toward the mat rather than
  sitting square behind them. Square back control on a barrel-chested opponent
  is a slow-motion escape.

### Their long arms

Long arms are a defensive weapon: they reach your choking wrist earlier, they
generate more torque when they peel, and they can post far away to build a base
and stand.

- Give them nothing to grab. **No exposed fingers, no exposed wrist.** The
  palm-to-shoulder and hand-in-armpit grips both bury the end of your arm
  where a two-on-one cannot get purchase.
- Make the hand fight two-versus-one in *your* favour by taking the legs out of
  the equation — a body triangle means you never have to spend a hand on hooks
  and can commit both arms upward.
- When they get a real two-on-one on your choking arm, **switch sides**. Their
  two hands are now committed to one side of their own neck; the other side is
  undefended, and the arm you were not using is already over the shoulder.

### Their short arms

Short-armed opponents are poor at peeling and excellent at hiding. They will
curl into a tight ball where the chin sits in a gap your hand cannot enter,
because there is no lever long enough for you to open it from the outside.

- Do not attack the gap. Attack the **arm**: pull one of their wrists across
  their own body and pin it there with your under-armpit hand (a gift wrap).
  Trapping the arm rotates the shoulder and forces the chin off the chest
  without any wrestling for the hand.
- Alternatively, use your **legs**. A body triangle squeeze that compresses the
  ribcage makes them breathe, and a chin comes up when a person needs air.

### Much heavier than you

The choke is not the problem; holding the back is. A heavier opponent will strip
hooks, sit their weight down onto you, and stand up out of turtle with you still
attached.

- **Body triangle over hooks**, and lock it on the side away from their strong
  turn. If your legs will not close around their torso, do not force a bad
  triangle — hooks with active heels and a very high chest connection are safer.
- Stay **hip-to-hip on one side**, never flat on your back underneath them. If
  your spine is on the mat and their spine is on your chest, you are carrying
  their whole weight and every escape they try works.
- Take the seatbelt **before** the choke, and be willing to spend a full minute
  just holding the position and denying the hand fight. Against a heavier
  opponent the RNC is a war of attrition where you have the better trench.
- If they stand with you on their back, either ride it or get off deliberately —
  do not hold a half-finished choke while a bigger person walks you into the
  mat.

### Much lighter than you

Two problems, both caused by scale mismatch. First, your arm goes so far around
a smaller neck that the elbow ends up past centre. Second, your own bulk holds
your chest off their back, so you are attached at the arms only.

- Deliberately **shorten**: hand to your own opposite shoulder, elbow high and
  tight. Treat their neck as a small loop that needs a small arm, not as a
  target for depth.
- Get your chest connected by **riding lower and to the side** rather than
  directly on top — a heavier person perched square on a smaller person's spine
  bounces off every time they move.
- Squeeze less and slower. The margin between "not on" and "unconscious" is
  narrower on a lighter partner, and you will not feel the difference in your
  arms. This is a training-partner safety issue as much as a technical one.

### Very flexible, strong chin tuck

Flexible defenders do not fight your arm; they remove the target. Chin buried,
near shoulder shrugged to the ear, spine rounded — there is no neck to attack.

- Attack the **head position, not the neck**. Rear hand on the far cheek or
  forehead, rotate their face away from your choking arm, then insert into the
  side that opened. A rotated head cannot maintain a deep chin tuck.
- Alternatively, break the tuck from underneath: slide your under-armpit forearm
  up under their jaw as a wedge and use it to lift the chin, then feed the real
  choking arm through the gap it makes.
- Their flexibility also means they will spin inside your legs. Keep the legs
  honest first; a beautiful chin-clearing setup is worthless if they are already
  facing you.

### Very stiff and heavily muscled

Thick deltoids, high traps, a neck that measures the same as their head. This is
the build where the honest answer is that the classic RNC may simply not be
available.

- Use their own muscle: **trap the near arm up across their neck** and strangle
  with their deltoid on one carotid and your forearm on the other. Big shoulders
  make this *easier*, not harder — the same trade the
  [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) makes.
- Hold the **body triangle** and squeeze the ribcage. The atlas lists this as a
  legal, legitimate finish in its own right against some builds, and even when
  it does not finish, it tires them and eventually the chin lifts.
- Take the arms instead of the neck. Armbars and kimuras from back control do
  not care how thick a neck is.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Assume the palm-to-shoulder or hand-in-armpit grip is your
default, not your backup. Ride higher on the back so your arm attacks downward,
and make the head-turn a standard part of your entry rather than a rescue. You
will finish far more people through the gift wrap and the trapped-arm strangle
than through the classic grip.

**Long arms.** Your risk is over-insertion on everyone who is not enormous. Set
a hard rule: the elbow stops on their sternum line. You can also afford grips
nobody else can — hand behind your own head, or a deep bicep grip with the wrist
hidden.

**Small hands or a weak grip.** Never rely on gripping their body or your own
wrist. Palm-to-bicep, palm-to-shoulder and armpit grips are all grip-strength
independent; they hold by geometry, not by squeezing.

**Short legs / narrow hips.** A body triangle around a wide torso may simply not
close. Use hooks with your heels actively digging behind their thighs, keep your
chest connection extremely high, and accept that you will fight the hand battle
one-handed more often — which makes the buried-hand grips even more important.

**Much lighter than your usual partners.** Your back control has to be
technically perfect because you cannot fall back on weight. Stay hip-to-hip,
never flatten out underneath them, and treat the choking arm as something you
insert only once the hand fight is already won — not as the thing that wins it.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You're squeezing hard and their face is red, but nothing is happening | You have an air/face crank, not a strangle — your forearm is on the trachea or the jaw, not either side of it | Re-centre: the crook of your elbow goes on the sternum line, and let the windpipe sit in the V |
| Your hand can't reach your own bicep | The loop is longer than your arm — thick neck, heavy traps, or you're too low on their back | Climb higher on their back and switch to palm-to-shoulder or hand-in-armpit |
| The choke is tight on one side only | Over-insertion; the elbow has gone past their centreline | Back the arm out 3–5 cm until the elbow is on the sternum line |
| They keep peeling your choking hand | Your wrist and fingers are exposed, and you are hand-fighting one-handed because a hand is doing a leg's job | Bury the hand in your own armpit or on your own shoulder; switch hooks to a body triangle so both hands are free |
| They spin and end up in your guard | The legs came off before the arms did — a position problem, not a choke problem | Re-establish hooks/body triangle before you re-insert the arm; hold the seatbelt through the spin |
| Their chin is glued to their chest and there is no gap | You're attacking the gap instead of the head | Rear hand rotates their face away first, or gift-wrap an arm to force the chin up |
| It's on, and they're calmly waiting it out | It probably is not on — a real bilateral carotid strangle does not permit calm | Check elbow centring and head drive before you spend any more effort squeezing |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Two hands on your choking arm, pinned to their chest | Switch sides — attack the neck with the under-armpit arm while their hands are committed |
| Deep chin tuck, no gap | Gift wrap one arm, then strangle with their own shoulder on one carotid |
| They peel the arm and turn in toward you | Armbar from the back on the arm they turned with, or ride to mount as they turn |
| They strip a hook and slide down | Technical mount, then re-take the back or attack the far-side arm |
| They stand up out of turtle with you attached | Ride to a rear body lock and mat-return, or drop off and re-shoot rather than hold a losing choke |
| They defend the arm so well you can't insert | Body triangle squeeze, or harvest the wrists — kimura and armbar are both live from back control |
| Gi only: they beat the arm entirely | Feed the collar — the bow-and-arrow and sliding collar chokes use the same seatbelt |

Feeding *into* the RNC: turtle top, technical mount, arm drag to the back, leg
drag to the back, front headlock to the back. See
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part IX for the full set of roads in.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (before the arm crosses).** This is where the fight is actually
won. Chin down *before* the arm arrives, hands high protecting the gap between
chin and chest, elbows tight to your ribs. Fight the **seatbelt**, not the
choke — if you can strip the over-shoulder arm you have removed the choking arm
from the equation entirely. Get one shoulder to the mat and start sliding your
back down toward their hips.

**Middle defense (the arm is across but the grip is incomplete).** Two hands on
one arm — always two on one, never one on one. Pull the choking arm **down and
across your chest**, not out to the side, and turn your chin *into* the crook of
their elbow so the windpipe, not the carotids, takes the pressure. Simultaneously
slide your shoulders down toward their hips and pin your back to the mat on one
side so they cannot stay behind you. Clear one hook, land in half guard.

**Late defense (grip locked, head trapped).** It is much worse than early
defense, and pretending otherwise is how people get hurt. Once the second hand
is behind your head and the elbow is centred, there is no leverage available to
you and only a few seconds of consciousness. Pulling on a locked RNC does
nothing but spend the oxygen you have left. **Tap.** The atlas is blunt about
this: the tap is a technique, not a failure, and it is the reason you get to
train tomorrow.

Full positional escape detail lives in [back escapes](../escapes/back-escapes.md).

## 8. Drills

**1. Insertion reps (solo-partner, no resistance).** From an established
seatbelt, feed the choking hand under the chin, stop with the elbow on the
sternum line, take the grip, then remove it. **3 sets of 20 per side.** No
squeezing at all. You are training the stopping point, not the finish.

**2. The grip ladder.** With three partners of different neck sizes, finish the
same choke three ways on each: classic hand-to-bicep, palm-to-shoulder,
hand-in-armpit. **Two reps each, both sides.** The point is to feel which grip
each build demands, so that in a live round you pick it in the first second
rather than the tenth.

**3. Hand-fight positional sparring.** Start with the seatbelt secured and a
body triangle on; their hands are already in a good defensive position. Top
player wins by inserting the choking arm past the chin — the finish does not
count. Bottom player wins by clearing a hook or facing up. **90-second rounds,
reset, switch.**

**4. Head-turn entry.** Bottom partner holds a maximal chin tuck and shoulder
shrug and does nothing else. Top partner must rotate the head away with the rear
hand, then insert. **10 reps per side.** This is the single most transferable
drill against thick-necked and flexible defenders.

## 9. Safety

The RNC is a **blood choke**. Done correctly it produces unconsciousness in a
handful of seconds with no warning arc — there is no "getting worse" phase for
your partner to read. Apply it progressively rather than explosively, and
**release the moment they tap, go limp, stop defending, or make any sound that
isn't deliberate.**

If a partner goes out: release everything immediately, roll them onto their
side, keep the airway clear, and let them come round on their own. Do not shake
them, do not sit them up fast, and do not let them keep training that session.
Repeated chokes to unconsciousness are not a party trick and should not be part
of anybody's practice.

Applied correctly this is a strangle and is **legal at every belt in every major
ruleset** — IBJJF gi and no-gi, ADCC, submission-only, MMA. Applied incorrectly,
with the forearm on the jaw and the rear hand levering the head, it becomes a
**neck crank**, which IBJJF bans at every belt (see
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part XII). If your partner is complaining about
their jaw or the back of their neck rather than going quiet, you are doing the
banned version — fix the elbow position or let go.
