# Arm Triangle

> A strangle in which **their own shoulder closes one carotid and your bicep closes the other** — the most reliable choke from mount and side control, and the one that works on necks too thick for anything else.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) |
| **Comes from** | High mount, side control, north–south, half guard top, kesa gatame |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** head-and-arm choke, kata gatame, side choke

---

## 1. The mechanism

Say it precisely, because everything else on this page follows from it:
**their own trapped arm's shoulder compresses the carotid on that side, and your
bicep compresses the carotid on the other side.** Your hands lock behind their
head, but the grip is not the choke — the grip only stops the wedge falling
apart. The compression comes from shrinking the space inside the loop, and the
loop contains their head plus one of their own arms.

That single fact explains why the arm triangle beats builds that beat every other
choke. You are not trying to wrap a thick neck with a limb of fixed length; you
are letting a large deltoid do half the work. Bigger shoulders make the choke
*better*, not worse.

It also explains why **hip position, not grip strength, is the finishing
mechanism**. Once the wedge is assembled, what closes it is your chest dropping,
your elbows drawing toward each other, and — above all — your hips travelling
off to the far side and away from their head, which rotates your shoulder into
their jaw and drives their own arm deeper into their neck. A person squeezing
harder in mount is applying a face compression. A person walking their hips is
applying a strangle.

It is not an air choke. A correctly placed bicep sits on the side of the neck,
not on the trachea. If they are coughing and going purple, you have slipped onto
the windpipe; if they go quiet and calm and then limp, you are on the carotid.

## 2. The standard application

From mount against a same-size opponent.

1. Isolate one arm. Push it across their own face — off a failed americana, a
   frame they gave you, or a punch in MMA — until their upper arm lies across
   their own throat.
2. Drop your head to the mat on the **same side their trapped hand is pointing**,
   ear beside their ear. **Your shoulder pins their trapped bicep against their
   own jaw and neck** — not against the middle of their face.
3. Feed your near arm under their head and neck, and lock a **gable grip**, palm
   to palm. Do not interlace fingers; hand behind the neck, not around it.
4. Take the slack out before you move: elbows in, chest heavy on their trapped
   shoulder.
5. **Come off to the opposite side of their trapped arm** into a very tight side
   control. Your hips must leave the mount — on most bodies you cannot finish
   this from on top.
6. Walk your legs out wide and low, toes driving, both knees off the mat, hips
   sprawled **away from their head**.
7. Squeeze: elbows toward each other, your shoulder driving into their jaw, your
   chest driving into their chest. **The grip does not tighten — your hips do.**
8. If nothing changes, walk your feet toward their hips and drop your chest
   lower, then re-check the trapped arm's height.

A note on step 5: some people do finish arm triangles from mount, usually on
much smaller opponents. On a same-size or larger opponent, **staying in mount is
the single most common reason this choke fails.**

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The arm triangle has a fixed loop —
your two arms plus your chest — and a variable payload: their head plus one arm.
Every adjustment below either shrinks the payload or moves your body so that the
loop closes from a better direction. On a thick neck, the direction is
everything.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | Grip strength cannot reduce a loop set by your own arm length; squeezing achieves nothing | Move your hips. Off to the far side, sprawled away from their head, feet walking toward their hips — every centimetre of hip travel rotates your shoulder into their jaw |
| **Thin neck** | Loop closes with slack left over; they slide the head out or turn in | Drive your locked hands down to the mat behind their head, pinch the elbows, and pull their trapped arm higher past their far ear |
| **Broad shoulders / thick deltoids** | Their shoulder does the near side beautifully, but your bicep now has further to travel on the far side | Get deeper toward their head until your bicep touches their far ear line; drop into the notch behind their trapped shoulder |
| **Short arms** | Their trapped arm doesn't reach past their own head, so the shoulder never wedges | Rotate the arm — thumb toward their own ear — and drive the elbow across with your ribcage rather than pulling the wrist |
| **Long arms** | Their free hand frames into your hip or neck and holds you off the far side | Kill the free hand before you commit: pin it under your knee or step over it |
| **Much heavier than you** | Their bridge lifts you off, and the finish becomes a strength contest you lose | Head on the mat as a third support point, hips low and wide with both knees off the mat, and finish from a north–south-ish angle rather than a side-control one |
| **Much lighter than you** | Closes so fast you crush the face and windpipe instead of the carotids | Bicep on the side of the neck, not the throat; slow the application right down and pin their hips so they can't shrimp |
| **Very flexible / mobile hips** | They turn into you and recover guard through the space your hip travel creates | Block the near hip with your knee as you come off the mount; keep your hips low and glued as you travel |
| **Very stiff, strong shrug** | They shrug the trapped shoulder and armour the carotid | Feed the arm further across before locking and use head pressure to turn their face *away* from the trapped arm |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

This is the build the arm triangle exists for, and also the build that exposes
whether you understand it. The instinct is to squeeze harder. That cannot work:
the loop is made of your two arms locked at a fixed length, and their neck plus
arm does not compress by grip force. What *does* change is the **angle at which
the loop closes**, and that is set by where your hips are.

The specific hip mechanics:

- **Get off the mount, always.** In mount, your arm has to span head plus arm
  horizontally with no help from gravity. Off to the far side, your chest weight
  and the mat replace the centimetres you are missing. On a thick-necked opponent
  this is not a preference, it is a requirement.
- **Aim your hips at roughly 90° to their spine, on the far side of their trapped
  arm**, with both knees off the mat and your toes driving. Your hips should be
  **lower than your shoulders** — the pressure runs downhill into their neck.
- **Walk your feet away from their head.** This is the adjustment that finishes
  thick-necked opponents. Every centimetre your hips travel toward their feet
  rotates your near shoulder deeper into their jaw and drives their own deltoid
  further into their carotid. If it is loose, do not squeeze — walk. If it goes
  slack when you walk, you have gone too far: come back toward their hips and
  drop your chest instead.
- **Turn your own head.** Look at their far hip. That head-turn rotates your
  shoulder mass another 2–3 cm into the notch between their jaw and their trapped
  bicep, and it costs nothing.

Two payload-side adjustments to combine with the hips:

- **Get their trapped elbow past their far ear** before you lock anything. Their
  deltoid should be under their jaw, not resting on their collarbone. Use your
  free hand to push their wrist across; if the arm keeps sliding back, pin the
  wrist with your own head as you feed the grip.
- **Shorten the loop.** A gable grip is the general-purpose lock, but on a thick
  neck **your choking hand cupping your own opposite bicep is shorter** and buys
  you real reach. Never interlace fingers — thick-necked opponents bridge hard
  and fingers break.

If the neck genuinely defeats you even with perfect hips, switch sides of their
head, or thread through the armpit instead with a [D'Arce](./darce.md) — which
buys several centimetres by entering a different hole.

### Thin neck

The loop closes easily and there is slack left inside it. They slide their head
out backwards, or turn into you before you have finished travelling.

- **Compress downward.** Drive your locked hands toward the mat behind their head
  rather than pulling them up into your own chest, and pinch your elbows toward
  each other. Down beats in when there is spare room.
- **Take the payload up.** Pull their trapped arm higher — hand past the far ear,
  elbow across their throat line — so the loop has more inside it, not less.
- **Move faster through the transition.** A thin-necked opponent's escape window
  is the moment you leave the mount; be there for as short a time as possible.
- **Go slowly on the finish.** Thin necks go out very fast under a correct arm
  triangle and the person often cannot tap with either hand.

### Broad shoulders and thick deltoids

Good news and bad news. Their deltoid closes the near carotid harder than a small
one ever could — that half of the choke is free. But your bicep now has to reach
across a wider chest and a wider neck to close the far side, and your chest will
not glue to theirs because their shoulder is in the way.

- **Get deeper toward their head.** Your choking arm's bicep should end up on the
  **far ear line**, not resting on their far shoulder. A bicep parked on a
  deltoid feels tight and compresses nothing.
- **Drop into the notch.** Instead of trying to lie chest-to-chest, aim your own
  chest into the pocket **behind their trapped shoulder** — between their trapped
  arm and their head. That is the only place your weight transmits into the neck
  on this build.
- **Get the trapped arm as straight as possible.** A bent trapped arm on a broad
  opponent takes up loop volume without adding compression; straightening it lays
  the deltoid flat into the neck.

### Their short arms

The wedge depends on their trapped arm crossing past their own head. Short arms
do not reach, so their shoulder sits beside the neck rather than in it, and you
end up choking with your bicep only — which is half a choke.

- **Rotate, don't translate.** Turn their trapped arm so their **thumb points at
  their own ear** (internal rotation). That rolls the shoulder into the neck
  without needing extra reach — exactly the same fix as the
  [triangle](./triangle-choke.md) against short arms.
- **Drive with your ribcage.** Once the arm is across, push their elbow further
  with your own chest and ribs rather than with your hand; your hand is needed
  for the grip.
- **Silver lining:** short-armed opponents cannot post as effectively to bridge,
  so you have more time to get the wedge right. Use it.

### Their long arms

The trapped arm is a gift — long arms wedge deep. The **free** arm is the problem:
it frames into your hip or your neck and holds you off the far side, which is
exactly where you need to be.

- **Kill the free hand before you travel.** Pin it under your knee, trap it
  under your own armpit, or step over it as you come off the mount. Trying to
  finish while a long free arm frames your hip means you never complete the hip
  walk, and the hip walk is the choke.
- **Watch the far-side underhook.** A long free arm can reach around your back and
  underhook as you land in side control, which turns your finish into their
  escape. Land with your near armpit tight to their shoulder to deny it.

### Much heavier than you

They will bridge, and their bridge lifts your whole body. Squeezing harder makes
you an easier passenger.

- **Put your head on the mat.** A head on the mat is a third point of support and
  it is the specific thing that beats a bridge. Ear on the mat beside their ear;
  do not lift it to look at what you are doing.
- **Hips low, knees off the mat, feet wide, toes driving.** A tripod of two feet
  and one head is far harder to bridge than two knees.
- **Ride the bridge to a better angle.** If they lift you, do not fight back to
  side control — travel further, toward **north–south**. From that angle their
  bridge has nothing to lift and your bodyweight falls straight down the line of
  their spine into the wedge.
- **Hook their far leg** with your near foot if they bridge repeatedly. It costs
  nothing and it removes the drive.
- Keep the grip a **gable**. Under a heavy bridge, an S-grip pops and interlaced
  fingers break.

### Much lighter than you

Your own bulk closes the loop easily, which is the risk. The choke arrives before
you feel it, and what you can feel through your own arms is compression of their
face and windpipe rather than their carotids.

- **Check the bicep.** It should be on the **side** of their neck. If it is on
  the front, you have an air choke and a face crush, and you will hurt somebody.
- **Slow everything down.** Apply over seconds, not instantly, and release at the
  first sign of a tap from any limb.
- **Pin the hips.** A lighter opponent's real escape is shrimping out from
  underneath before you finish travelling; your knee on their near hip stops it
  more reliably than your weight does.

### Very flexible / mobile hips

The hip walk that finishes the choke also opens the space they need to turn into
you and recover guard. Flexible opponents exploit that window better than anyone.

- **Block the near hip with your knee** the entire time you are travelling. It is
  the only thing you should be doing with that leg.
- **Stay low through the transition.** People lose this choke by rising onto their
  toes and hands to travel quickly; travel slowly and heavily instead.
- **If they do turn in, take the back.** A defender who turns toward you with one
  arm still trapped across their own neck has given you their back with an arm
  already gone.

### Very stiff with a strong shrug

Heavily muscled defenders shrug the trapped shoulder up and rotate the head into
it, which armours the carotid on that side.

- **Feed the arm further across before you lock.** A shrug can protect a shoulder
  that is beside the neck; it cannot protect one that is already past the
  midline.
- **Turn their face away from the trapped arm** with your own head or your
  shoulder. That stretches the near side of the neck open and defeats the shrug
  geometrically.
- **Be patient with the hip walk.** On this build the choke often arrives 10–15
  seconds after it "should", as their shrug fatigues.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Never attempt to finish from mount. Use the shortest lock you
have — choking hand to your own opposite bicep — get your hips further out to
the far side than feels necessary, and put your head on the mat every time. The
hip walk gives you the reach your arms do not.

**Long arms.** You can afford palm-to-palm and you can afford to be less deep,
which is exactly the trap: check that your bicep is on their **neck** and not
parked on their far shoulder. Long arms produce loops with slack in them.

**Thick arms and a big chest.** Your own bicep fills the loop, which is a real
advantage — but the same bulk can stop you getting deep enough to reach the far
side of the neck. Drop the shoulder into the notch behind their trapped arm
rather than trying to lie flat across them.

**Small hands.** Gable grip only. Never interlace fingers under a bridging
opponent, and treat the S-grip as a temporary hold rather than a finishing lock.

**Much lighter than your partners.** Angle and hip travel replace weight
completely here — this is the choke that scales best across a size gap. Commit to
head-on-the-mat, knees off the mat, and hooking the far leg, and be willing to
travel all the way to north–south to finish.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You're squeezing as hard as you can and nothing happens | You are still in mount, so the loop has no help from gravity or your chest weight | Come off to the far side of their trapped arm and walk your hips away from their head |
| Their face is red and they're coughing | Your bicep is on the trachea, not the carotid | Slide toward their head until your bicep is on the side of the neck; back off the pressure while you fix it |
| It was tight, then it went slack when you moved | You walked your hips too far toward their feet | Come back toward their hips a few centimetres and drop your chest lower |
| They bridge and roll you off | Head off the mat, knees on the mat — a two-point base | Ear on the mat, both knees up, feet wide and driving; hook their far leg |
| Your bicep is on their far shoulder, not their neck | Not deep enough toward their head | Slide up until your bicep touches their far ear line |
| Their trapped arm keeps sliding back out | The elbow never got past their far ear, and there's nothing holding it | Push the wrist across with your free hand and pin it with your own head before you take the grip |
| They turn in and recover guard as you come off mount | No knee blocking the near hip during the transition | Knee to the near hip the whole way across; travel low and heavy |
| Your fingers hurt | You interlaced them | Gable grip. Always |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They turn away from you to relieve the pressure | Take the back — the trapped arm is already useless to them |
| They turn *into* you and drive | Mount, or the far-side kimura on the arm they just committed |
| They push your head off with the free hand | [D'Arce](./darce.md) on the same trapped arm, entering through the armpit |
| They hide the arm and never let you cross it | Americana or straight armlock from mount; the frames they use to hide it are the entries |
| The choke stalls but the position is perfect | Hold it. It is a legitimate pin — they burn energy defending and the tap often comes late |
| They bridge you toward north–south | Go with it and finish from there; the angle is usually better, not worse |
| You lose the grip entirely | Re-mount, or take side control and reset — a failed arm triangle should never cost you position |

The [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), [D'Arce](./darce.md) and
[anaconda](./anaconda.md) are the same head-and-arm wedge assembled from three
different directions. Learn the wedge once and all three become the same choke
with different entries.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (before your arm crosses your face).** Almost the whole game.
Never let an arm get pushed across your own throat — keep your elbows inside,
frame with the near forearm on their hip, and if an arm does start to travel,
fight it back before their head comes down. The specific rule: **your elbow
belongs on your side of your own centreline.**

**Middle defense (arm is across, they are still in mount).** You have time here,
and knowing that is worth a great deal — **most people cannot finish an arm
triangle from mount.** Use it. Bridge toward the trapped-arm side, get your
trapped shoulder off the mat, and turn into them. Simultaneously walk your own
hips away from the side they want to travel to, and put your free hand on their
near hip to stop the journey. If you can get your trapped hand's thumb pointing
at your own ear and drive that elbow back across your centreline, the wedge is
gone.

**Late defense (they are off to the side, hips out, head on the mat).** Much
worse than early defense, and the deterioration is sudden — the moment their hips
land on the far side, your window closes. From there the only real answers are
turning into them very hard (which usually gives up your back) and the tap. The
arm triangle is also the choke people hold longest, because the defender can
still breathe and does not panic, so the warning you get is minimal. **Tap.**

## 8. Drills

**1. The hip walk.** Partner lies still with the wedge already assembled and you
already off to the side. Without changing your grip at all, walk your feet toward
their head and back toward their hips, and have them tell you when it tightens
and when it slacks. **3 sets of 60 seconds per side.** The goal is to find *your*
optimal hip line and remember it.

**2. Mount-to-side transition.** From the locked wedge in mount, come off to the
far side ten times in a row, with your partner giving light resistance and trying
to turn in. **10 reps per side.** Knee blocks the near hip every time.

**3. Three necks, one wedge.** Assemble and finish on three partners of clearly
different neck thickness. On the thickest, do it twice: once with a gable grip
and once with hand-to-your-own-bicep. **Two reps each.** You are learning which
lock each build needs.

**4. Positional sparring from the wedge.** Start with the arm across and the grip
locked, but you are still in mount. Top player wins by finishing; bottom player
wins by clearing the arm or escaping. **90-second rounds, reset, switch.** This
is also the best arm-triangle *defense* drill in existence.

## 9. Safety

A blood choke, and **legal at every belt in every major ruleset** — IBJJF gi and
no-gi, ADCC, submission-only, MMA. There is no crank variant to worry about the
way there is with the guillotine, but there are two real risks.

First, **it is the choke people hold too long.** Because the defender can breathe
freely and never panics, the outside signs that a strangle is landing are muted.
Release the instant they tap, go limp, or stop defending purposefully — do not
wait for drama.

Second, **the face**. A misplaced arm triangle crushes the nose, jaw and windpipe
rather than the carotids. That is uncomfortable, occasionally injurious, and
never the point. If your partner is coughing or their face is going purple rather
than pale, reposition or release.

If a partner goes out: release everything, roll them onto their side, keep the
airway clear, and let them come round on their own. They do not train again that
session.
