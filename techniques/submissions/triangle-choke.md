# Triangle Choke

> A strangle built out of your legs and *their own shoulder* — the reason a bottom position is an attacking position, and one of the two or three most common finishes at every level of the sport.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, high guard, collar-sleeve and spider guard, mount (mounted triangle), top and side (reverse / side triangle), a failed armbar |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** sankaku jime, triangle, front triangle

---

## 1. The mechanism

The triangle closes both **common carotid arteries**, and only one of them is
closed by you. Your near thigh — specifically the hamstring and the back of the
knee, not the kneecap — presses one carotid. The other carotid is closed by
**their own trapped shoulder**, which your other leg drives across into the side
of their neck when it presses down on your shin or ankle. That is the whole
trick, and it is why the answer to "my triangle isn't tight" is almost never
"squeeze harder."

Three consequences fall straight out of the mechanism:

- **One arm in, one arm out is mandatory.** Both arms out and there is nothing
  filling the far side, so you have a slow neck crank. Both arms in and there is
  no neck contact at all — you have a body triangle over the head, which
  controls but does not strangle.
- **The trapped arm must cross their own centreline.** If their trapped elbow is
  still on their own side of their body, the shoulder is beside the neck rather
  than in it.
- **Angle beats squeeze.** Your calf needs to lie across the **back of their
  neck**, which is narrow and soft. Square-on, it lands on the base of the skull,
  which is wide and hard, and no amount of adductor strength compresses bone.

---

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard against a same-size opponent.

1. Break their posture and control one wrist. Establish one arm in, one arm out —
   either push their far arm inside your knee, or pull their near arm across and
   let the far one stay outside.
2. Open your guard and **climb your hips up their torso** — the near foot steps
   on their hip, the far leg shoots over the shoulder. **The triangle is a hip
   movement, not a leg throw.** Your hips must leave the mat.
3. Shoot the leg over the shoulder and immediately clamp it down; catch your own
   shin with the opposite hand so it cannot be posted away.
4. Lock the figure-four: the back of your near knee over your far ankle. Do not
   squeeze yet — locking and finishing are separate jobs.
5. **Pull their trapped arm hard across your centreline** so their trapped hand
   ends up past your opposite armpit. This is the step people skip and the reason
   most triangles are loose.
6. **Angle off 30–45° toward the trapped-arm side.** Pull on your own top shin
   and walk your bottom shoulder around until your calf crosses the back of their
   neck rather than the base of the skull.
7. Pull their head down with your free hand (or both hands on your own top shin),
   and curl your top leg down.
8. Finish: knees pinched toward each other, hips elevated, head pulled forward.
   The squeeze is the last 10% of the work.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The triangle is the most build-sensitive
submission in the standard curriculum, because the gap your legs must close is
*their neck plus their shoulder* — and both of those vary by a factor of two
between real human beings.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad shoulders** | Your ankle never reaches the back of your knee; the figure-four locks on the foot or not at all | Pull the trapped arm across your centreline so *their* shoulder fills the gap; angle 30–45° to the trapped-arm side; if it still won't lock, grip your own shin instead of the ankle |
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | Your calf sits on the trapezius shelf and never reaches the carotid | Walk the hamstring higher until your calf touches their **far ear**, not their far shoulder; turn their head away from the trapped arm |
| **Thin neck** | The loop has slack; they slip the head out backwards | Lock deeper — cross at the mid-shin rather than the ankle — and finish with the head pull, not the knee squeeze |
| **Short arms** | Pulling the wrist across doesn't move the shoulder far enough; the elbow, not the deltoid, ends up in the gap | Grip above their elbow, not the wrist, and rotate the arm so their thumb points at their own ear |
| **Long arms** | They post the trapped hand and build a frame you can't collapse; the free hand reaches your top foot | Strip the post immediately by pulling the elbow across; hide your top foot inside your own knee pit |
| **Much heavier than you** | The stack — their weight folds your knees into your face and your hip elevation is outmatched | Get the angle *before* you lock; control their posting arm or far shoulder; finish with the head pull, never the squeeze |
| **Much lighter than you** | Your legs go so far around that the calf lands on their far shoulder; loop is loose | Cross deeper, pin the trapped wrist to your own chest, and elevate your hips to take their base off the mat |
| **Very flexible / stacks well** | They fold into the stack and their spine takes it; your knees end up on your own chest | Angle early and roll to your side so their drive goes past you rather than into you; hook their far leg to kill the forward drive |
| **Very stiff, heavily muscled** | Thick deltoid actually helps you, but their lat strength rips the trapped arm free | Pin the trapped wrist against your own chest with a cross-grip before you finish; expect the tap from shoulder pressure, not just the neck |

### Broad shoulders

This is the archetypal triangle problem and the clearest illustration of the
mechanism. The standard instruction is "lock the figure-four and squeeze," and
on a broad-shouldered opponent it simply cannot execute: the gap your legs must
span is neck width **plus** deltoid depth, and your legs run out of length. You
end up locking on your own foot, or on nothing, and the squeeze transmits into
your own ankle rather than their neck.

Three adjustments, applied in this order:

1. **Pull their trapped arm hard across your centreline.** Their trapped hand
   should finish *past your opposite armpit* — not resting on your ribs on the
   same side. Once the arm is there, their own shoulder is occupying the space
   your leg was failing to reach, and the far carotid is being closed by their
   own body. This changes the arithmetic more than anything else you can do.
2. **Angle 30–45° off-centre toward the trapped-arm side.** Square on, your calf
   lies across the base of their skull, which on a broad-shouldered opponent is
   the widest and hardest line you could pick. Rotating toward the trapped-arm
   side drops your calf onto the **back of the neck**, which is several
   centimetres narrower and compressible. Get the angle by pulling on your own
   top shin and walking your bottom shoulder around, not by kicking your leg
   further over.
3. **If your legs still will not lock, grip your own shin.** This is a legitimate
   finishing position, not a failure state. Two hands on your own top shin,
   pulled down toward your opposite hip, plus hip elevation, produces a tighter
   strangle on a broad opponent than a barely-closed figure-four does. Many
   people with average legs finish every triangle they ever hit this way.

Two supporting details worth the effort: **curl your own shoulders off the mat**
toward the trapped-arm side — a hard crunch brings the back of your knee 5–8 cm
closer to their neck, which is often the entire deficit. And **elevate your
hips** onto their shoulder rather than lying flat; hip elevation converts the
leftover gap into downward pressure through their own trapped shoulder.

If none of it closes, this build is a natural switch to the
[arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) from top, or to the omoplata — do not spend
three minutes squeezing a triangle that geometry has already refused.

### Thick neck and heavy traps

Related to broad shoulders but a different failure. Here the legs may lock fine;
the problem is that your calf comes to rest on the trapezius, which is a muscular
shelf that sits *above and lateral to* the carotid. You get a lot of pressure on
their shoulders and none on their blood supply.

- **Aim for the ear.** The cue that fixes this is spatial: your calf should end
  up touching their **far ear**, not their far shoulder. Before you lock the
  figure-four, actively walk your hamstring higher up the back of their neck —
  hands on your own shin, pull it toward your opposite hip, and shuffle it up
  two or three times.
- **Turn their head away from the trapped arm** with your free hand under their
  jaw. This stretches the near side of the neck, lifts the carotid out from
  under the trap mass, and makes the same leg position bite where it previously
  slid.
- **The trapped arm matters more on this build than any other.** With a thick
  neck, your leg is going to under-deliver no matter what; their own shoulder has
  to carry most of the strangle. Pull the arm across, then keep pulling — treat
  it as a live grip you maintain, not a step you completed.
- **Drive the hips up, not just in.** Hip elevation presses their neck up into
  your hamstring and their own shoulder into their carotid simultaneously. Flat
  on your back, you have neither.

### Thin neck

Thin necks lock easily and finish badly, because everything is loose. Your legs
close with room to spare, which sounds good and means the loop is bigger than the
target inside it. They slide the head backwards out of the loop, or simply
posture up through the slack.

- **Cross deeper.** Instead of putting the back of your knee over their ankle,
  cross at the **mid-shin or upper calf**. A deeper cross makes a smaller loop.
- **Finish with the head pull.** On a thin neck, pulling their skull toward your
  chest with both hands compresses far more effectively than pinching your
  knees. Two hands on the back of the head, elbows in, curl up.
- **Expect it to arrive fast.** Thin-necked partners go out quickly and often
  without the usual scramble. Apply progressively and be ready to release.

### Their short arms

The trapped-arm mechanic assumes you can drag the arm across far enough that the
*shoulder* rotates into their own neck. With short arms, you pull the wrist
across your body and the elbow — not the deltoid — is what ends up in the gap.
An elbow is narrow, bony and does not fill anything.

- **Grip above the elbow, not at the wrist.** Take the tricep and drive the whole
  upper arm across; the wrist follows, but the wrist was never the point.
- **Rotate rather than translate.** Turn their arm so their **thumb points at
  their own ear** (internally rotate). That rolls the shoulder into the neck
  without requiring any additional reach, which is exactly what a short arm
  cannot give you.
- **Scoop from underneath.** Your free hand goes under their trapped elbow and
  lifts it across, rather than pulling their forearm over the top. The lift
  rotates the shoulder; the pull just straightens the arm.

### Their long arms

Long arms are a defensive asset in the triangle for two reasons: the trapped hand
can post far away on the mat and build a frame you cannot collapse, and the free
hand can reach past your hip to strip your top foot.

- **Strip the post before you lock.** The moment the leg goes over, pull the
  trapped elbow across; do not wait until the figure-four is set. A posted long
  arm gives them a straight line from the mat into your hip, and that line
  becomes a stack.
- **Trap the wrist against your own chest** with a cross-grip. Their arm is long
  enough to escape if it has anywhere to go; give it nowhere.
- **Hide your top foot.** Thread it into your own knee pit rather than leaving
  the shin and ankle out where a long free arm can reach and peel it.
- Their length also works for you: once the elbow is across, a long arm buries
  the shoulder deeper into their neck than a short one ever will. The fight is
  entirely about getting it there.

### Much heavier than you

The problem is the stack. Their weight, plus gravity, plus a posted arm, folds
your knees toward your face. Hip elevation is your only lever and against a
significant weight difference it loses.

- **Angle before you lock, not after.** A stack applied to a square triangle
  folds you in half. A stack applied to a triangle that is already 45° off-centre
  rolls you onto your side, where their weight goes *past* you instead of *into*
  you. This is the single most important adjustment against a bigger opponent
  and it must happen early — once they are driving, you will not get the angle.
- **Control the posting arm.** Overhook their free arm, or grip their far
  shoulder and pull it toward you. A person who cannot post cannot stack.
- **Hook their far leg with your free foot.** It costs you nothing and it removes
  the drive from their legs, which is where a stack actually comes from.
- **Finish with the pull, not the squeeze.** Two hands on your own top shin,
  trapped arm across the centreline, head pulled down. Squeezing your adductors
  against a bigger person's neck is a strength contest you chose to enter.
- **Have an exit.** If the stack arrives and your hips are on the mat, release to
  an armbar or omoplata rather than holding a triangle you are about to get
  passed out of. Losing the position is worse than losing the submission.

### Much lighter than you

The mirror problem. Your legs go so far around that the calf lands on the far
*shoulder* rather than the neck, and the whole loop is slack. Meanwhile they can
often just stand up out of it.

- **Cross deeper** — mid-shin or upper calf — and consciously *under-rotate*.
  With a smaller opponent you do not need the full leg-over commitment.
- **Pin the trapped wrist to your own chest.** Smaller opponents escape triangles
  by extracting the arm far more often than by posturing out.
- **Elevate their base off the mat.** Against a much lighter opponent your hips
  can genuinely lift them; once their knees leave the ground they have no base
  to posture with, and the finish becomes trivial.
- **Slow down.** A tight triangle on a smaller partner arrives faster than your
  hands can register.

### Very flexible / good stacking posture

Flexible defenders survive stacking positions that would fold most people, which
means "hold on and out-pressure them" is not a plan. They also shrug the trapped
shoulder and rotate the head to buy carotid space.

- **Kill the shoulder shrug.** Clamp the trapped wrist across your chest so the
  shoulder cannot retract. A shrug only works if the arm has somewhere to
  retreat to.
- **Get to your side.** Roll toward the trapped-arm side as they stack so their
  compression passes beside you. A flexible opponent can out-fold you; they
  cannot out-fold a target that is not in front of them.
- **Switch rather than grind.** Against a flexible stacker, the omoplata and the
  armbar are usually closer than the triangle finish, and the triangle already
  did the work of trapping the arm.

### Very stiff and heavily muscled

Big deltoids are good news for the mechanism — more meat gets driven into their
own neck. The problem is elsewhere: heavy lats and a strong shoulder girdle mean
they can rip the trapped arm out, and their thickness may genuinely exceed your
leg length (see broad shoulders).

- **Own the wrist first.** Cross-grip the trapped wrist to your own chest before
  you spend any effort on the legs.
- **Expect the shoulder tap.** On this build the pressure on the trapped shoulder
  often becomes intolerable before the strangle lands. That is a legitimate
  finish, but it means you should be watching for the tap earlier than usual.
- **Use the angle, not the pinch.** Adductors versus a heavily muscled neck is a
  contest you lose; geometry is not.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short legs.** Treat "grip your own shin" as your **primary** finish, not your
backup — a shin grip plus a hard angle plus the trapped arm across the centreline
beats a barely-locked figure-four every time. Get the angle earlier and take more
of it (closer to 45° than 30°). Also lean on the versions where gravity helps:
the mounted triangle and the reverse/side triangle from the top let your body
weight replace the leg length you do not have.

**Long legs.** Your risk is over-rotation — the calf sails past the neck and
lands on the far shoulder, where it feels locked and does nothing. Cross deeper
and check the contact point: calf on the back of the neck, touching their far
ear.

**Thick thighs.** Your own leg mass can block the lock. Cross at the mid-shin
rather than the ankle, and use the shin grip. On the plus side, thick hamstrings
deliver far more compression per centimetre of contact than thin ones, so once
it is on, it is on.

**Stiff hips.** Do not try to get the angle by throwing your leg further over —
your hips will not allow it and the attempt costs you the position. Get the angle
by **walking your bottom shoulder** around on the mat and pulling on your own top
shin. Also spend your setup effort on getting the hips off the mat and climbing
up their torso before the leg goes over.

**Much lighter than your partners.** Angle and the trapped arm are everything;
the leg squeeze is decoration. Also, commit to controlling their posting arm
early — every triangle you lose to a bigger person, you lose to a stack, and
every stack starts with a post.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The lock is on but nothing is happening | Their trapped arm has not crossed your centreline — one carotid is completely open | Pull the trapped elbow across until their hand is past your opposite armpit, and keep holding it |
| Your ankle won't reach the back of your knee | Their shoulders are wider than your legs are long | Grip your own shin instead, curl your shoulders up, and elevate your hips |
| Their face goes red, they cough, nothing else | Your leg is on the front of the throat / windpipe, not the sides of the neck | Angle further toward the trapped-arm side so your hamstring crosses the neck, not the throat |
| It feels tight but they posture up out of it | Your calf is on the base of their skull, not the back of the neck — no compression, easy lever | Angle 30–45° toward the trapped arm, then re-clamp |
| They're stacking you and your knees are on your face | You locked square and they got to post | Roll to your side toward the trapped arm; kill the post; if you're already folded, bail to armbar or omoplata |
| The trapped arm slides out | You held the wrist, not the elbow, and their shoulder was free to retract | Cross-grip the wrist to your own chest and grip above the elbow when you drag |
| Both their arms are inside | This isn't a triangle — you have a body triangle over the head | Push one arm out with your knee and re-lock, or use it as a control position and hunt the sweep |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They posture up hard and straighten the trapped arm | Armbar on the trapped arm — it is already isolated and extended |
| They stack you and drive forward | Omoplata as you roll out, or spin under to take the back |
| They pull the trapped arm free | Re-guard, or switch the triangle to the other side using the arm they just committed |
| They hide both arms in | Body triangle over the head — hold it, hunt the sweep, or push one arm out and re-lock |
| They stand up to escape | Ankle pick or tripod sweep — a standing person in a half-triangle has no base |
| They grip your top foot and peel | Switch to the shin grip and finish, or transition to the mounted triangle if you can get on top |
| They defend perfectly and posture | Kimura on the free arm, or return to closed guard and reset the grip fight |

The classic chain from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Tier 6 is
**armbar ↔ triangle ↔ omoplata**. All three attack the same isolated arm from
the same guard; if you own the chain, their defense to any one of them is the
entry to the next.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (the leg is over but the lock is not set).** This is nearly all
of the defense. The instant a leg crosses your shoulder: **posture up**, drive
your hips back, and get the trapped arm's elbow to the mat on the far side of
your own body with the thumb up. Put your free hand on their hip and drive it
away. Critically, **do not let them angle** — as long as you stay square to them,
their calf is on the base of your skull and nothing is compressing.

**Middle defense (locked, but you still have posture).** Get your trapped
shoulder driving forward across their centreline — you want your own trapped
elbow on the far side of *their* body, not yours. Grip your own bicep or your
own collar to protect the neck, stand up, and stack: walk toward their
**trapped-arm side** to unwind the angle they built. Then drive their top knee
across your body and step out. The atlas rates this "posture and stack" defense
at Diff 3 / Com 5 — it is a core skill, not a niche one.

**Late defense (angled, arm across, head pulled down).** Far worse than early
defense, and the gap between the two is bigger for the triangle than for almost
any other submission. Once the angle is there and their trapped arm is across
your centreline, both carotids are already shut and you have seconds, not
minutes. Struggling accelerates it. **Tap.** Trying to power out of a finished
triangle is how people wake up on the mat.

One extra note: in MMA and self-defense contexts the historical escape from a
late triangle is a slam. Slams are banned in IBJJF at every belt and in most
grappling rulesets — do not treat "I could have slammed out" as a defense in a
sport context, and do not put yourself in a position where a partner feels the
need to try it.

## 8. Drills

**1. The angle drill.** Partner kneels in your open guard, one arm in, one arm
out, no resistance. Lock a square triangle, then — without unlocking — walk your
shoulders and pull on your own shin until you are 45° off-centre. Feel it go from
loose to lethal. **10 reps per side, no finish.**

**2. Three finishes on three bodies.** With three partners of noticeably
different shoulder width, finish the same triangle three ways on each: full
figure-four, shin grip, and shin grip plus hip elevation. **Two reps each.** The
outcome you want is knowing, within one second of locking up, which finish this
particular body demands.

**3. Trapped-arm positional sparring.** Start with the triangle locked but the
trapped arm *not yet* pulled across. Bottom player's only goal is to get the arm
past their opposite armpit; top player's only goal is to keep the elbow out and
posture. **45-second rounds.** This isolates the step that decides most triangles.

**4. Stack survival.** Start locked, with the top player already posted and
driving. Bottom player practises rolling to their side toward the trapped-arm
side and bailing to the omoplata when it fails. **90-second rounds, reset.**
Trains both the escape from the stack and the discipline of leaving on time.

## 9. Safety

The triangle is a **blood choke** and legal at every belt in every major ruleset.
Done correctly it strangles; done with both of their arms outside your legs it
becomes a **neck crank** on the cervical spine, which IBJJF bans at every belt
and which can hurt people badly. If you have both arms out, let go and re-set —
you do not have a submission, you have an injury waiting.

Apply progressively. The strangle arrives faster than most beginners expect,
particularly on thin necks, and the recipient often cannot tap with the trapped
arm — **watch for a tap with the free hand, a foot tap, or a verbal tap**, and
release on any of them. If a partner goes quiet, release first and ask
afterwards.

For the person defending: stacking loads the defender's neck under their own
body weight. If you are being stacked and you feel compression in your neck
rather than your legs, that is a reason to tap, not a reason to push harder.
