# Straight Ankle Lock

> Their foot trapped in your armpit, the blade of your forearm under the Achilles, your hips bridging — the first leg lock anyone should learn and the only one that is legal at every IBJJF belt.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Leg lock — ankle extension plus Achilles compression |
| **Comes from** | Outside ashi garami, single-leg X, 50/50, seated/butterfly guard as they stand |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | Every IBJJF belt, gi and no-gi. The only leg lock with no belt restriction. |

**Also known as:** Achilles lock, footlock, botinha, ashi garami finish

*Ratings match [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md). One honest caveat on the 2/5: that
rating describes the finish, which really is simple. Entering the position
without donating a guard pass is closer to a 3, and choosing the right version
against an unusual leg — which is what section 3 is about — is a 3 as well.*

---

## 1. The mechanism

Two things happen at once, and knowing which one is doing the work is the whole
skill.

**Joint extension.** Your armpit and your grip hold their foot; the blade of your
forearm sits under the leg just above the heel as a fulcrum; your bridge drives
their tibia forward over that fulcrum. The talocrural joint — the ankle hinge —
runs out of plantarflexion, and the anterior capsule and the tissue across the
front of the ankle take the load.

**Tendon compression.** At the same time, the Achilles tendon and everything over
it is crushed against the hard edge of your radius. On most people this is what
actually produces the tap. It hurts long before the joint is anywhere near
danger, which is a large part of why this lock is safe enough to be legal at
white belt.

Those two pain generators are not equally available on every body. Padding kills
the compression; a hypermobile ankle kills the extension. Every adjustment in
section 3 is a decision about which of the two you are going to finish with.

One more thing about the mechanism: the load does not have to stop at the ankle.
If the foot rotates while you extend, the torque runs straight up the tibia into
the knee — that is the mechanism of a heel hook, applied by accident, without any
of the control that makes a deliberate heel hook survivable. Keep the foot
square.

---

## 2. The standard application

From outside ashi garami, against a partner your size.

1. **Enter outside ashi.** Their leg between yours; your outside foot on their
   hip (on the hip bone, not the thigh — you need it to push); your inside knee
   pinched hard against the outside of their thigh. **Their knee stays outside
   the line of your body. The moment it crosses your centreline you are reaping**,
   which is illegal in the gi at every belt and legal in no-gi only for adult
   brown and black belts.
2. **Feed the foot deep.** Slide your arm along the outside of their calf and
   pull their foot into your armpit until the crease of your elbow sits behind
   their heel and their toes are past your shoulder line.
3. **Set the blade.** Rotate your forearm so the bony thumb-side edge — the
   radius — is what touches the tendon, not the flat, meaty underside of your
   forearm. **This single rotation is worth more than any amount of squeezing.**
4. **Close the grip.** Palm-to-palm on your own wrist for mobility, or figure-four
   (gripping hand to your own bicep) for a stronger, less adjustable version.
5. **Trap the foot.** Roll your shoulder forward and drop your chin onto their
   toes so the foot cannot rotate or slide out. A foot that can rotate leaks
   every bit of force you generate.
6. **Kill their base.** Push their hip away with your outside foot and pinch
   their thigh with your inside knee so their femur cannot retreat and they
   cannot sit up into you.
7. **Deal with the free leg.** Their other leg is the engine of every escape.
   Hook it, push the knee away with your free hand, or trap it — before you
   finish, not after they start escaping.
8. **Finish with three vectors at once:** extend your hips into a bridge, pull
   your elbow down toward your own near hip, and lean your shoulders back.
   **Do not curl your arm.** The arm is a hook; the hips and the shoulders are
   the motor.
9. Apply over three to four seconds and release the instant they tap.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

This is the section that matters. The straight ankle lock has more body-type
sensitivity than any other beginner-legal submission in the sport, because you
are attacking a small joint through a variable amount of padding with a fixed
piece of your own anatomy.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick, muscular calves, high-set Achilles** | The calf belly pads the tendon; your blade lies on muscle, and muscle is a spring | Slide the forearm 1–2 in (3–5 cm) lower onto the tendon itself, rotate to the radius edge, finish with a hip bridge plus a shoulder-back lean instead of an arm curl |
| **Skinny calves, prominent tendon** | The blade bites instantly — and your arm slides up the leg under load | Anchor the heel in the elbow crease first, pinch elbow to ribs, figure-four the grip, then apply over 3–4 seconds |
| **High, rigid arches** | The instep ridge props your forearm forward onto the lower tibia, where compression does nothing | Walk the blade down until it drops into the hollow above the heel; then use the ridge as the stop that keeps the foot in |
| **Flat, flexible feet** | No instep ridge — the foot squirts out of the armpit as you bridge, and the joint has extra range | Rotate your chest 20–30° toward the foot so the armpit pocket runs diagonally; figure-four so your wrist blocks the instep; chin on the toes |
| **Big feet** | The foot bottoms out on your shoulder before the heel reaches your elbow crease | Feed the foot diagonally across your chest toward your far shoulder; shorten the grip to a wrist grab; accept a lower blade and add hips |
| **Small feet** | Nothing fills the pocket; the foot slides through and the blade rides up | Collapse the pocket — elbow to ribs, shoulder rolled forward, figure-four grip; or change locks, because the heel is a better handle than the tendon here |
| **Hypermobile ankles** | The joint may never reach end range — there is no extension pain to find | Win on pressure, not range: radius edge, blade on the tendon insertion, slow hip finish. If it is not there, switch locks rather than adding rotation |
| **Very stiff ankles** | End range arrives in the first inch of your bridge | Halve your speed; expect the tap before you think you have started |
| **Long legs** | Your bridge pulls them toward you instead of loading the ankle | Straighten the hip-post leg first until the leg is a rigid rod, then line your hip up with theirs before bridging |
| **Short, stocky legs** | Their hip arrives with your leg still bent — no room to extend | Switch to single-leg X so your feet frame from a shorter base, or drop the outside foot to their far hip |
| **Much heavier than you** | Your hips cannot rise under their weight, so the bridge is dead | Change the lever from vertical to rotational: rigid arm, rotate your whole torso away and drop your outside shoulder back |
| **Much lighter than you** | They rotate out before it loads, and their joint reaches end range early | Control the free leg before the grip closes; then finish at a third of your normal speed |

### Thick, muscular calves and a high-set Achilles

This is the build that beats the textbook version most often, and it is worth
understanding in detail because the same reasoning transfers everywhere else.

On a heavily muscled lower leg, the tendon is not where you think it is. The
gastrocnemius belly sits low and wide, and the Achilles does not narrow into a
clean cord until it is very close to the heel. Your forearm, placed where it
works on an average leg, is lying on muscle. You bridge; the muscle deforms;
some of the force disappears into tissue; the joint never gets to end range and
the tendon never gets pinched. The lock feels tight to you and merely
uncomfortable to them.

Three changes, in order:

1. **Slide your forearm one to two inches (3–5 cm) lower**, toward the heel,
   until the blade sits on the tendon itself rather than the calf belly. Find it
   with your fingers *before* you close your grip — walk your fingertips down the
   back of the leg until the soft mass gives way to a hard cord, and put your
   forearm there.
2. **Rotate your wrist about 45°** so the radius — the bone on the thumb side of
   your forearm — makes contact rather than the flat of the forearm. Pressure is
   force divided by area; this turns a contact patch the width of your palm into
   a line. On a padded leg it is often the difference between a tap and nothing.
3. **Change the finish.** The arm curl is dead here — you cannot out-curl a calf
   that size. Keep the arm rigid and finish with a hip bridge *plus* a
   shoulder-back lean. The extra range those two body movements produce is
   exactly what clears the padding; an arm curl gives you maybe two inches of
   travel, a bridge and lean gives you six.

Two consequences of the lower blade position. First, your elbow crease is now
behind the heel and less of the foot is in your armpit, so re-check the trap —
roll the shoulder forward and put your chin on the toes or the whole thing
slides out under load. Second, you are now finishing with joint extension rather
than tendon pain, which means the warning period is shorter. Bridge in one slow
continuous motion, not a pump.

### Skinny calves and a prominent tendon

The mirror problem. The tendon is right there under a thin layer of skin, so the
blade bites immediately — but the lock has two specific failure modes anyway.

The first is that with nothing filling the space, your forearm slides *up* the
leg under load and you end up compressing the mid-calf or the lower shin, which
does nothing at all. Fix it structurally, not with grip strength: pull the heel
firmly into the crease of your elbow before you apply any pressure, pinch your
elbow tight into your own ribs so the arm cannot travel, and take a figure-four
grip rather than palm-to-palm — the figure-four cannot lengthen, the Gable grip
can.

The second is that a thin, hard tendon produces a very early tap from pain
alone. Do not read that early tap as evidence the lock was tight; on a tougher
opponent the same lock will fail. And a lighter, thinner leg reaches joint end
range sooner too, so both the pain and the danger arrive early. Apply over three
to four seconds regardless of how easy it feels.

### High, rigid arches

High arches change this lock more than most people realise, and they cut both
ways.

The gift: a high-arched foot has a pronounced instep ridge just forward of the
ankle. Seat that ridge against the front of your shoulder and it acts as a
mechanical stop — the foot cannot slide out of your armpit no matter how hard
you bridge. Against this build you can be more committed with the finish than
usual.

The trap: the same ridge props your forearm *forward*, so your blade ends up an
inch above the tendon, on the lower tibia. That feels tight — bone on bone
always does — but the tibia does not care, and the ankle joint is not being
extended either. Before you close the grip, walk the blade down until you feel
it drop off the bone into the softer hollow just above the heel.

A rigid foot also transmits force cleanly and usually has less plantarflexion
available, so once the blade is correct the lock arrives fast with little
warning. Halve your normal speed.

### Flat, flexible feet and low arches

Mechanically the hardest version of this lock, and the one that produces the
most "I had it and it slipped" moments.

Two problems compound. There is no instep ridge, so nothing physically stops the
foot travelling out of your armpit as you bridge — the classic failure is that
the lock tightens for a quarter of a second, the foot pops free, and you are
left holding a shin. And a flexible foot has more plantarflexion available, so
the joint reaches end range late, sometimes not before your bridge is exhausted.

Four adjustments:

- **Change the shape of the pocket.** Rotate your chest 20–30° toward their
  trapped foot so your armpit closes across the top of the foot on a diagonal
  rather than along it. The diagonal pocket is a couple of inches longer and it
  grips the foot in two directions instead of one.
- **Change the grip to a figure-four**, specifically so the wrist of your
  gripping hand lies across the top of their foot and physically blocks it from
  sliding forward. Palm-to-palm gives you no such block.
- **Chin or jaw on the toes.** Cheap, unglamorous, and it is often the entire
  difference.
- **Accept that the finish is compression, not range.** Blade placement and the
  radius edge matter more against this build than against any other, because
  extension is not going to arrive in time.

### Big feet

Pure geometry. A long foot bottoms out against your shoulder before the heel has
travelled far enough back to sit in your elbow crease, so you end up with a
shallow lock and a forearm across the middle of the calf.

- **Feed the foot diagonally.** Instead of driving it straight into the armpit,
  angle it across your chest toward your far shoulder. That diagonal pocket is
  two or three inches longer than the straight one, which is usually the entire
  deficit.
- **Shorten your own chain.** Switch from palm-to-palm to gripping your own
  wrist, or figure-four to your bicep. Both pull your elbow crease back toward
  the heel by removing slack from the arm.
- **Trade blade height for hip range.** The blade will sit lower on the tendon
  than ideal; compensate with a longer, slower bridge and a deeper shoulder-back
  lean rather than trying to fix it with a squeeze.
- **Go slower than you think.** A long foot is a long lever. Every degree of
  your bridge produces more torque at their ankle than the same degree would on
  a small foot.

### Small feet

The opposite: there is not enough foot to fill the pocket, so the foot slides
through the armpit, the blade rides up the calf, and the whole structure has
slack in it.

- Collapse the pocket around the foot: pinch your elbow hard into your own ribs
  and roll your shoulder forward over the top of the foot so the armpit closes
  onto it rather than around it.
- Take the shortest grip available — figure-four, hand to bicep.
- Be honest about tool selection. A small foot is a poor handle for a straight
  ankle lock, but the heel is still a perfectly good one. Where your belt and
  ruleset allow it, the [toe hold](./toe-hold.md) or the
  [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) has far more purchase on this build. The straight
  lock is often simply the wrong choice here.

### Hypermobile ankles

This is the build the straight ankle lock loses to, and you need to recognise it
early rather than grinding away at a lock that is not coming.

Someone with a genuinely hypermobile ankle — a dancer, a swimmer, anyone who can
sit back on their heels with the tops of both feet flat on the mat — may have so
much plantarflexion available that your bridge runs out before their joint does.
There is no extension pain to find. Whatever you are going to get, you are going
to get from compression.

1. **Stop trying to win on range.** Win on pressure: radius edge, blade placed
   precisely, slow steady hip extension so the compression has time to register.
2. **Move the fulcrum lower still** — right down onto the tendon's insertion at
   the top of the heel bone, which is the sharpest, least padded point on the
   whole structure.
3. **Do not add rotation to make it work.** Twisting the foot while you extend
   converts this into a knee attack with none of the position, control or
   legality of a deliberate heel hook. This is the single most common way people
   injure training partners with a "beginner-safe" submission.
4. **Change locks.** A hypermobile ankle usually sits on an ordinary knee. Where
   your belt and ruleset allow, the [kneebar](./kneebar.md) is the honest answer,
   and the entanglement you are already in feeds it.

**The mirror image — very stiff ankles.** Heavy squatters, and almost anyone with
a history of ankle injury, reach end range almost immediately. On them, the
first inch of your bridge is the whole submission. Set everything, then apply at
half your normal speed and expect the tap before you feel like you have begun.

### Long legs

Against a long-legged opponent the lock fails in a specific and diagnosable way:
you bridge, and instead of the ankle loading, their whole body slides toward
you. Your bridge is spending itself on moving a person rather than extending a
joint, because the extra length of their femur and tibia is slack in the system.

- **Take the slack out first.** Straighten your outside leg and drive their hip
  away until the trapped leg feels like a rigid rod running from your armpit to
  your foot. Test it: if you can still pull their hip toward you, you are not
  ready to finish. Only then bridge.
- **Line the hips up.** Move your own hips laterally toward their trapped-leg
  side so that your hip, their hip and their ankle sit on one straight line.
  Off that line, part of your bridge becomes rotation and leaks out at their
  hip joint.
- **Beat their reach.** Long legs come with long arms — they will sit up, grab
  your collar or your head, and start the standard defense before your finish
  arrives. Hook or push the free leg early; the free leg is what powers the
  sit-up.

### Short, stocky legs

The opposite geometry. Their hip arrives at your outside foot while your leg is
still bent, so you have no runway to extend into and every push moves you
instead of them.

- **Change frames.** Move from outside ashi to single-leg X: inside foot deep at
  their hamstring or under their far buttock, outside foot on their far hip. Your
  legs then push in two directions from a shorter base, which is exactly what a
  short, compact opponent takes away from you.
- Alternatively, drop your outside foot from their near hip to their far hip so
  the frame lengthens and you buy back a few inches of extension range.
- Expect them to be strong in a small space. Do not try to win with the bridge
  alone — the shoulder-back lean is where the extra range comes from here.

### Much heavier than you

The bridge is the casualty. Bridging means your hips lift a share of their
bodyweight, and if someone much heavier settles down over your entanglement,
your hips do not rise at all.

- **Change the lever from vertical to rotational.** Keep the arm rigid and rotate
  your entire torso away from their leg, dropping your outside shoulder back
  toward the mat behind you. Their ankle extends over your forearm because you
  rotated, not because you lifted. Rotation is available at any weight ratio;
  a bridge is not.
- **Pull the elbow toward your own near hip** as you rotate, and let your knee
  pinch on their thigh supply the counter-force.
- **Get out from underneath first.** If their mass is sitting on your
  entanglement, walk your hips six inches (15 cm) toward the outside so their
  weight is beside you rather than on top of you before you finish.
- **Know the race you are in.** A much heavier opponent will often stand up and
  rip the foot straight out, because at that weight difference they do not need
  technique. Either finish before they stand or use their stand-up — the
  single-leg-X sweep and the wrestle-up both live right there.

### Much lighter than you

Two separate problems, and the second one matters more.

First, a lighter opponent rotates out of the entanglement almost for free.
There is not enough inertia in their body for your knee pinch to hold, so they
spin, hide the heel, and slide the foot out. The answer is control, not
tightness: secure the free leg, or at minimum push their far knee, before your
grip closes.

Second — and this is a safety issue, not a technical one — a lighter opponent's
ankle reaches end range sooner and their tissue tolerates less. Everything you
would normally do in one second should take three or four. You should be
finishing on the last twenty percent of your available bridge, not the first
twenty. If you find yourself thinking "I have loads of range left," that is
precisely when to stop adding.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your pocket is short, so big feet defeat you sooner than they
  defeat other people. Use the diagonal feed as your default rather than as a
  correction, take a wrist grip instead of a figure-four (a figure-four costs
  four to six inches of reach), and expect to finish with hips more than arms.
- **Long arms.** You have surplus length, which turns into slack. Your habitual
  error will be catching the calf instead of the tendon. Pull the foot deeper
  than feels necessary and pinch the elbow to your ribs to spend the extra
  length on tightness rather than distance.
- **Big chest and lats.** Good news — your armpit pocket is deep and your trap is
  strong. The cost is that a foot seats less easily; rotate your chest toward the
  foot on entry rather than trying to drive it straight in.
- **Small hands or a weak grip.** Stop reaching for the figure-four, which is
  grip-dependent. A palm-to-palm Gable grip needs no finger strength at all, and
  all the tension should be coming from your hips anyway.
- **Short legs.** Your outside foot will not reach a tall opponent's hip. Put it
  on the near hip bone and switch to single-leg X, where your leg length matters
  much less.
- **Lighter than most of your training partners.** You will lose the bridging
  contest most nights. Build your ankle lock around the rotational finish and
  around single-leg X, where your legs, not your hips, generate the force.
- **Stiff hips or limited hip extension.** Common over 40 and after any back
  injury. Your bridge is short, so the shoulder-back lean has to do more work —
  drill the lean as a separate movement until it is worth as much to you as the
  bridge.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Grip is tight, they look mildly annoyed | Your blade is on the calf belly, not the tendon | Slide 1–2 in (3–5 cm) toward the heel until you feel a hard cord; rotate to the radius edge |
| Their foot slides out as you bridge | Nothing is blocking the top of the foot | Chin on the toes, shoulder rolled forward, figure-four so your wrist crosses the instep |
| You bridge and they slide toward you | Slack in the chain — your hip-post leg is bent | Straighten the outside leg and drive their hip away until the leg is rigid, then bridge |
| Your bicep burns and nothing happens | You are curling the arm | Arm rigid; finish with hip extension and a shoulder-back lean |
| They sit up, grab your collar and start peeling | You never dealt with the free leg | Hook the free leg or push their far knee *before* the grip closes |
| Their knee rotates across your body and it slips | They are spinning out — and you may now be reaping | Release, re-enter, re-establish the knee line before re-gripping |
| The heel feels loose in your elbow | Foot fed too shallow; usually a big foot or short arms | Re-feed diagonally across your chest; shorten to a wrist grip |
| Works on some partners, never on others | You only own one version of the finish | Build the compression finish and the extension finish separately — see section 3 |
| Everyone taps instantly | Stiff-ankled partners, or you are applying too fast | Slow down. This is a safety failure, not a sign the lock is good |
| Tight lock, then they stand up and walk out | Much heavier opponent, or no free-leg control | Finish earlier, or take the sweep off their stand-up instead |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They rotate the knee toward you and hide the heel | Follow the rotation into the single-leg-X sweep — or, for adult brown/black in no-gi, the outside [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) they just gave you |
| They point the toes and boot out the tendon pressure | Switch to the [toe hold](./toe-hold.md) (brown/black), which attacks the same foot in the direction the boot does not defend |
| They sit up and grip your head or collar | Release the foot, come up on your elbow, take the sweep or the wrestle-up |
| They pull the foot free and stand | Chase the standing leg — single leg or ankle pick as they retreat |
| They straighten the leg hard and drive their heel past your hip | The knee has come to you — take the [kneebar](./kneebar.md) (brown/black) |
| Their free leg comes over your head | Catch it and take 50/50, or step over into the saddle |
| They flatten you and start passing the knee line | Let the leg go and re-guard. A leg is not worth a pass |

**What feeds into it:** outside ashi off a failed guard pass; single-leg X from
butterfly or seated guard as they stand; the back step from headquarters; the
imanari roll; K-guard. The atlas rates the entry positions separately — outside
ashi 2/5, single-leg X 3/5, saddle 4/5 — and that ordering is also the order you
should learn them in.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is cheap. Late defense is a gamble with your Achilles.**

**Early — before the grip closes.** This is where the escape actually lives. The
moment you feel someone's knee land against the outside of your thigh with your
leg between theirs, the entanglement — not the grip — is already most of your
problem. Turn your knee down toward the mat *toward them*, plant your free foot
on their hip or their near knee, and pull your hips backward. Getting your knee
to the floor kills the entanglement before it exists.

**Middle — grip closed, no pressure yet.** Three things, together, not
separately:

1. **Boot the foot.** Point your toes hard and turn the blade of your foot into
   their forearm so the flat of your foot, not the tendon, is what their radius
   is resting on. This buys you seconds, not minutes.
2. **Kill their base.** Push their far knee away with your free foot so they
   cannot bridge, and stop them squaring their hips to you.
3. **Sit up and free the heel.** Come up on your elbow, control their knee or
   lapel, and slide the heel out the way it went in — over the top of their arm,
   not down through the crook of the elbow. The atlas rates this escape 2/5 and
   4/5 for frequency, which is a fair description: it is not hard, but you have
   to start it early.

**Late — the blade is on the tendon and the bridge has started.** You have
seconds, and the honest answer is to tap. Spinning, kicking or trying to rip the
foot free at this stage is how people tear an Achilles or sprain an ankle badly
enough to miss a season. The atlas puts it plainly: the tap is a technique, and
it is the only reason you get to train tomorrow.

## 8. Drills

1. **Blade-finding, no finish.** Partner lies flat and passive. Enter outside
   ashi, then, before gripping, walk your fingertips down the back of their calf
   until you feel the tendon become a cord. Set the forearm there and close the
   grip. Your partner's only job is to say "muscle" or "tendon." 10 reps per
   side, 3 rounds. Do this with three differently built partners and you will
   have learned section 3 in twenty minutes.
2. **Two-finish drill.** 6 reps of the compression finish (radius edge, blade on
   the tendon, slow steady squeeze) and 6 reps of the extension finish (rigid
   arm, hip bridge plus shoulder-back lean) per side. Alternate. The point is to
   feel the difference clearly enough that you can pick one deliberately mid-roll.
3. **Body-type circuit.** Rotate through four partners of different builds, two
   minutes each, one entry and one finish attempt per partner, then write one
   line per partner on what you had to change. This drill *is* the skill this
   page describes.
4. **Positional sparring — outside ashi, grip closed.** Attacker starts with the
   foot trapped and the grip already set. Attacker must finish or sweep;
   defender must escape, pass the knee line or stand up. 90-second rounds, reset
   on any outcome. Switch roles every round.

## 9. Safety

**The two rules from [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) apply here even though this
is the beginner-legal leg lock: apply slowly, and tap early.**

- **Apply slowly, always.** Three to four seconds from "grip closed" to "they
  should be tapping." There is no situation in training where a fast footlock is
  correct. Ankles and Achilles tendons heal slowly and imperfectly.
- **Keep the foot square.** Rotation plus extension is a knee attack. If you find
  yourself twisting the foot to get a reaction, stop — you have left the
  technique this page describes and entered heel hook territory without the
  position, the control, or the belt.
- **Tap early.** The ankle gives more warning than the knee, but not much, and
  the Achilles gives almost none once it is loaded. Nothing about a training
  round is worth a tendon.
- **Release by opening the grip**, not by throwing their leg away. Throwing the
  leg on the tap is how people get hurt *after* the submission.
- **New partners get a slower version.** Never train the surprise ankle lock at
  speed on someone whose reactions you do not know.

**Legality.** The straight ankle lock is legal at every IBJJF belt in both gi and
no-gi — the only leg lock with no belt restriction. But the *position* has its
own rules, and this catches people out: **knee reaping is illegal in the gi at
every belt and legal in no-gi only for adult brown and black belts.** An
otherwise perfectly legal ankle lock applied from an entanglement where their
knee has crossed your centreline is still a disqualification. Keep the knee line
clean. Rules also change — check the current official ruleset for your event, as
the atlas advises.
