# Ezekiel Choke

> A choke built entirely from your own sleeve: two forearms scissoring a neck with your own cuff as the lock — and the only choke on this list you can finish from *underneath* someone's mount.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke, becomes an air choke done badly) — 🥋 gi-primary |
| **Comes from** | Mount, high mount, side control, kesa gatame, north–south, back control, turtle top — and from inside their closed guard or their mount |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | Gi: all belts, all major rulesets. No-gi: depends on whether the ruleset permits gripping your own clothing — check the event |

**Also known as:** sode guruma jime ("sleeve wheel choke"), ezequiel

> **Note on the atlas rating.** The atlas marks this 🥋 gi-only, at Diff 3 / Com 3. That marker is correct *for the version that actually works*. A no-gi variant exists and is covered in §3, but it is honestly a different technique with a much lower success rate, and calling the move gi-only is the right call. Ratings left as the atlas has them.

---

## 1. The mechanism

Two forearms and a neck. One arm goes behind their head — that arm is the post, and it pulls their head forward into the second. The second forearm crosses the front of their neck, blade down, and that is the arm that closes the arteries. The two arms scissor toward each other and their neck is between them.

The sleeve grip is what makes it work. Your front hand grips inside your own opposite cuff, which locks the loop closed at a fixed circumference — exactly the job the second hand does in a rear naked choke. Without that lock, the front arm slides off as soon as they turn their head. With it, the loop cannot open, so their only defence is to stop you shortening it.

Done properly the ulnar edge of the front forearm — the hard bone on the pinky side — lands on a carotid and it is a clean blood choke. Done sloppily the front forearm lands flat across the windpipe or on the jaw and it becomes a very painful air choke and face crank that a determined person will sit through. Aim the bone at the side of the neck.

**Where it earns its place:** it is the only high-percentage choke you can finish from inside somebody's guard, and one of very few available from inside somebody's *mount*. That single property is worth more than its raw finish rate.

---

## 2. The standard application

From mount, against a same-size opponent. Roles are what matter — mirror everything for the other side.

1. **Get to high mount** or at least ride your knees into their armpits. From low mount the choke is a lot of work; from high mount their shoulders are pinned and their elbows cannot reach.
2. **Thread your post arm under their head**, palm up, so your forearm lies behind their neck and your hand comes out past their far ear. **Deep matters here** — get your bicep, not just your forearm, behind their head, because arm depth is what buys you circumference later.
3. **Take the sleeve grip.** Your other hand reaches across and grips *inside* your own post-arm cuff — four fingers hooked into the sleeve opening, from the inside. It is your gi, not theirs.
4. **Bring the front forearm across their neck**, blade (pinky-side bone) down, aiming for the side of the neck rather than the middle of the throat. Your hand is still holding the cuff, so the arm arrives as a closed loop.
5. **Put your head on the mat past their ear**, on the post-arm side. **This is the detail that turns an arm squeeze into a bodyweight choke** — and it also stops you getting bridged off.
6. **Draw your elbows together and curl your chest forward.** The post arm pulls their head up and forward, the front arm drives down and across. Do not pull straight down into their throat; pull the front elbow *toward the mat on the far side of their head*.
7. **Ride the bridge.** They will bridge. Keep your hips heavy and your head on the mat and let them wear themselves out — the choke tightens on their movement rather than losing to it.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The Ezekiel is a loop of fixed circumference wrapped around a neck of variable circumference, and that is the whole story. Everything below is a way of changing the size of the loop, changing where on the neck it lands, or changing the geometry so a loop that is the wrong size still finishes.

One thing sets this choke apart from the others in this folder: **the gi that matters is yours, not theirs.** Your sleeve length, cuff width and jacket stiffness are the variables. That means unlike the collar chokes, you can actually *choose* your equipment — and you should choose it deliberately.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Thick neck, heavy traps | The loop's circumference is smaller than their neck; you finish on the jaw | Grip the very tip of your cuff to lengthen the loop; get the bicep (not the forearm) behind their head; put the blade on **one** carotid rather than trying to encircle |
| Thin neck | Loop is far bigger than the neck; the forearm slides and gathers nothing | Grip further **up** your own sleeve — mid-forearm, not cuff — to shorten the loop; angle so your wrist, not your mid-forearm, sits on the carotid |
| Broad shoulders / heavy traps | The post arm cannot get through behind the head — their shoulder blocks it | High mount or technical mount first so the far shoulder lifts off the mat; or take it from the side-control/kesa line where the neck is exposed from the side |
| Much heavier / stronger | From bottom they posture and rip; from top they bridge you off | Build the sleeve grip **before** you commit the post arm; from bottom, bridge their head down first, then thread |
| Much lighter | They slip out from under mount before the loop closes | Sleeve grip first, then thread; keep your head on the mat as the third point of base |
| Very stiff / muscular | Shoulders shrug to the ears and pinch the post arm out | Drive their shoulder flat with your chest before threading; go to high mount so the shrug has nowhere to go |
| Very flexible | Deep chin tuck traps your front forearm on their sternum, not their neck | Lift the chin with the post-arm hand before the front arm arrives; or attack the far side of the neck around the tuck |
| Long arms | They frame into your hips and stop you closing distance | Kill the frame first — pin one arm with your shin from high mount before threading |
| **Your** gi: loose jacket, long wide sleeve | Long loop — great for thick necks, sloppy on thin ones | Adjust grip position along the sleeve to match the neck |
| **Your** gi: tight, short, competition-cut sleeve | Short loop, minimal cuff to grip | Grip the extreme edge; prioritise post-arm depth over everything else |
| No gi at all | No cuff to lock the loop; grip slides on sweat | Bicep grip instead of sleeve grip; accept a much lower success rate — see below |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

This is the build that exposes the Ezekiel's core weakness. The loop is made of your two forearms plus whatever length of sleeve you have gripped, and its circumference is fixed the moment your hand closes. If their neck plus their traps is bigger than that circumference, you will feel the choke land on their jaw and their cheekbone instead of their neck — painful, ineffective, and unpleasant for both of you.

**(a) Grip further out on your own cuff — the very tip.** This is the single highest-value adjustment and almost nobody teaches it. Every centimetre further out along your sleeve that you grip adds a centimetre to the loop. On a thick neck, grip the extreme edge of the cuff, sometimes with only two or three fingers hooked in. It feels less secure. It is the difference between a choke and a face crank.

**(b) Get your bicep behind their head, not your forearm.** People thread the post arm until their wrist is past the far ear and stop. Keep going: slide until the crook of your elbow is behind their neck and your bicep is the thing making contact. That buys you several centimetres of usable loop, because the section of arm that is *behind* the neck is no longer part of the section that has to reach around it.

**(c) Stop trying to encircle. Attack one artery.** On a genuinely thick neck, wrapping the whole thing is not on the table. Instead, aim the ulnar blade of your front forearm at the **far** carotid — the one on the opposite side from your post arm — and drive it there diagonally. Your post arm handles the near side by pulling the head forward and slightly rotating it. You are now doing a one-sided blood choke plus head flexion, which finishes people, rather than a two-sided compression that does not fit.

**(d) Load it with your head, not your arms.** Forehead on the mat past their ear on the post-arm side, then drive your chest forward over the top. Your bodyweight arrives through the front forearm. On a thick, strong neck, arm strength will not do it and bodyweight will.

### Thin neck

The mirror problem, and it fools people because everything threads easily. On a slim neck the loop closes with room to spare, so the front forearm slides around without ever compressing anything, and you end up curling your arms harder and harder against a gap.

**Grip further up your own sleeve.** Move your grip back off the cuff toward mid-forearm. Every centimetre back shortens the loop. That is a real, adjustable dial on this choke and it should be the first thing you reach for on a small neck.

**Change which part of the arm lands.** On a thin neck, put your *wrist* — the narrow, hard part just above the hand — on the carotid, rather than the middle of the forearm. The mid-forearm is soft and wide and it bridges over a small neck; the wrist sinks in.

**Bring your front elbow across their centreline** as you finish, so the loop closes diagonally rather than symmetrically. A diagonal loop is functionally smaller than a square one.

### Broad shoulders and thick traps

The problem here is entry, not finish. A wide-shouldered opponent lying flat has no gap between their trapezius and the mat, so the post arm has nowhere to go and you end up shoving a forearm under their skull.

**Make the gap before you need it.** From mount, ride to **high mount** with your knees in their armpits — this lifts and rounds their upper back slightly and opens a channel. Better still, go to **technical mount** when they turn: their upper shoulder is now completely off the mat and the post arm slides in with no resistance at all. The Ezekiel from technical mount against a wide-shouldered opponent is far easier than the same choke from flat mount.

Alternatively, change position entirely: from side control or kesa gatame, the neck is exposed from the side rather than from above, and the post arm can come around the head without fighting a shoulder at all. Broad shoulders are a reason to switch the *position*, not the grip.

### Much heavier or much stronger than you

Two completely different problems depending on which end you are on.

**From the top:** they will bridge, and while your hands are locked in a sleeve grip you have no posts. The fix is head position — forehead on the mat past their ear turns you into a tripod. Your head is the base you gave up when both hands went to the choke. Set it *before* you tighten, not after they start bridging.

**From the bottom — inside their mount, which is the Ezekiel's signature use.** Against a much bigger person the standard order fails: you thread the post arm, they posture up, and now your arm is behind their head with your face exposed. Reverse the order. **Take the sleeve grip first, while both arms are still in front of your own face in a defensive frame** — this looks like defence, costs you nothing, and is invisible. Then bridge to drive their head forward and down, and only when their head is low do you thread the post arm around it. You are threading into a head that is already where you want it rather than trying to pull a heavy head into position with one arm.

### Much lighter than you

They will not stay under you long enough to be choked. From mount a lighter opponent shrimps out during the two seconds it takes to thread and grip.

Same reordering as above: **sleeve grip first, thread second.** The grip costs you no base and no time. Then thread and finish in one motion. Keep your head on the mat throughout — against a light, mobile opponent your base is what fails, not your choke. And go carefully: the Ezekiel is a hard, bony choke and it arrives faster on a small neck than you expect.

### Very stiff or very muscular

Muscular opponents shrug their shoulders to their ears and pinch the post arm out of the gap. Trying to force it through is how you lose the position.

Flatten the shoulder first: drive your chest down onto their near shoulder and pin it to the mat, or from high mount put your shin across their bicep so the arm cannot help the shrug. A shrug is a lot weaker when the arm on that side has no leverage. Then thread.

Muscular necks also mean the blade of your forearm sits on a hard, developed sternocleidomastoid rather than beside it. Aim slightly further forward — into the hollow between the muscle rope and the windpipe — not on top of the muscle.

### Very flexible

A deep chin tuck lets a flexible opponent trap your front forearm against their own sternum, so the choke lands on their chest and their chin rather than their neck. Grinding harder just hurts their face.

Use the post-arm hand: as it comes out past their far ear, cup under their jaw and lift, opening a gap between chin and chest, then slide the front arm into it. If that fails, go around the tuck instead of through it — attack the far side of the neck with the blade, above the level of the chin, and use head flexion to finish.

### Long-armed opponents

Long arms mean strong frames. From mount they will get a forearm into your hip and keep your chest far enough away that your post arm cannot reach. Deal with the frame before the neck: from high mount, drop a shin across one bicep to kill that frame, then thread with the arm on that side.

### Your own gi — the variable everyone forgets

The Ezekiel is the only choke in this folder where **your** equipment is the deciding factor, and the effect is large.

**A loose, well-worn jacket with a long, wide sleeve** gives you a long loop and a big cuff you can find blind. That is excellent on thick necks and clumsy on thin ones — you will need to move your grip up the sleeve on smaller partners or you will just be gathering air. A soft cuff also collapses under load, so grip it firmly with all four fingers.

**A tight, competition-cut jacket with a short sleeve** gives you very little cuff to grip and a short loop. On a thin neck this is ideal — the loop is already small. On a thick neck you are in trouble, and post-arm depth becomes the only variable you have left, so prioritise it. Note that rulesets specify how far the sleeve must reach toward the wrist and check it at the gi check; a competition-legal sleeve is usually shorter than the training gi you learned the move in, which is why people's Ezekiel mysteriously stops working on competition day. Train it in the gi you compete in.

**A stiff, starched sleeve** is easy to locate by feel and hard to grip deeply — the cuff will not fold into your hand. Hook the edge rather than trying to bunch it.

### No gi — be honest about this

There is a no-gi Ezekiel and it is much harder. Two versions exist: grip your own bicep instead of your sleeve, or grip the sleeve of a rashguard.

**The bicep-grip version** works, but the lock is weak. Skin on sweaty skin slides, so the loop opens exactly when you load it, and the choke degrades into a face crank or a jaw compression. It is best used as a short-window opportunity — from technical mount or as they turn into you — rather than as a position you settle into.

**The rashguard-sleeve version** is worse than it sounds. A rashguard sleeve is compression-fit to the arm, so there is almost no material to hook, the cuff is short, and it is slick with sweat. You get a fraction of the loop control the gi gives you.

Two honest caveats. First, gripping the rashguard is a **ruleset question** — some no-gi rulesets ban all clothing grips, including your own, and IBJJF no-gi in particular is restrictive about clothing grips. Check the specific event rather than assuming. Second, understand what you are actually finishing with: without a reliable lock, the no-gi Ezekiel usually finishes as a compression on the jaw and face rather than a blood choke. That is a crank in everything but name, several rulesets ban neck cranks outright, and it is a poor thing to be doing to a training partner. If you train mostly no-gi, this choke is not where your time goes.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** The loop is short by default, so you are excellent on thin necks and stuck on thick ones. Compensate with post-arm depth (bicep behind the head, always) and with position — take the choke from technical mount or kesa, where the neck is approached from the side and less circumference is needed.

**Long arms.** You have loop to spare, which makes thick necks manageable, but you will consistently be too loose on average necks. Grip well up your own sleeve as a default, not as an adjustment.

**Small hands.** Getting four fingers into a stiff cuff is hard. Hook two or three fingers on the very edge and keep the wrist rigid; the grip only has to prevent the loop from lengthening, not carry the load.

**Thick forearms.** Your forearm fills more of the loop, so the effective circumference is smaller than your arm length suggests. Grip further out on the cuff than a slim-armed partner would, and expect to finish on smaller necks with almost no adjustment.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** The bottom-of-mount Ezekiel is one of the genuinely good options available to a smaller person in a terrible position, precisely because it needs no space and no strength — just a sleeve grip and a bridge. Drill the "grip first, bridge, then thread" order until it is automatic; it is the version that works against someone who outweighs you.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Choke is on their jaw and cheek | Loop is too small for their neck | Grip the extreme tip of your cuff; drive the bicep, not the forearm, behind their head |
| Loop feels loose, forearm slides around | Loop is too big for their neck | Move your grip up your own sleeve toward the elbow |
| They cough and go red but never tap | Front forearm is flat on the windpipe | Rotate the blade of the forearm onto the side of the neck, in the hollow beside the muscle rope |
| Post arm will not go behind their head | Their shoulder is flat on the mat blocking the channel | High mount or technical mount first; or take it from side control |
| You get bridged off mid-choke | Both hands committed with no third base point | Forehead on the mat past their ear on the post-arm side, before you tighten |
| Your sleeve grip keeps slipping out | Soft or short cuff, or fingers only half in | Four fingers fully inside, wrist rigid; re-grip rather than squeeze harder |
| Their chin is buried and your arm is on their chest | Deep chin tuck | Cup and lift the jaw with the post-arm hand first, then insert |
| Works in training, fails in competition | Competition-cut sleeve is shorter — the loop shrank | Train the choke in the gi you compete in |
| No-gi version turns into a face crush | No lock; the loop opened under load | Accept it, release, and take a different choke — this is the honest limit of the no-gi version |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Both hands to peel the front forearm | Armbar or straight armlock from high mount — both arms are up and off the mat |
| Turn away from the front arm | Technical mount, then the back — you already have an arm behind their head |
| Bridge hard toward the post-arm side | Ride the bridge to technical mount and take the [bow and arrow](./bow-and-arrow.md) instead |
| Successfully hide the neck under mount | Arm triangle, using the arm they raised to defend |
| From bottom: they posture up out of your frames | Their collar is now in reach — [cross collar choke](./cross-collar-choke.md) or a guard recovery |
| From bottom of their mount: they post a hand on the mat | Trap-and-roll (upa) on that side; the sleeve grip is already a hand-trap |
| They shrug and armour the neck from turtle | [Loop choke](./loop-choke.md) or clock choke; their head is already down |

Feeding in: this choke is the natural answer whenever you have their head cradled — technical mount, kesa gatame, north–south — and whenever *you* are in trouble underneath and want an attack that costs no space. It is also the standard punishment for someone who buries their head in your chest from inside your guard.

## 7. Defending it

**Early.** Watch the hands, not the neck. The tell is a hand crossing the body toward the opposite cuff — that grip is the choke, and it is much easier to prevent than to break. Keep an elbow inside their hips and your chin tucked to the side that their post arm is coming from, and do not let a forearm settle behind your head unopposed. From bottom mount, keep both of your own elbows tight so there is no channel.

**Middle — post arm in, sleeve grip set, front arm arriving.** Get a shoulder up to your own ear on the front-arm side to jam the space, and turn your head *toward* the post arm, not away from it — turning away opens the near carotid straight into the blade. Two hands on their front forearm, and drive it toward their own head (up past your chin), not down toward your chest; the loop is easiest to lengthen at the top. Bridge to disrupt their head base at the same time; a choke without a third point of base is a choke that can be bridged off.

**Late — loop closed, head loaded, elbows drawing in.** There is very little here. The Ezekiel is a short, bony, high-pressure choke and it does not negotiate. Turning hard into the post arm and trying to come up is the only real option, and it works mainly against people who have not set their head on the mat. **Late defense is much worse than early defense** — this is a blood choke when it is tight and a jaw crush when it is not, and neither of those is worth exploring. Tap.

## 8. Drills

**1. Grip-first reps from bottom mount (15 reps).** From inside their mount, with your arms in a defensive frame, take the sleeve grip without moving your elbows. Then bridge, then thread. The point is the *order* — grip, bridge, thread — because that order is what makes this work against someone bigger. Do it slowly until it looks like defence.

**2. Loop-sizing ladder (10 reps each).** Same choke on three different necks — the biggest in the gym, the smallest, and your own size — deliberately changing where along your sleeve you grip each time. Say the grip position out loud: *cuff tip / mid-cuff / mid-forearm.* This one drill teaches most of §3.

**3. Gi swap (2 × 5 minutes).** Do the same drill in your loosest training gi, then in your competition gi. Same partner, different loop, different technique. If your Ezekiel only works in one of them, you have found something useful.

**4. Positional round — technical mount, 90 seconds.** Start in technical mount with the post arm already behind their head. Top attacks the Ezekiel and its chains; bottom escapes. Technical mount is where this choke is easiest and it is under-drilled from there.

---

## 9. Safety

The Ezekiel has a bad safety profile relative to the other chokes in this folder, for one reason: **when it is not working, it is grinding a forearm bone across someone's jaw, cheek and windpipe.** That hurts a great deal, it can bruise or cut a lip against teeth, and it tempts you to push harder rather than to fix the angle. If it is not closing, do not lean on it — release and re-aim, or take something else. A choke that is not on the arteries is not a choke.

When it *is* on the arteries it is fast, so apply progressively rather than snapping the elbows together. Release immediately and completely on the tap; their head is loaded by your bodyweight and you need to unload it, not just relax your hands.

Watch for taps you cannot see. Their arms are often trapped and their face is buried in your chest, so a verbal tap may be muffled and a hand tap may be impossible. Listen, and check.

Legality: in the gi it is a strangle and it is legal at every belt in every major ruleset. In no-gi it depends entirely on whether the ruleset allows you to grip your own clothing — some do not — and on whether what you are actually applying is a strangle or a neck crank. Neck cranks are banned by IBJJF at every belt. If your no-gi version is finishing on the jaw rather than the neck, you are on the wrong side of that line.
