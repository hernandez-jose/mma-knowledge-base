# Bow and Arrow Choke

> The highest-percentage finish available from the highest-percentage position: a deep collar grip, their far pant leg, and a body extension that pulls their head one way and their hips the other.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) — 🥋 gi-only |
| **Comes from** | Back control, turtle top, technical mount, crucifix |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major gi rulesets |

**Also known as:** arco e flecha, bow-and-arrow, BNA

---

## 1. The mechanism

A blood choke. Their own collar is pulled hard across the far side of their neck and closes the far carotid; the near carotid is closed by the edge of the collar on that side, plus the pressure of your chest and the leg you drape over their shoulder. Same target as every collar choke: the soft triangle beside the windpipe, not the windpipe.

What makes this one different — and what makes it the best gi choke in the sport — is that **you do not finish it with your arm.** The collar grip is an anchor point, and your whole body is the lever. You extend your torso, drive their shoulder away with the leg you have draped over it, and hold their far hip in place with a pant grip so their body cannot follow their head. Their neck sits between two vectors travelling in opposite directions, and it is the only compressible thing in the chain. Hence the name: their body is the bow, your collar hand draws the string.

This also means the choke gets *tighter as you get more tired*, which is unusual and valuable. Extension is cheap. Squeezing is not.

---

## 2. The standard application

From back control with the seatbelt, against a same-size opponent.

1. **Establish the seatbelt properly** — choking-side arm over their shoulder, other arm under their armpit, hands connected. Their upper back should be flat against your chest with no daylight.
2. **Open the collar with your under-arm hand and feed it to your over-arm hand.** Your under-hook hand pulls their far lapel down and away from their neck; your over-the-shoulder hand receives the cloth. **Feeding beats digging** — a hand that arrives at cloth already held goes twice as deep as a hand hunting for it.
3. **Set the collar grip deep, thumb inside**, four fingers outside, sliding until your thumb is past the far side of their neck. Thumb-in gives you the reach here; you are coming over a shoulder and reach is the scarce resource.
4. **Keep that elbow low and heavy on their shoulder.** A floating elbow lets them shrug the collar loose. The elbow riding their collarbone is what stops the shrug.
5. **Fall to the hip on the choking side and swing your bottom leg across.** You come off their back and onto your own hip, rotating so your chest ends up roughly perpendicular to their spine. Your bottom leg stays hooked across the front of their hips.
6. **Drape your top leg over their choking-side shoulder** so your hamstring or calf sits on their shoulder and upper arm. This leg is the wedge that stops them turning in to you, and it is half the choke.
7. **Take the far pant leg** with your free hand — at the knee, on the outside of their far leg. **This is the grip people leave out, and without it the choke is a wrestling match.** It stops them turning in, it stops them scooting away, and most importantly it gives you something to extend *against*.
8. **Extend.** Pull the collar toward your own sternum, push their shoulder away with the draped leg, lift the pant grip, and straighten your body out as if you were doing a long, slow back extension. Their head comes toward you, their far hip goes away from you, and the collar closes.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The bow and arrow is a three-point structure — collar, shoulder-leg, pant grip — and different builds break different points. The failure is almost never "not strong enough"; it is that one of the three points is in the wrong place for that person's frame. Their gi matters as much as their body.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Thick neck, heavy traps | The over-the-shoulder hand runs into the trap ridge and never reaches the far side of the neck | Cross-feed the collar with the under-arm hand; climb your chest above their shoulder line so the hand comes *down* onto the collar instead of across it; accept a collarbone-deep grip and close the gap with the shoulder-leg |
| Thin neck | Grip goes deep, then slides across the windpipe instead of the carotid | Grip shallower and aim the collar edge at the far carotid; let the extension tighten it rather than the depth |
| Broad shoulders / wide back | The draped leg cannot reach over the shoulder; the seatbelt is stretched thin | Rotate less — 45°, not 90°; foot on the far hip and knee behind the shoulder instead of a full drape; body triangle before you start |
| Long torso | Neck-to-far-knee is longer than your reach — you cannot hold collar and pant at once | Take the pant grip at the belt or hip, or grip your own shin, and buy the distance back with body extension |
| Short torso / compact | You over-rotate and slide off the back before the pant grip lands | Rotate less, keep the bottom hook in longer, pant grip before you fall to the hip |
| Much heavier than you | You cannot rotate them, and falling back traps you under their weight | Drag the far knee across their centreline first; stay high on your posted elbow; use the leg as a wedge, not a lifter; consider finishing square with a sliding collar instead |
| Much lighter than you | They spin in and out of the position faster than you can build three grips | Pant grip *first*, before the collar; it kills the turn-in that is their whole escape |
| Very stiff / muscular | Shoulders shrug up and armour the neck; spine will not curl | Load the pant grip so their hips rotate — a twisted spine cannot shrug; pull their far shoulder down before your collar hand goes over |
| Very flexible | Inverts, spins, or rolls out before you finish | Pant grip early and heavy — the far knee is what a roll needs, so own it |
| Tight / thick / starched gi | Collar will not fold around the neck; you cannot feed it | Peel the lapel open with both hands before gripping; grip the collar *lip*; finish with a shallower, blade-like grip |
| Loose, worn-out gi | You get a beautiful deep grip and pull two handfuls of slack | Roll the wrist to gather slack, pin the jacket to their body with your chest, expect a strip attempt |

---

### Thick neck and heavy traps

The bow and arrow's collar hand approaches from over the shoulder, which means it has to travel *across* the trapezius before it reaches neck. On someone with a thick neck and a heavy upper back, the hand runs out of reach on top of the muscle every time. The standard advice — "get it deeper" — is not available; there is no deeper.

**Cross-feed instead of digging.** Your under-hook hand can reach their far lapel far more easily than your over-the-shoulder hand can. Use it: pull their far lapel down and out with the under-hook hand until you have several centimetres of collar peeled off the neck, then bring the over-the-shoulder hand down and simply *take the cloth that is being offered to it.* Your choking hand should never search. Once it has the cloth, feed it further by pulling with the under hand while the over hand slides — a two-handed rethread, not a one-handed reach.

**Change your approach angle.** On a thick-necked opponent, climb your chest higher up their back so your shoulder is above theirs and your choking hand comes *downward onto* the collar rather than horizontally across the trap. From below the shoulder line you are fighting the widest part of them. From above it you are dropping into the neck.

**Accept the collarbone grip.** You will often only reach as far as their far collarbone. That is fine, because the missing depth can be supplied by the leg: draping the leg over their shoulder and driving it forward pushes the shoulder away from the neck, which effectively lengthens your collar grip without moving your hand. On a thick neck, the shoulder-leg is not a control detail — it is a load-bearing part of the choke.

**Twist before you squeeze.** A thick trap can only armour the neck when the spine is stacked and square. Use the pant grip to rotate their far hip toward you first; the rotation travels up the spine, the far shoulder drops, and the neck comes out from under the muscle.

### Thin neck

Everything goes in easily, which is the trap. With a slim neck you finish with slack in the system and the collar tends to migrate to the front of the throat, where it makes a painful air choke that they can tolerate for a long time.

Grip **shallower** — stop at the far side of the neck, not behind it — so the edge of the collar sits on the carotid line rather than wrapping past it. Keep the choking elbow lower and closer to their collarbone so the collar tracks along the side of the neck as you extend rather than riding up under the chin. And let the extension do the work: on a thin neck, a shallow grip plus a full body extension is dramatically tighter than a deep grip plus a pull.

### Broad shoulders and a wide back

Two things break at once. The seatbelt is stretched across more real estate, so your chest connection is weaker; and your draped leg lands on their deltoid rather than their shoulder, so it never wedges the joint.

**Rotate less.** The standard version turns you to roughly perpendicular. Against a broad back, stop at about 45° — enough to get the extension line, not so much that your leg has to span the whole shoulder girdle. **Swap the drape for a wedge:** instead of laying your whole leg over the shoulder, put your foot on their far hip and your knee behind their choking-side shoulder blade. The knee does the same job as the drape (stops the turn-in, drives the shoulder forward) over a much shorter distance.

**Use a body triangle rather than hooks** for the setup phase. On a wide torso the hooks are shallow and get stripped exactly when you release one to rotate. The body triangle survives the transition.

### Long torso

A long-torsoed opponent puts their far knee outside your span while your collar hand is committed. You end up choosing between the pant grip and the collar, and people usually choose wrong and abandon the pant grip.

Do not abandon it — move it. **Grip the far side of their belt, or their pant at the hip, instead of the knee.** It is a shorter reach and does most of the same work: it blocks the turn-in and it gives you an anchor to extend against. If even that is out of reach, grip your own shin on the shoulder-leg — you lose the hip control but keep the extension structure, and the choke still finishes. Then buy back the missing distance with your body: extend your spine and push through your bottom leg rather than reaching with your arm.

### Short torso or very compact opponents

The opposite failure. Compact opponents mean everything is close together, and the standard rotation over-shoots — you swing to perpendicular and find yourself off the side of their back with no hooks and no chest connection, and they escape into the space you just made.

Rotate less and later. Keep the bottom hook in for longer than feels natural, and **take the pant grip before you fall to the hip**, not after. On a compact frame the pant grip is easy to get early and it anchors your rotation so you cannot overshoot.

### Much heavier than you

This is the build where the standard version genuinely fails, and it fails in a specific way: rotating to your hip under a much heavier person does not turn them — it turns *you*, and then their weight lands on the leg you just draped and pins you flat with no hooks.

**Drag the far knee across their centreline before you rotate.** Take the pant grip early and pull their far knee toward their own opposite hip. That twists their hips, kills their base, and — critically — makes them lighter on the side you are about to rotate to.

**Stay high on a posted elbow.** Do not lie flat. Keep your under-hook-side elbow posted on the mat and your shoulders above their spine. The extension then comes from your legs and torso rather than from you falling backward under a heavier body.

**Use the leg as a wedge, not a lifter.** Do not try to elevate a heavier person's shoulder with a draped leg. Drive the knee forward into the back of their shoulder as a static wedge and let your extension pull the collar into the gap.

And be honest about the alternative: against someone very much heavier, a sliding collar choke finished square from back control — where you never give up the hooks and never rotate — is a better bet than a bow and arrow. The bow and arrow spends position to buy an angle. Against a big opponent, position is expensive.

### Much lighter than you

Smaller opponents rarely defend the choke; they defend the *position*, and they do it by spinning. They turn into you and scramble before you have built three points of contact.

**Reverse the order: pant grip before collar grip.** The turn-in is their whole escape, and the far pant grip is what kills it. Once their far knee is controlled they cannot rotate, and you can build the collar grip at leisure. Also expect their neck to be small — see the thin-neck adjustments — and go slower on the finish than you think you need to.

### Very stiff or very muscular

A muscular opponent defends by shrugging both shoulders to their ears and keeping their spine stacked and rigid. That shrug genuinely does armour the carotids, and no amount of collar depth beats it directly.

Beat it indirectly. **Rotate their spine.** Pull the far pant grip hard toward you and slightly upward — this rotates their pelvis, which forces their lumbar and thoracic spine into rotation, and a rotated spine cannot hold a symmetrical shrug. The far shoulder drops, the neck lengthens, and your grip suddenly bites. You can also pre-empt it: use your under-hook hand to pull their far shoulder *down* before your collar hand ever goes over the top.

### Very flexible

Flexible opponents survive back attacks by inverting, rolling, or turning their chin all the way past their own shoulder. Against them, the pant grip is not a finishing aid — it is the thing that stops the escape happening at all, because the roll they want requires the far leg to be free.

Take it early, take it at the knee if you can reach, and keep it. If they start to roll anyway, ride the roll rather than fighting it: the bow and arrow survives a roll better than most back attacks because the collar and pant grips are still valid at any orientation.

### Tight, thick or heavily starched gi

A new, stiff, starched jacket is the single most under-discussed variable in this choke.

**What breaks:** the collar will not fold around the neck. You cannot cross-feed it because it does not bend; it springs back off the neck as soon as you release it, and four fingers barely fit inside. Deep grips are simply not available on a stiff collar over a shoulder.

**What gets better:** the stiff collar transmits force along its whole length like a board. On a soft gi a shallow grip dies in the fabric; on a starched gi it delivers a hard edge directly to the carotid. So **stop chasing depth and start chasing the edge**: grip the top lip of the collar with four fingers in, keep your wrist locked, and extend. The choke then works more like a bread cutter across the side of the neck than a wrap around it — and it finishes.

Practical detail: peel the collar open with *both* hands before you grip. On a stiff gi that is the only way to create an opening, and it is worth spending the extra second of position to do it.

### Loose, worn-out or oversized gi

The reverse. Everything feeds beautifully, your hand goes anywhere, and then you extend and nothing happens because you are pulling twenty centimetres of stretched cotton.

**Take the slack up before you extend.** Roll your gripping wrist a full turn inward so a wrap of cloth gathers around it. If it is still loose, use your free hand to pull the collar's other end down toward their hip before you commit to the pant grip — the collar is a loop and tightening one end tightens the other. **Then pin the jacket.** A loose gi slides on the body, which means they can strip your grip by shrugging the entire jacket rather than by attacking your hand. Keep your chest and your draped leg on the cloth so the jacket cannot travel.

Also expect a much deeper grip to be both possible and necessary — on a worn gi, take the depth, because it is free.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** The over-the-shoulder reach is your bottleneck. Cross-feed on everybody, not just thick necks, and get used to finishing from a collarbone-depth grip with the shoulder-leg supplying the rest. Take the pant grip at the belt rather than the knee as your default.

**Long arms.** Reach is not your problem — connection is. Long arms let you take a deep collar grip while your chest floats off their back, and a floating chest loses back control. Set the grip, then re-glue your chest before you rotate.

**Short legs.** The full shoulder drape may not reach on a normal-sized opponent, let alone a broad one. Default to the wedge version: foot on the far hip, knee behind their shoulder. It is not a downgrade.

**Long legs.** The drape is easy and you will be tempted to over-rotate. Stop at 45°. Long legs also make the body triangle a better default than hooks for the setup.

**Small hands.** Four fingers will not fit a thick collar. Take thumb-in grips (which you want here anyway) and accept that on a starched gi you may only get two or three fingers on the lip. A locked wrist matters more than a full hand.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** The bow and arrow is one of the better submissions for a smaller person, because extension scales better across a strength gap than squeezing does. But the rotation is where you get crushed. Build all three points *before* you fall to your hip, and never rotate into someone's weight.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You extend hard and nothing tightens | You have no pant grip, so there is nothing to extend against | Take the far pant leg at the knee or the belt before you extend |
| They turn in and face you | Missing or late pant grip; the far knee was free | Pant grip first against anyone who scrambles |
| Collar grip strips as you rotate | Your choking elbow floated off their shoulder | Elbow low and heavy on their collarbone throughout the rotation |
| Grip stalls on top of their trap | You are reaching, not being fed | Peel the lapel with your under-hook hand and hand the cloth to the choking hand |
| They shrug and armour the neck | Their spine is square and stacked | Pull the pant grip to rotate their pelvis; the shrug cannot survive spinal rotation |
| Your leg sits on their arm, not their shoulder | Broad shoulders; the drape does not span them | Foot on the far hip, knee behind the shoulder blade instead |
| You end up flat on your back under them | You rotated into a heavier opponent's weight | Post your under-hook elbow, stay on your side, drag the far knee first |
| Choke is on the windpipe | Grip too deep or elbow too high on a thin neck | Shallower grip, elbow lower, aim the collar edge at the side of the neck |
| Deep grip, huge pull, no result | Worn-out gi, all slack | Roll the wrist inward one full turn; pull the collar's far end down with your free hand |
| You lose the position finishing | You gave up both hooks to rotate | Keep the bottom hook until the pant grip is set |

## 6. Chains

Back control is worth 4 points under IBJJF and it is the best position in the sport — so the chains here should mostly keep you *on the back*, not trade off it.

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Two hands on the collar grip, both arms up | Armbar on the exposed arm, or switch to the far-side collar |
| Turn in toward the choking side | Take the near-side armbar, or ride to mount — they have given you their back's front door |
| Shrug and hide the neck | Attack the far lapel across the throat instead (sliding collar), or switch sides entirely |
| Strip the pant grip and scoot | Re-hook and return to square back control; retake the seatbelt before re-attacking |
| Roll to escape | Follow the roll — collar and pant grips both survive it; finish on the other side |
| Defend the collar successfully but flatten out | [Ezekiel](./ezekiel.md) from the back, or a straight arm attack |
| Post an arm to base out of the rotation | Crucifix: trap the posted arm with your legs |

Feeding in: this choke is the natural finish from a turtle back-take, from technical mount when they turn away, and from the crucifix. It is also the reason to take a deep collar grip early in any back control — the grip is worth having before you know what you will do with it. A [cross collar choke](./cross-collar-choke.md) grip from mount converts directly into this one when they turn away.

## 7. Defending it

**Early — the only comfortable defense.** Defend the collar before it exists. Pin your own lapels closed with your chin and your shoulders, keep your near hand fighting *inside* their collar hand, and never let a hand travel over your shoulder unopposed. Two hands on their one gripping hand, and strip it toward their thumb. Also protect your far knee: if you feel a hand arrive on your pant leg, deal with it immediately — it is a much cheaper problem than the choke it is about to become.

**Middle — collar in, not yet rotated.** Do not fight the collar. Fight the *rotation*. As they fall to their hip, follow them: turn your own hips the same direction and slide your shoulders down toward the mat so their chest cannot stay above your spine. Get your near hand inside the collar to create a wedge of your own hand between the cloth and your neck. Bring your far knee back toward your own chest so it cannot be gripped.

**Late — rotated, leg over your shoulder, pant grip set.** This is a losing position and you should treat it as such. The one thing that occasionally works is turning hard into the choking side and trying to come up on top of the draped leg, sacrificing everything else. It is a low-percentage save. **Late defense against a bow and arrow is much worse than early defense**: unlike a joint lock, there is no gradually increasing pain to negotiate with, and by the time it is genuinely tight you have a second or two of consciousness left. Tap.

## 8. Drills

**1. Feed-and-grip only (3 × 2 minutes).** From established back control, the only goal is to cross-feed the collar and set a legitimate deep grip with the elbow riding their collarbone. They defend the grip only — no escapes. Switch. This drills the step that actually decides the choke.

**2. Three-point checklist reps (10 reps per side).** Slow, cooperative reps where you say the three points out loud as you build them: *collar — leg — pant.* Then extend. The point of saying it is that under fatigue the pant grip is the one people drop, and it is the one that finishes.

**3. Body-type ladder (10 reps each).** Same choke on the widest back in the gym, the narrowest, and someone your size. Note what you had to change: rotation angle, drape versus wedge, knee versus belt grip.

**4. Positional round — back control, 90 seconds.** Start with seatbelt and both hooks, collar grip already established. Top attacks only the back; bottom escapes. Reset every 90 seconds. Track how often you finish versus how often you lose the position — losing the position while chasing the rotation is the most common real-world failure and this drill exposes it.

---

## 9. Safety

A tight bow and arrow is fast. Because your extension is a whole-body movement, it is very easy to apply far more force far more suddenly than you intended — much more so than with a hand-powered choke. **Extend slowly and progressively.** Let them feel it arrive.

The pant grip plus the shoulder-leg puts real rotational load on their lower back and their shoulder as well as their neck, particularly against a stiff opponent. If you feel their spine loading before you feel the neck close, your angle is wrong: re-aim the collar rather than pulling harder.

Their tap may be hard to see. Their choking-side arm is often trapped under your leg and the other hand is usually fighting the collar, so listen for a verbal tap and watch for a foot tapping the mat. Release both grips completely on the tap — the collar first.

Legality: it is a strangle, legal at every IBJJF belt and in every major gi ruleset. It is gi-only. Under IBJJF you will normally already have 4 points for the back before you attack; note that rotating out of back control can cost you the position (and any further points) if the choke fails, which is a tactical rather than a legal consideration.
