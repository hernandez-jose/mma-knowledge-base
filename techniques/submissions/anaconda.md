# Anaconda

> A head-and-arm strangle threaded **under the neck and out through the near armpit**, then finished by rolling them onto their back — the D'Arce's mirror image, and the front headlock's best answer to an opponent who buries their head.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) |
| **Comes from** | Front headlock, sprawl off a shot, turtle top |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets *as a strangle*. A shallow version that finishes on the spine is a neck crank — IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt |

**Also known as:** anaconda choke; the finishing roll is the **gator roll**

---

## 1. The mechanism

Same wedge as the [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) and the
[D'Arce](./darce.md): the loop contains their head and one of their own arms.
**Their own trapped shoulder closes the carotid on the near side; your threading
arm closes the far side.** What changes is the route your arm takes to get there,
and therefore which bodies it fits.

**The anaconda threads under the neck first and exits through the near armpit.**
Your choking arm slides under their throat, continues across, and your hand comes
out beneath their near armpit, where it catches your own opposite bicep. Your
elbow ends up under their chin. Your free hand lands on their back or far
shoulder. Then you **roll** — over your own shoulder, taking them with you — so
they finish on their back or side with you on your hip beside them, chest driving
into their trapped shoulder.

The difference from the D'Arce, stated exactly:

| | Arm enters | Arm exits | Elbow ends up | Finish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D'Arce** | Under the **near armpit** | Past the **far side of the neck** | Deep, near their far shoulder | Flatten them, drop to your hip, pinch |
| **Anaconda** | Under the **neck** | Out the **near armpit** | Under their chin | Roll them ("gator roll"), then pinch |

That difference decides everything about body types. The armpit is a large,
forgiving hole that every human being has. The gap under a bowed head is not —
some necks do not have one. So the D'Arce is the more universally available
choke, and the anaconda is the specialist: superb on long necks and on people who
duck their head deep, poor on short thick necks where there is nothing to thread
under.

The roll is not decoration. It converts a choke you were holding against their
base into a choke where **their own bodyweight is pressing their shoulder into
their neck** while your chest presses from the other side.

## 2. The standard application

From a front headlock against a same-size opponent whose head is down — typically
off a sprawl or a snap-down.

1. Get a chin strap or a hand under their chin and **pull their head down and
   toward their far shoulder.** This lifts the near side of the neck off their
   chest and creates the slot you are about to thread into.
2. Thread the choking arm **under their neck**, palm up, aiming your fingers at
   their **near armpit** — travel along the collarbone, not straight down through
   the chin.
3. Keep threading until your hand emerges under the near armpit. **Your bicep,
   not your wrist, must be the surface on their neck.**
4. Catch your own opposite bicep. Free hand goes flat on their back or far
   shoulder blade.
5. Pinch your elbows together and drop your chest onto their trapped shoulder.
   Take the slack out **before** you move.
6. **Roll over your own shoulder** — not over your head — in the direction of
   your threading arm, taking them with you. Keep your elbows pinched the whole
   way; the most common way this is lost is opening an arm to post.
7. Land on your hip beside them with their back or side on the mat, your chest
   driving into their trapped shoulder, knees walking toward their head.
8. Finish by pinching the elbows and dropping your chest — not by pulling on
   their head.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The anaconda is the most build-selective
choke on this shelf. It requires a threadable gap under a bowed neck, and that
gap varies enormously — from a wide, obvious tunnel on a long-necked opponent to
literally nothing on a short, thick-necked one. Read the neck before you commit,
because the alternative is a neck crank you did not intend.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | There is no gap under the bowed neck; the arm stalls at the wrist and you end up levering the spine | Pull the chin toward their **far** shoulder first to open a slot, travel along the collarbone, and if it still stalls, switch to the [D'Arce](./darce.md) |
| **Short neck / high shoulders** | The chin sits on the chest with no tunnel at all | Do not force it. This build is a D'Arce, an [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), or a back take |
| **Thin / long neck** | Easiest thread you'll ever get — and the loop ends up loose | Exit at the armpit and grip immediately; walk your knees toward their head to remove slack **before** you roll |
| **Broad shoulders / thick deltoids** | The armpit exit is blocked and the roll is heavy | Extend and pin their trapped arm before threading so the shoulder flattens; grip your own forearm if the bicep is out of reach |
| **Short arms** | Their trapped arm doesn't extend, so their own shoulder never wedges | Skip the roll and finish flat: drive their trapped shoulder in with your chest and knee |
| **Long arms** | They post the far hand wide and kill the roll; they can also peel the threading hand | Clear or block the far post first; roll toward the side their arm is posted on, so the post has nowhere to go |
| **Much heavier than you** | The gator roll is a lift, and you will not lift them | Do not roll them — roll **yourself**. Fall to your own shoulder and let their weight follow, or skip the roll and finish flat |
| **Much lighter than you** | The roll overshoots and you land past them; the choke closes far faster than expected | Shorten the roll, keep your chest glued, apply progressively |
| **Very flexible / good scramblers** | They spin out or granby before the grip closes | Grip first, position second; knee against their far hip to remove the spin |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

This is the build that turns anacondas into neck cranks, and it does it silently
— the arm goes in far enough to feel like progress, stops at the wrist, and every
subsequent effort levers their head into flexion against their own spine. They
tap, you think you choked them, and what you actually did is banned under IBJJF
rules at every belt.

What to do about it:

1. **Make the gap before you thread.** Chin-strap hand under their chin, and pull
   their head **down and toward their far shoulder**. Rotating the head away
   lifts the near side of the neck off their chest and manufactures a tunnel
   where a thick neck offers none. This is the single most useful adjustment on
   this build and almost nobody does it.
2. **Travel along the collarbone, not under the chin.** Aim your fingers at their
   **near armpit** and slide along the top of their chest. Trying to burrow
   straight under a thick chin is a longer, narrower path through the densest
   tissue on their body.
3. **Elevate the head first.** If you can lift their head even slightly with the
   chin strap before threading, the neck extends and gains a few centimetres of
   accessible space underneath.
4. **Shorten the grip.** If your hand emerges but cannot reach your own bicep,
   take your own **forearm** or hook your own shoulder. The grip fastens the
   shape; it does not create the compression.
5. **Know the switch.** If the arm stalls with only your wrist under the neck:
   stop. That is a crank. Withdraw and take the [D'Arce](./darce.md) instead —
   the armpit entry exists on this body even though the neck entry does not.

### Short neck, high shoulders

Distinct from a thick neck, and even less forgiving: the head simply sits low
between high shoulders, so no amount of head rotation opens a tunnel. This is the
one build where the honest instruction is **do not attempt this choke.**

- Take the [D'Arce](./darce.md) — the armpit is still there.
- Or the [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) if you can flatten them out.
- Or, best, use the front headlock for what the atlas says it is best for: **going
  behind.** Snap them down, spin to the back, take the
  [rear naked choke](./rear-naked-choke.md). Against short thick necks the back
  take is a far higher-percentage road out of the front headlock than any front
  choke.

### Thin or long neck

The build the anaconda was made for. The thread is trivially available, and
people who duck their head deep — wrestlers on a stuffed shot, guard players
turtling — hand it to you. The failure here is slack.

- **Grip the instant your hand clears the armpit.** Do not admire the thread.
- **Take out slack before the roll**, not after: walk your knees toward their
  head, pinch your elbows, drop your chest. A loose anaconda that gets rolled
  becomes a loose anaconda on the ground, and rolling does not tighten it.
- **Do not over-thread.** A long neck lets your arm keep going until your bicep
  passes the far side of the neck entirely and lands on their far shoulder. The
  reference point is your **bicep across the front and side of their neck** with
  your elbow under their chin.
- **Apply progressively.** Long thin necks under a correct anaconda go out very
  quickly, and the person is usually face-down where you cannot see their
  expression.

### Broad shoulders and thick deltoids

Their deltoid is doing the near carotid for you, which is genuinely helpful. The
problems are the exit and the roll: a big shoulder blocks the armpit, and a broad
frame is heavy to turn over.

- **Extend and pin the trapped arm before threading.** Pull their near arm
  forward and straighten it, or pin the wrist under your knee. An extended arm
  flattens the deltoid and opens the armpit; a bunched-up arm closes it.
- **Grip whatever you can reach.** Your own forearm is fine. The shape holds
  because your elbows pinch, not because of where your fingers landed.
- **Shorten the roll.** Instead of a full gator roll, roll only far enough to put
  them on their side, then drive your chest down. Broad opponents are heavy to
  turn 180° and you do not need 180°.

### Their short arms

The near-side wedge depends on their trapped arm being extended so their own
shoulder is pushed into their own neck. Short arms do not extend far enough past
your body, so you get a lot of head control and half a choke.

- **Skip the roll.** Finish flat, with them face-down and you on top. Drive their
  trapped shoulder forward into their neck with your **chest and your near knee**
  — you are supplying the leverage their arm length cannot.
- **Walk your knees toward their head** to compress the loop from a better angle.
- Short arms also mean they are worse at getting a defensive hand between their
  own chin and chest, so the thread is often *more* available on this build than
  you would expect.

### Their long arms

Long arms defeat the anaconda in a specific way: the far hand posts wide on the
mat and simply refuses to be rolled. They can also reach back and peel your
threading hand before you grip.

- **Deal with the post first.** Either clear it — knock it out with your free
  hand or your shoulder — or **roll toward the posted side**, so their own arm
  has nowhere to travel and folds under them.
- **Grip before position.** Their reach means the window between "arm threaded"
  and "hand peeled" is short. Take the bicep grip, then sort out your hips.
- **Block the stand-up.** A long-armed opponent's other exit is posting and
  standing; a leg over their back or a knee at their far hip stops it.

### Much heavier than you

The gator roll is, functionally, a lift. Against someone much heavier it is not
available, and attempting it puts your neck under their body.

- **Roll yourself, not them.** The anaconda roll is properly a *sacrifice* roll:
  you fall to your own shoulder and let their weight follow you over. Initiate by
  dropping your own hip and shoulder to the mat, not by trying to turn their
  torso.
- **Or skip the roll entirely.** Finish flat with them face-down: elbows pinched,
  chest heavy on their trapped shoulder, knees walking toward their head. This
  works and it removes the strength requirement.
- **Take it off their forward drive.** A heavier person shooting a takedown or
  driving out of turtle supplies all the momentum the roll needs. That is the
  moment.
- **Never roll if your own head or neck will end up underneath them.** This is
  the specific injury mechanism to avoid, and it does not care how good the choke
  was.

### Much lighter than you

The choke is available immediately and the roll overshoots — you end up past
them, on your back, with them on top of your grip.

- **Shorten the roll.** Roll to your side, not through to your other shoulder.
  Land on your hip with your chest still attached.
- **Keep the chest glued** throughout. Against a lighter opponent, momentum you
  generate is momentum they cannot resist, which means it is easy to throw them
  further than you intended.
- **Apply slowly.** A small neck inside a big arm closes fast and cranks first.
  This is the most common way people hurt smaller partners with this technique.

### Very flexible and good scramblers

They will not defend the choke. They will granby, spin out or sit through before
you close the grip, and their flexibility means the roll they can absorb is
larger than yours.

- **Grip first, position second.** The grip is what survives a scramble.
- **Kill the spin with your knee** at their far hip before you commit the
  threading arm.
- **If they roll first, go with it.** An anaconda with pinched elbows finishes
  mid-scramble as well as it does from a set position — you just have to not open
  your arms to post.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Thread along the collarbone rather than deep under the chin, and
grip your own **forearm** or shoulder rather than chasing your bicep. Accept that
short thick necks are simply not your choke — the [D'Arce](./darce.md) enters a
bigger hole and should be your default from the front headlock.

**Long arms.** You can thread almost anyone, which means over-threading past the
neck onto the far shoulder is your standard failure. Reference point: **elbow
under their chin, bicep on their neck.**

**Thick arms.** Your bicep fills the loop well, but a thick upper arm may not fit
under a bowed neck at all. Open the gap with the chin strap first, and be quick
to switch to the D'Arce.

**Small hands or a weak grip.** Bicep and forearm grips only, and never your own
wrist — the roll loads the grip in a direction a wrist grip cannot hold.

**Stiff back or a history of neck trouble.** The gator roll is a shoulder roll
under load. Build it up slowly with the drill below, and if it is not comfortable,
finish flat instead — the flat finish is fully legitimate and costs you nothing
but style points.

**Much lighter than your partners.** Use the sacrifice roll, not the lift, and
take the choke off their forward momentum rather than trying to create it. Also
consider skipping the roll: from turtle top, a flat anaconda with your knees
walking toward their head is a bodyweight finish rather than a strength one.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The arm stalls halfway under the neck | No gap — thick or short neck, and you threaded straight at the chin | Pull the chin toward their far shoulder first and travel along the collarbone; if it still stalls, switch to the D'Arce |
| They tap complaining about their neck | Shallow thread — you cranked the spine, you did not strangle | Release. This is banned under IBJJF rules at every belt |
| Grip is locked but nothing happens after the roll | Slack was never taken out before the roll | Pinch elbows, walk your knees toward their head and drop your chest — rolling does not tighten a loose choke |
| You roll and lose the grip | You opened an elbow to post during the roll | Elbows stay pinched all the way over; land on your hip, not your knees |
| You roll and end up past them, on your back | Overshot — usually against a lighter partner | Roll to your side only, keep the chest attached |
| They post the far hand and won't turn | Their post is doing its job | Clear the post, or roll toward the posted side so it folds under them |
| Only one side of their neck is compressed | Their trapped arm isn't extended, so the shoulder isn't wedged | Extend and pin the trapped arm; drive the shoulder with your chest and near knee |
| They sit out / granby before you finish | You spent time on position instead of grip | Grip first; block the far hip with your knee |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| No gap under the neck — you can't thread | [D'Arce](./darce.md) — go under the near armpit instead |
| They flatten out and stop defending the arm | [Arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), or finish the anaconda flat |
| They post the far hand and stop the roll | Peruvian necktie, or spin behind to the back |
| They turn away from you | Take the back and the [rear naked choke](./rear-naked-choke.md) |
| The head comes up as they posture | [Guillotine](./guillotine.md) — the gap that just opened is a guillotine's entry |
| They roll first | Go with it — a pinched anaconda finishes mid-roll |
| Choke fails but the roll worked | You are in side control or mount with their arm trapped. Take the position; that's a win |

The [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), [D'Arce](./darce.md) and anaconda are one
wedge with three entries. Reading the neck and picking the right entry within the
first second is most of the skill.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (before the thread).** Same rules as the D'Arce, plus one. Do not
leave your head below your hips. Do not leave a near arm extended. And
specifically against the anaconda: **close the gap under your own neck.** Chin
hard to your own chest, and drive your near shoulder up toward your ear so the
tunnel under your neck disappears. A defender with no gap cannot be anaconda'd,
regardless of how good the attacker is.

**Middle defense (arm threaded, grip forming).** Fight the grip, and **walk
toward the choking arm** — moving toward the threading side unwinds the wedge,
moving away tightens it. Get your trapped shoulder off the mat and post your far
hand wide to make the roll impossible; a defender with a wide base and a heavy
head is very hard to turn. Sit-out or hip-heist away from the choking arm's side
if the base holds.

**Late defense (grip locked, rolled, you're on your back).** Substantially worse
than early defense. Once you have been rolled, their entire bodyweight is on the
wedge and your own shoulder is being driven into your own neck by gravity — the
choke tightens by itself and there is no lever you can reach. Worse, the shallow
version compresses your spine before it compresses your carotids, so gambling
risks a neck injury rather than a nap. **Tap.**

Positional detail lives in [turtle escapes](../escapes/turtle-escapes.md) and
[side control escapes](../escapes/side-control-escapes.md).

## 8. Drills

**1. Gap-making reps.** Partner on all fours with their chin buried. Chin-strap
hand under the chin, pull their head down and toward their far shoulder, then
thread — **no grip, no finish.** **3 sets of 10 per side.** You are training the
step that makes the anaconda possible on thick necks.

**2. Gator roll, no choke.** From a front headlock with your arm through, roll
your partner over your own shoulder and land on your hip beside them. **20 slow
reps per side, choke arm loose.** Roll over the shoulder, never the head. Build
speed only once the landing is boring.

**3. Read-the-neck sorting.** Three partners: one long-necked, one average, one
thick or short-necked. From the same front headlock, choose anaconda, D'Arce or
back take within two seconds and execute. **Three reps each.** The correct answer
for the thick-necked partner will usually not be the anaconda — recognising that
is the skill being trained.

**4. Front headlock positional sparring.** Start in a front headlock with their
near arm in. Top wins with an anaconda, D'Arce or back take; bottom wins by
standing, sitting out, or getting to guard. **90-second rounds, reset, switch.**

## 9. Safety

A blood choke, **legal at every belt in every major ruleset** — when it is
actually a blood choke. The failure case is the same as the D'Arce and more
common here, because the anaconda's entry is the one that most often stalls: a
**shallow** anaconda, with only your wrist under the neck, levers the cervical
spine into flexion. That is a **neck crank**, banned by IBJJF at every belt (see
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part XII). The test: if your bicep is not the
surface on their neck and you are pulling on their head to compensate, let go.

The roll adds a second, distinct risk. **Roll over your shoulder, never your
head**, check the space behind you before you commit, and never roll in a way
that puts your own neck under a heavier partner. In a crowded open mat, finish
flat instead.

Apply progressively and release the instant they tap. Their face is usually
pointing at the mat, so you will not see their expression — listen, watch for a
tap with either hand or a foot, and treat sudden stillness as a tap.
