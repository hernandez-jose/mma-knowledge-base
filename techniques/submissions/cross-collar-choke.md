# Cross Collar Choke

> The first gi choke anyone learns and the last one anyone stops using: two collar grips, elbows driven down past their ears, both carotids closed by cloth and forearm.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) — 🥋 gi-only |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, mount, high mount, technical mount, knee-on-belly |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major gi rulesets |

**Also known as:** juji jime, cross choke, X-choke; *nami juji jime* (both thumbs in), *gyaku juji jime* (both hands four-fingers-in), *kata juji jime* (one of each)

> **Note on the rating.** The atlas rates this 2/5, and that is right for *learning* it — a white belt can be taught it on day one. Landing it on a resisting, same-size training partner is closer to a 3. The rating is left as the atlas has it.

---

## 1. The mechanism

This is a blood choke. Both of your forearms — or the collar cloth stretched between them — press into the carotid triangle on each side of their neck, just in front of the big rope of the sternocleidomastoid. Occlude both common carotids and they lose consciousness in seconds. The trachea is not the target; if you feel cartilage crunching under your wrist bone you have the choke in the wrong place, and it will hurt them a lot without finishing.

The part people misunderstand is what the cloth actually does. The gi does not choke them. The gi is a **leash of fixed length** that stops your hands travelling apart. Once both grips are set, the loop around their neck has a fixed circumference, and the only way to shrink it is to change the *shape* of the loop — which is what happens when you drive your elbows down and out past their ears while your hands stay put. Your elbows going to the mat is the finish. Your hands pulling apart is not: pulling apart just stretches the jacket.

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard against a same-size opponent. The mount version is the same choke with gravity added — see §6.

1. **Break their posture.** Feet on their tailbone, pull their head down with a collar or a neck grip until their chest is within a forearm's length of yours. A cross collar choke on a postured opponent is a grip, not a choke.
2. **Feed the collar before you grip it.** With your left hand, grab their right lapel low — around their sternum — and pull it down and across toward their left hip. This peels the collar off the back of their neck and opens a gap between the cloth and their trapezius. **This step is the one everybody skips, and it is the one that decides whether the first grip goes deep.**
3. **First grip, deep.** Right hand into their right collar, thumb inside the collar, four fingers outside, sliding along the inside of the collar until your knuckles are behind their neck — the tag at the back of the collar is a useful landmark. Your knuckles should be sitting on the far side of their neck.
4. **Pin that elbow to your own ribs.** The deep grip dies the moment your elbow floats. Elbow tight to your ribcage keeps the wrist from unwinding and makes the grip almost impossible to strip.
5. **Second grip, under the first.** Your left hand travels *underneath* your right forearm and takes their left collar, four fingers inside, thumb outside, roughly at the collarbone. Under is tighter; over is faster to enter and easier to defend. **Second hand under, not over.**
6. **Pull them flat onto you.** Chest to chest, no daylight. If there is space between your chest and theirs, the space is where the choke is escaping to.
7. **Drive your elbows down past their ears toward the mat**, and let your wrists rotate so the blade of the forearm rolls into the neck. **Do not pull your hands apart.** Your hands stay where they are; your elbows travel.
8. **Add the legs.** Kick your heels up toward their shoulder blades, or bring one leg over their shoulder and press their head down with your shin. This adds head flexion, which shortens the neck and brings the carotids closer to the surface.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The cross collar choke has a fixed geometry — two hands, one loop, one neck — and almost every body type breaks that geometry in a specific, predictable way. Their *gi* is as much a body-type variable as their neck is, so it gets its own subsections.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Thick neck, heavy traps | Deep thumb-in grip cannot get past the trap muscle; the loop's circumference is smaller than their neck | Feed the collar first to pre-load slack; take the **second** grip four-fingers-in so the forearm blade crosses the carotid; finish elbows-to-the-mat past the ears, not hands apart |
| Thin neck | Loop closes with slack left over; you slide off the carotids onto the windpipe | Grip **shallower**, aim the blades at the sides of the neck, take the second grip lower on the collar and rotate the wrists to eat the slack |
| Broad shoulders | Their deltoids jam your biceps and stop your elbows travelling past their ears | Angle off 45°: one foot to their hip, hips out, attack one carotid down the diagonal instead of both symmetrically |
| Long torso | From guard you cannot reach the tag and keep your chest connected | Take the first grip at the far collarbone, not the back of the neck, and close the distance with your legs instead of your arms |
| Much heavier / much stronger | They posture through it and rip the grip | Grip *before* you break posture, then break posture onto the grip; switch to the lapel-fed variant so cloth, not reach, supplies the depth |
| Much lighter | From mount they slip out before the second grip lands | Go high mount first; base the second forearm on the mat so the choke arrives before the pin fails |
| Very stiff / muscular | Elbows glued to ribs; the second hand cannot get in | Take the second grip *under* their arm rather than over the top, or wedge a knee into their elbow first |
| Very flexible | Extreme chin tuck buries the throat between the collarbones | Lift the chin with the far-hand blade under the jaw before the second grip goes in |
| Tight / thick / starched gi | Collar stands off the neck like a board; no slack, no fold | Shallow grip on the collar *lip*; let the stiffness work as a blade; feed slack up from the skirt |
| Loose, worn-out gi | Infinite slack; you gather two handfuls of cloth and nothing closes | Roll the wrist to take the slack up, re-grip lower, and expect to need a much deeper first grip |

---

### Thick neck and heavy traps

This is the build the choke was *not* designed around, and the standard version fails in a very specific way: your deep thumb-in hand runs into the ridge of their upper trapezius before it reaches the side of the neck, so your knuckles end up sitting on muscle instead of on the carotid triangle. Getting stronger does not fix this. Three changes do.

**(a) Pre-load the slack before the thumb goes in.** With your non-gripping hand, take their lapel low — sternum height or lower — and pull it down and across toward their opposite hip. The collar is a continuous loop of cloth: pulling down at the bottom pays out slack at the top, and it rotates the whole jacket so the collar lifts off the trap. Now your gripping thumb enters a gap that already exists rather than trying to bulldoze one. Do this every time on a thick neck; do it *before* the hand moves, not after it has stalled.

**(b) Take the second grip four-fingers-in, not thumb-in.** With a thumb-in grip your knuckles and the back of your hand are the contact surface — a small, lumpy, high-pressure area that a thick sternocleidomastoid and a layer of trap simply absorb. With a four-fingers-in grip your palm faces their neck and the contact surface becomes the **radial blade of your forearm** — a long, straight bone edge that lies along the whole carotid line instead of poking at one point of it. You lose two or three inches of reach and gain a compressing surface that actually reaches the artery. On a thick neck that trade is always worth it. (This makes the choke a *kata juji jime* — one hand each way. That is a real, named, correct version, not a compromise.)

**(c) Finish with your elbows, not your hands.** On a normal neck you can get away with a lazy finish. On a thick one you cannot. Keep both hands exactly where they landed and drive both elbows down toward the mat, travelling *past their ears* — not toward each other, and not apart. This rotates both forearms inward around their neck like two closing gates and shortens the effective loop without needing another inch of grip depth. From mount, add your forehead: put your head on the mat past their ear on the deep-grip side and let your bodyweight, not your biceps, deliver the elbow drive.

Two more things that help on this build specifically. Do not chase the collar tag — take the first grip at the *far collarbone* and accept it. And flex their neck aggressively with your legs or your chest before you squeeze: neck flexion pulls the carotids forward out from under the trap and shortens the distance your forearm has to travel.

### Thin neck

The opposite problem, and it fools people because the grips go in beautifully. With a narrow neck, both grips reach the back of the neck easily and you finish with a loop that is *larger* than the thing inside it. You squeeze, they feel pressure on the front of the throat, nothing closes, and you have given them a painful air choke they can grit through for thirty seconds.

Adjust three things. **Grip shallower** — stop at the side of the neck, not behind it, so the blade of each forearm sits on a carotid rather than wrapping past it. **Take the second grip lower**, at the collarbone or just below, so the cloth spans the throat as a tight band rather than draping loosely. **Rotate the wrists** hard as you finish — on a thin neck the rotation is doing most of the tightening because there is no bulk to press against. If you finish and feel their cartilage rather than the soft hollow beside their windpipe, back the grips out an inch each and re-aim to the sides.

A thin neck also means the classic both-thumbs-in *nami juji jime* is available and is the tightest version — take it here, because this is one of the few builds where the extra depth costs you nothing.

### Broad shoulders

Broad shoulders do not defend the neck; they defend the *approach* to the neck. From closed guard, their deltoids sit against the inside of your biceps and physically block your elbows from getting past their ears, which kills the finishing mechanic even when both grips are perfect.

Stop attacking symmetrically. Put one foot on their hip, hip-escape out to about 45°, and turn your shoulders so you are attacking down a diagonal — your deep hand's forearm crosses under their jaw from one side rather than both forearms trying to close evenly. From that angle only one elbow has to travel, and it travels into open space beside their head instead of into their shoulder. Pair it with a leg over their far shoulder to pin that side down so they cannot rotate away from the pressure.

From mount the same problem shows up as the second hand not being able to get in. Fix it by riding higher — knees into their armpits — so their shoulders are behind your knees rather than in front of your hands.

### Long torso

A long-torsoed opponent in your closed guard puts their neck further from your hips than your arms are long. You end up choosing between a deep grip with your chest disconnected (they posture out) and a connected chest with a shallow grip (nothing closes).

Take the shallow grip on purpose, at the far collarbone, and buy back the depth with your legs: climb your guard high, feet at their armpits or one shin across their back, and use the legs to pull their upper body down into a grip you have already set. Do not reach. If you find yourself reaching, your feet have slipped down their back.

### Much heavier or much stronger than you

Against a big, strong opponent, the sequencing has to invert. The standard order is *break posture → grip*. Against someone who can posture at will, that order gives them the chance to strip you every time. Reverse it: **grip first, while they are still upright and relaxed and their collar is easy to reach, then break the posture onto the grip you already own.** Take the first grip while they are tall, pin your elbow to your ribs, and only then pull them down. A deep grip with a pinned elbow is far harder to strip than it is to acquire.

Second change: stop trying to supply depth with reach and supply it with cloth. Pull their opposite lapel out and feed it across their neck, then grip *that* — the fed lapel gives you the compressing surface without needing your hand behind their neck. Third: expect to fail and be ready for it. A heavy opponent's posture-up is the cleanest omoplata and triangle entry you will get all round.

### Much lighter than you

The problem here is not the neck; it is that they will not stay under you long enough. From mount a much smaller opponent bridges, shrimps, and slides out during the two seconds it takes to set the second grip.

Set the first grip and then **go to high mount before the second grip**, knees under their armpits, so their shoulders are pinned before you commit a second hand. When you take the second grip, put the forearm of that arm on the mat beside their head as a base instead of floating it — you keep your base and get your grip in the same motion. Also mind the pressure: a strong choke on a much lighter partner arrives faster than you expect and their tap may be a leg slap or a verbal call because both their arms are trapped.

### Very stiff or very muscular

The bottleneck is the second hand, not the first. A muscular opponent with their elbows welded to their ribs leaves no channel for the second hand to travel under the first. Rather than fighting for the channel over the top — which is the version they can see and block — go **under their arm**: your second hand travels beneath their tricep and comes up to the collar from the outside. It is a shorter, hidden path.

If that fails, make the channel: drive a knee or shin into the crook of their elbow to peel one arm off their ribs, then insert. Thick lats also mean a thick jacket over the shoulders; add an extra beat of collar feeding.

### Very flexible

Flexible opponents defend this choke by burying their chin so deep that their throat disappears between their collarbones. The cloth then sits on their jaw and their chin, and no amount of elbow drive reaches an artery.

Before the second grip goes in, use the blade of that hand under their jawline and lift — a short, firm upward scoop that opens two or three centimetres of neck — and slide the grip into the gap you just made. From mount you can do the same job with your chest by driving forward over their forehead so their head extends. Once the second grip is in, their chin tuck stops mattering, because you are attacking the sides of the neck, not the front.

### Tight, thick or heavily starched gi

Their jacket changes this choke more than most people's necks do, and a stiff gi changes it in both directions at once.

**What gets harder:** a new, thick, starched collar does not fold. It stands off the neck like cardboard, it will not conform to the carotid, and four fingers barely fit inside it. The slack you are used to paying out from the skirt does not travel up a stiff collar — the cloth is too rigid to redistribute. Deep grips are genuinely unavailable.

**What gets easier:** a rigid collar transmits force along its length like a plank. A shallow grip on a soft gi does nothing because the cloth folds and the force dies in the fabric; the same shallow grip on a starched gi delivers a hard edge straight into the neck. So on a stiff gi, **stop chasing depth and start chasing the edge.** Grip the *lip* of the collar — the very top edge — with four fingers in, keep the wrist stiff, and use the collar itself as the blade, in a bread-cutter-like action across the neck rather than a wrap around it. Feed slack from the skirt upward with a firm two-handed pull before you grip, because if the collar is going to give you any at all, that is the only way you will get it.

Note that most rulesets police gi stiffness at the gi check and restrict excessively starched jackets. In competition you can broadly assume a legal, conforming collar; in training you cannot assume anything.

### Loose, worn-out or oversized gi

The mirror image. A soft, stretched, well-washed gi feeds like a dream — your hand goes anywhere, the collar folds around whatever you want — and then the choke does nothing, because you are holding two fistfuls of slack. The loop you built has a circumference of their neck plus fifteen centimetres of stretched cotton.

Three fixes, in order. **Roll the wrist** on each grip after it lands — a full rotation inward gathers a wrap of cloth and shortens the leash without moving your hand. **Re-grip lower** on the second collar, because the collar is tightest where it is anchored near the sternum and loosest at the top. And **go deeper than you would on a stiff gi** — the depth is free here, so take it; the first grip should go well past the back of the neck.

Expect them to strip a loose gi grip more easily too: the jacket slides on their body, so they can shrug the whole collar out from under your hand. Pin the jacket to their body with your chest or your forearm while you finish.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** You cannot own the tag-deep grip on anyone larger. Build the choke around cloth instead of reach: feed the lapel across with the free hand and grip the fed lapel, or take a shallow first grip and make the second grip do the work with a four-fingers-in blade. Also, close distance with your legs rather than your torso — a high closed guard adds effective arm length.

**Long arms.** The choke is easy to enter and hard to finish, because a long arm bends at the elbow in the wrong place and your elbows end up wide rather than down. Consciously shorten yourself: elbows pinned to your ribs from the moment the first grip lands, and finish by dropping your elbows past their ears rather than pulling.

**Small hands.** Four fingers will not fit inside a thick or starched collar. Use thumb-in grips for depth and accept fewer four-fingers-in options; on stiff collars, grip the lip with two or three fingers and a stiff wrist rather than fighting to get the whole hand in.

**Thick forearms.** Good news: your forearm is the compressing surface, and a big one closes a loop faster. You will need less depth than a slim-armed training partner does — which means you should be taking *shallower* grips than the instructional you learned it from.

**Weak or tired grip.** This choke is grip-endurance-expensive and your grip fails before your arms do. Take grips late, pin the elbow immediately (a pinned elbow holds a grip that a tired hand cannot), and prefer four-fingers-in, which loads the strong finger flexors rather than a thumb hook.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** You will not out-muscle the posture battle, so make the grip permanent and let the rounds run long. The cross collar is one of the few submissions where a small person genuinely can beat a big one with pure structure — but only if the elbow stays pinned.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| They gasp, cough, go red, but never tap | You are on the trachea, not the carotids | Back both grips out an inch and re-aim the blades to the sides of the neck, in the soft hollow beside the windpipe |
| Grips are deep, you squeeze hard, nothing happens | You are pulling your hands apart against a fixed-length leash | Freeze the hands; drive both elbows down to the mat past their ears |
| First grip keeps getting stripped | Your gripping elbow is floating away from your ribs | Elbow to your own ribcage the instant the grip lands, before anything else |
| Second hand cannot find a path | Their elbows are glued to their ribs | Go under their tricep to the collar, or wedge a knee into their elbow crook first |
| Grip stalls halfway in on a thick neck | You never created slack | Pull their lapel down and across at sternum height first, then insert |
| Choke feels tight but they posture straight out | You broke posture *before* gripping and lost the sequence | Grip while they are tall, then break the posture onto the grip |
| You are gathering cloth and it never tightens | Worn-out gi, all slack | Roll each wrist inward one full turn to gather the slack, re-grip lower |
| Their chin is on your wrist and you cannot get under | Deep chin tuck | Scoop under the jaw with the blade of the second hand and lift before inserting |
| You get swept trying to finish from mount | Both hands committed, head high, no base | Head to the mat past their ear on the deep-grip side — it bases you *and* increases the elbow drive |

## 6. Chains

The cross collar's real value is that it forces a defensive shape that opens everything else. Nobody defends a collar choke with their elbows in.

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Posture up hard from closed guard | Armbar or triangle — their arms are extended and their head is up |
| Two hands to peel your deep grip | Omoplata or the sweep to mount — both their arms are off the mat |
| Turn away from the deep grip under mount | Take the back through technical mount; the deep collar grip is already a [bow and arrow](./bow-and-arrow.md) grip |
| Duck the head down and drive forward | [Loop choke](./loop-choke.md) — their posture-down is exactly what that choke wants |
| Trap the deep-grip elbow to their ribs | Switch to the [Ezekiel](./ezekiel.md) with the free arm; the trapped elbow is already behind their head |
| Bridge violently from bottom mount | Ride to technical mount and keep the collar grip; the choke follows the back take |

Feeding in: the cross collar is the natural follow-on from a scissor sweep (you already have collar and sleeve), from any mount where they turn in, and from a stalled guard-break where they have committed both hands to your knees.

## 7. Defending it

**Early — by far the best time.** Fight the grip, not the choke. Keep your own collar closed by pinning your lapels down with your elbows, keep your hands inside theirs in the grip exchange, and break the *first* grip before the second one exists. One-handed cross collar chokes do not finish. Attack their gripping thumb, or grip their sleeve with both hands and drive their elbow away from their own ribs — once that elbow floats, the grip is dead.

**Middle — both grips in, not yet tight.** Turn your chin toward the elbow that is *deeper*, not away from it; turning into the deep side shortens their lever. Get your shoulder up to your ear on that side to jam the space. Then create distance with your hips, not your hands: bridge and shrimp so your neck moves away from the fixed leash. Frame on their bicep — the bicep, not the wrist. Do not try to peel two grips with two hands; peel one, and only one.

**Late — both grips in and tight.** This is a bad place and you should not plan to live here. There is no reliable late defense against a properly finished cross collar; the honest options are to explode into a position change (turn hard into the deep grip and try to come up) or to tap. Late defense against a blood choke is worse than early defense in a way that is not true for joint locks — the failure mode is not pain, it is unconsciousness, and it happens quickly and without much warning. **Tap.**

## 8. Drills

**1. Grip-only, no finish (3 × 2 minutes).** From closed guard, your only goal is to establish a legitimate deep first grip with your elbow pinned. Their only goal is to prevent it. No chokes, no sweeps. This drills the feeding motion in step 2, which is the step that actually decides the outcome.

**2. Body-type ladder (10 reps each).** Drill the same choke on three partners: the biggest neck in the room, the smallest, and someone your own size. Ten slow reps each with a verbal note after each set on what you had to change. This is the fastest way to internalise §3 — reading it is not the same as feeling the trap ridge stop your hand.

**3. Gi swap (2 × 5 minutes).** Do the same drill with them in the stiffest gi in the gym, then again in the softest, oldest one. Same partner, same choke, two completely different techniques. Nothing else teaches the gi-fit adjustments as fast.

**4. Positional round — mount, choke or nothing (90-second rounds).** Start mounted with one collar grip already established. Top player may only attack the cross collar and its direct chains; bottom player escapes normally. Reset every 90 seconds.

---

## 9. Safety

A blood choke works quickly and without much warning. Applied properly, they can go from "uncomfortable" to unconscious in a few seconds, and unlike a joint lock there is no escalating pain signal to warn them. **Apply steadily, not explosively, and release the instant they tap — all the way, both hands.** Do not hold on for an extra second to "make the point."

Be alert for taps you cannot see. With both of your hands on their collar and their arms often trapped, their tap may be a foot on the mat or a verbal call. If you have their head buried in your chest from mount, you may not hear a quiet verbal tap either — check.

If your grips are on the windpipe rather than the carotids, you are doing a painful and slightly risky air choke on the cartilage rather than a clean blood choke. That is worth fixing for their sake as much as yours. Never crank their head forward against a stalled choke as a substitute for the strangle — that is a neck crank, it is banned by IBJJF at every belt, and it is a genuinely dangerous habit.

Legality is simple here: it is a strangle, it is legal at every IBJJF belt from white up, and it is legal in every major gi ruleset. It is gi-only for the obvious reason — no cloth, no choke.
