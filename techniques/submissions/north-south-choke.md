# North–South Choke

> Not a squeeze. Their neck sits in the crook of your elbow and your shoulder is driven onto the other side of it by your bodyweight and a shrug — which is why it works when your arms are exhausted.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) — gi and no-gi |
| **Comes from** | North–south, side control, kesa gatame, a failed guillotine, a stuffed shot |
| **Difficulty** | 4/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** NS choke, north–south strangle

---

## 1. The mechanism

**Read this carefully, because almost everyone gets it wrong the first fifty times.** This is not an arm squeeze. If you are curling your bicep, the choke will not finish and your arm will be dead in twenty seconds.

Their neck sits in the V of your elbow. The two compressing surfaces are your **bicep and the mass of your shoulder** on one side of their neck, and your **forearm** on the other. Both carotids are attacked at once — the same target as any blood choke, the soft triangle beside the windpipe.

Closing the V is done by three things, none of which are your arm:

1. **A shrug.** You drive your choking shoulder up toward your own ear. That pushes the deep corner of the V — the elbow crook and the shoulder mass behind it — down and forward into the near carotid. This is the single most important input and the one people leave out.
2. **Hip position.** You slide your knees back away from their head and sprawl your hips low. That drops your chest, and your bodyweight lands on the shoulder that is already buried in their neck. If your hips are high and your knees are tucked underneath you, there is no way for weight to arrive and your arm has to do everything.
3. **Rotation.** You turn your chest toward the side where your shoulder is buried. That rotation is what carries your shoulder over the carotid rather than resting on their collarbone — and how far you rotate depends almost entirely on how wide their shoulders are. See §3, because that is where this technique actually lives.

Because the power source is bodyweight and skeletal position rather than muscular effort, this is one of the few submissions that gets *easier* deep into a hard round. It also means it is slow to build and near-impossible to escape once it is genuinely on — hence the atlas's 4/5.

---

## 2. The standard application

From north–south against a same-size opponent, gi or no-gi.

1. **Settle north–south properly first.** Chest on their chest, your head over their belly, your knees in tight to their shoulders so they cannot shrimp. If you are not heavy here, nothing below will work.
2. **Bring your choking arm down the front of their throat**, palm down, so the crook of your elbow catches under their jaw and their neck settles into the V. Your bicep is now on one side of their neck, your forearm on the other.
3. **Drive that hand deep** — travel it toward their far shoulder blade, not just their far armpit. **Depth of the hand is what lifts the elbow crook off their collarbone and onto their jawline.** A shallow hand puts the V on their chest.
4. **Connect the other arm.** Either grip your own choking-arm bicep, or thread your free hand under their far armpit and grip your own hands together. The free arm's job is to stop the V from opening, not to add squeeze.
5. **Put your head on the mat past their far ear.** This tripods you, loads your shoulder, and stops them turning their head away.
6. **Slide your knees back and sprawl your hips down.** Your chest drops, your weight transfers forward into the buried shoulder. **This is the step people skip and it is where the choking pressure comes from.**
7. **Shrug the choking shoulder toward your ear** and rotate your chest toward that side. Hold. Do not curl.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters, and this technique varies by body type more than any other choke in this folder.** The reason is simple: your two compressing surfaces are a fixed size — the V of your elbow and the width of your shoulder — and they have to fit around a neck and past a shoulder girdle whose dimensions change enormously from person to person. Their **shoulder width in particular changes your finishing angle by 30–60 degrees**, which is not a refinement, it is a different technique.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Broad shoulders / thick traps** | Your shoulder lands on their deltoid and never reaches the neck | Rotate much further toward the choking side — up to chest-facing-their-far-shoulder; drop your hips lower; drive the hand to the far *shoulder blade* so the elbow crook rides up onto the jaw |
| **Narrow shoulders** | The standard rotation over-rotates you off the top and they escape out the back | Stay squarer — barely 20–30° — and drive straight down; the shoulder already reaches the neck without rotation |
| Thick neck | Their neck is bigger than the V of your elbow; it will not seat | Swap surfaces: use the forearm blade across the throat rather than the crook, or take an **arm-in** version so their own shoulder becomes one of the compressing surfaces |
| Thin neck | Head slides out of the V before you load it | Block the head with your free forearm or your knee; drive the hand deeper so the V narrows around the neck |
| Long torso | Your knees cannot reach their shoulders while your chest stays on their chest | Sprawl the legs back further and load through your head; take the choke from a slightly angled, kesa-like line instead |
| Much heavier than you | They bridge you off before the position is built | Hips lower and wider, take the choke from an angled north–south so their bridge travels past you rather than into you |
| Much lighter than you | They shrimp and spin out during the build | Pin the near hip with a knee before the arm ever commits |
| Very stiff / muscular | Traps shrug up and armour the carotid | Pull their far shoulder *down* with your free hand under their armpit before you shrug yours in |
| Very flexible | They turn the head far enough to slip the V | Block the far ear with your ribs and your head on the mat before you tighten |
| No gi / heavy sweat | The arm slides and depth is lost under load | Lock hand-to-bicep rather than relying on friction; re-grip rather than re-squeeze |
| Gi | Extra option most people never use | Grip their far lapel with the choking hand to lock depth permanently |

---

### Broad shoulders and thick traps — the defining variable

This is the build that decides whether you own this choke or just know it.

The problem is precise. Your compressing surface on the near side is your shoulder and the mass behind it. On a narrow-shouldered person, that shoulder drops straight onto the side of the neck. On someone with broad shoulders and a thick trapezius, your shoulder lands on their **deltoid** — a rounded, padded, load-bearing lump that is two or three inches lateral of the artery you are trying to close. You will feel enormous pressure. They will feel their shoulder being squashed and their neck perfectly fine.

Three changes, all of them geometric:

**(a) Rotate much further.** The standard version has your chest roughly over their chest with maybe 20–30° of turn. Against a broad-shouldered opponent you need to keep turning — up toward 60–90°, until your chest is facing their far shoulder and your ribs are over the top of their head. That rotation walks your shoulder off their deltoid and into the notch between their jaw and their collarbone, which is exactly where the carotid is. Do it by walking your knees around their head toward the far side, not by twisting your spine.

**(b) Drop your hips lower.** Rotation alone puts your shoulder over the right spot; only weight puts it *into* the right spot. Slide your knees back further than you would on a smaller opponent and sprawl your hips down toward the mat, so your chest is closer to horizontal. On a broad frame you have further to descend before you make contact, and if your hips stay high you never get there.

**(c) Drive the hand to the far shoulder blade, not the far armpit.** This is the detail that changes where the V sits. A hand that stops at their far armpit leaves the crook of your elbow resting on their collarbone — on a broad-chested opponent that is the widest, hardest, least compressible place available. Push the hand another 10–15 cm to their far shoulder blade and the whole arm rotates: the crook of your elbow climbs off the collarbone and settles under the jawline, where the neck actually is.

If all three are set and it still will not close, their neck may simply be bigger than your elbow crook — go to the arm-in version below.

### Narrow shoulders and small frames

The mirror error, and it is the more common one among people who have just learned the broad-shoulder fix and started applying it universally.

On a narrow-shouldered opponent your shoulder already reaches the neck from square. If you rotate to 60–90° here, you rotate yourself off the top of them entirely: your chest comes off their chest, you post on your knees to stay balanced, and they shrimp out into the space you created. Stay **square — 20–30° at most — and drive straight down.** The vertical component is all you need, and it costs you nothing in position.

Narrow-shouldered opponents also tend to have less to block the head, so pair this with the thin-neck adjustment below.

### Thick neck

There is a hard limit on this choke: the V of your elbow has a fixed maximum, and some necks are bigger than it. On those people the neck never seats in the crook — it rests on the *outside* of your bicep, and you end up compressing their face.

Two honest answers.

**Swap the compressing surface.** Instead of seating the neck in the elbow crook, lay the **blade of your forearm** — the ulnar edge — straight across the front and side of their throat, with your shoulder driving the other side, and finish it more like a head-and-shoulder compression than a wrap. You lose the two-sided closure of the V but you gain a hard bone edge on one carotid, and combined with your shoulder mass on the other side it finishes.

**Take the arm in.** Bring one of their arms up beside their own head and inside your wrap, so the neck-plus-arm sits in your V. This sounds like it makes the problem worse — a bigger thing to encircle — but it does not, because **their own shoulder becomes a compressing surface**, driven into their own carotid exactly as in an arm triangle. Against a thick neck, arm-in is not a variation, it is the correct version.

### Thin neck

The head is the problem, not the neck. A small head in a large V slides straight out the front when you start to load it, and you end up with your arm around their face.

**Block the escape route before you tighten.** Your free forearm across their forehead, or your near knee brought up beside their ear, stops the head travelling. Then drive the choking hand deeper so the V narrows and there is no room for the head to move within it. Finish with less rotation and more depth.

### Long torso

A long-torsoed opponent puts their shoulders further from their hips than your kneeling stance is long. You end up with your chest on their chest but your knees somewhere near their ribs — which means they can shrimp freely and you have no way to lower your hips without losing chest contact.

Sprawl your legs back much further and take the load through your **head** on the mat past their ear, using it as a genuine third leg. Alternatively, do not fight the geometry: take the choke from an angled, kesa-gatame-like line — sitting slightly off to one side rather than square north–south — where the distance from your hips to their neck is shorter.

### Much heavier than you

The choke itself scales well across weight — it is bodyweight and structure, not strength. The *position* does not. A much heavier person bridges out of north–south before you have finished building anything.

**Angle out of true north–south.** Sit slightly off their centreline, toward the side your choking arm is coming from, so their bridge travels past your body instead of directly into your chest. **Widen and lower your base** — knees wider than shoulders, hips low, toes dug in — so there is nothing to tip. And accept a slower build: on a heavier opponent, get the hand deep and the head planted *before* you attempt to shrug, because once you commit your shoulder you have no way to re-base.

### Much lighter than you

They will not defend the choke; they will leave. A lighter opponent shrimps sideways out from under north–south in the time it takes you to set the arm.

Pin first, choke second. Bring your near knee tight against their near hip or shoulder — a physical block against the direction they shrimp — before your arm ever crosses their throat. Then build. Also mind the finish: this choke is heavy and slow to release once your bodyweight is committed, and a smaller partner may be unable to move their arms to tap.

### Very stiff or very muscular

A strong opponent's defence to this is a symmetrical double shrug — both shoulders driven to their ears, sealing the neck between them. It genuinely works, and pushing harder into it does nothing.

Break the symmetry: thread your free hand under their **far** armpit and pull that shoulder *down* toward their own hip. One shoulder pulled down destroys the seal, exposes the far side of the neck, and lets your buried shoulder descend on the near side. Do this before you shrug, not after.

### Very flexible

Flexible opponents get their chin past your bicep and turn their head far enough to open the near carotid. Their head becomes a wedge inside your V rather than a thing being compressed by it.

Take away the rotation before you take away the space: put your head on the mat past their far ear and clamp your ribs against the far side of their head, so the head cannot turn in the direction it wants to. Then tighten.

### Gi versus no-gi

The atlas rates this as working in both, and it does — but the mechanics differ.

**No-gi:** sweat is the enemy. Depth achieved in the first second slides away in the third, and your hand-to-bicep grip lubricates and pops. Do not respond by squeezing harder; **re-grip.** Take the depth again, then reconnect. Expect to re-set two or three times in a long attack, and keep your head on the mat throughout so re-gripping does not cost you the position.

**Gi:** you have an option most people never use — grip **their far lapel** with your choking hand once it is deep. That locks the depth permanently: no amount of sweat, bridging or shrugging shortens your arm once it is anchored to their jacket. It converts the hardest-to-maintain part of this choke into a fixed structure. If you train in the gi and you are not doing this, start.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Your V is small and your reach to their far shoulder blade is limited. Prioritise the arm-in version as your default rather than your backup, and get the rotation right — angle buys you what reach cannot.

**Long arms.** Depth is easy and it will tempt you into a shallow, wrapping choke where the crook of your elbow never seats. Deliberately drive the hand past the far shoulder blade and check that your elbow crook is under their jaw, not on their chest.

**Narrow shoulders (yours).** Your shoulder is a smaller compressing surface, so you need more depth and more weight to compensate. Lower hips, more rotation, head planted.

**Broad shoulders (yours).** You have the ideal build for this choke. Your problem is the opposite — your shoulder is so wide it can rest on their collarbone and feel tight without ever touching the neck. Check the position, not the pressure.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** This is a good submission for you, because the power source is position rather than strength. The cost is that holding north–south on someone heavier is hard. Angle off their centreline, keep your base wide and low, and build the choke in a fixed order — depth, head, hips, shrug — rather than grabbing and hoping.

**Heavier than most of your partners.** Go slowly and be careful. Your bodyweight arrives on a small neck very fast, their arms are usually trapped, and they may not be able to tap with a hand.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Your bicep is burning and nothing is happening | You are curling your arm instead of shrugging and dropping weight | Stop squeezing; shrug the shoulder to your ear, slide your knees back, drop your chest |
| Huge pressure, they are comfortable | Your shoulder is on their deltoid, not their neck | Rotate further toward the choking side and drop your hips lower |
| You rotate and slide off the top | You over-rotated on a narrow-shouldered opponent | Stay square, 20–30°, and drive down instead |
| The V rests on their chest, not their jaw | Hand is too shallow | Drive the hand to their far shoulder blade, not their far armpit |
| Their neck will not seat in your elbow | Neck is bigger than your elbow crook | Go arm-in, or swap to the forearm blade across the throat |
| Their head slides out the front | Thin neck, nothing blocking the head | Free forearm across the forehead or knee beside the ear before you tighten |
| They shrug and seal both sides | Symmetrical shoulder defence | Pull their far shoulder down with your free hand under the armpit first |
| Depth keeps slipping away | No-gi sweat | Re-grip rather than re-squeeze; in the gi, anchor to their far lapel |
| They bridge you over the top | Base too high and too square | Angle off their centreline; knees wide, hips low, toes dug in |
| They shrimp out during the setup | You committed the arm before pinning the hips | Near knee to their hip first, then the arm |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Push your head away with both hands | Kimura or armbar from north–south — both arms are extended and available |
| Shrug both shoulders and seal the neck | Arm-in version of this same choke, or switch to a straight arm attack on the near arm |
| Turn to their side to escape | Follow to side control and take a paper-cutter or arm triangle |
| Frame on your hips and shrimp | Ride to knee-on-belly and re-settle; or take the [Ezekiel](./ezekiel.md) from the kesa line |
| Bridge hard | Ride the bridge and switch to the far side; the choking arm is already deep for the other side |
| They hold a guillotine on you from bottom | Von Flue choke — the same shoulder-pressure principle, punishing the same mistake |
| Turtle up rather than staying flat | Front headlock, then the [Peruvian necktie](./peruvian-necktie.md) or a back take |

Feeding in: the north–south choke is the natural reward for a stuffed shot, for a failed guillotine attempt on their part, and for anyone who turns their face toward you from bottom side control. Getting to north–south is the hard part; the atlas rates north–south itself at 2/5 to hold and this choke at 4/5 to finish, which is an honest summary of where the difficulty lives.

## 7. Defending it

**Early.** Do not let north–south settle in the first place — that is genuinely the whole defence. From bottom side control, keep your near elbow tight and your chin tucked toward your own chest, and turn your face **toward** them, not away. When you feel them start to walk to your head, immediately turn onto your side and frame on their hips before their chest arrives. See [north–south escapes](../escapes/north-south-escapes.md).

**Middle — arm across your throat, not yet loaded.** Two things, both urgent. **Shrug both shoulders to your ears** to seal the sides of your neck — that really does work against a shoulder that has not yet dropped into position, and it buys you seconds. At the same time, get both hands on their choking-arm elbow and push it *toward their own head*, lengthening the V, rather than pulling it down. Then move your hips: shrimp your hips away hard so your neck comes out of the V rather than trying to remove the V from your neck.

**Late — shoulder buried, hips dropped, shrug on.** This is the point of the technique. It is called inescapable-when-tight for a reason: there is no lever left, their weight is on your carotid, and there is nothing to pry. The only thing worth trying is an explosive bridge to one side to disrupt their base before it settles fully — and that is a matter of a second or two, not a plan. **Late defense here is dramatically worse than early defense**, more so than with almost any other choke, because the pressure comes from bodyweight rather than muscle and it does not fatigue. Tap.

## 8. Drills

**1. Pressure-only, no choke (3 × 2 minutes).** Hold north–south. Their goal is to escape, yours is only to stay. No submissions. Everything about this choke depends on being able to hold the position long enough to build it, and most people cannot.

**2. The four-step build (10 slow reps).** From settled north–south, build the choke saying the steps out loud: *depth — head — hips — shrug.* No finishing, just the build, then release. The order is the technique. Doing it out of order is the most common reason it never works.

**3. Shoulder-width ladder (10 reps each).** Same choke on the widest-shouldered partner in the gym, the narrowest, and someone your size. Change only the rotation angle and note where your shoulder lands each time. This drill teaches the single most important variable in §3 faster than any amount of reading.

**4. Positional round — north–south, 90 seconds.** Start settled in north–south, both players' hands on the mat. Top attacks the choke and its chains; bottom escapes. Reset every 90 seconds. Alternate who starts on top.

---

## 9. Safety

This choke is slow to arrive and slow to release, which is a specific hazard. Your bodyweight is committed forward and down onto their neck, so "letting go" takes a beat longer than it does with a hand-powered choke. **Plan for that: apply progressively, and when they tap, lift your shoulder and your hips off them before you unwrap your arm.**

Their arms are very often trapped under your chest, and their face is buried, so a hand tap may be impossible and a verbal tap may be muffled. Before you start hunting north–south chokes on a new partner, agree that a foot tapping the mat or any audible sound counts. Then listen for it.

When it is misaligned, this choke crushes the jaw, the mandible and the front of the throat rather than the arteries, which is painful and pointless. If they are grimacing and going nowhere, re-aim rather than lean harder.

Legality: it is a strangle. Legal at every IBJJF belt and in every major ruleset, gi and no-gi. There is no belt restriction and no ruleset issue — the only caution is that if you finish it as a face crush or a jaw crank rather than a strangle, you have drifted into territory that IBJJF bans at every belt and that no partner will thank you for.
