# Omoplata

> A shoulder lock applied with your leg instead of your arm — and, more often than it finishes, a sweep and a control position that keeps paying whether or not they tap.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Joint lock (shoulder — internal rotation, leg-powered) · also a sweep · also a control position |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, high guard, rubber guard, De La Riva, spider and lasso guards, failed triangle or armbar |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** omo, shoulder lock with the leg, coil lock. *Omoplata* is
Portuguese for **shoulder blade**.

---

## 1. The mechanism

The omoplata is a [kimura](./kimura.md) powered by your hip instead of your
shoulder. Their arm is trapped between your legs with the elbow bent; your thigh
lies across the back of their upper arm and shoulder; and when you sit up and
rotate your hips toward their head, their humerus is driven into **internal
rotation** — hand toward their feet, elbow forced up and away from their back.
The load lands on the same structures the kimura attacks: the shoulder capsule
and the rotator cuff, with the elbow sometimes taking a share.

Your leg is a far stronger lever than your arm, which is why this hurts people
who shrug off a kimura. It is also why it hurts people faster than you intend.

Three things have to be true:

- **Their elbow stays bent and their forearm points away from you.** A straight
  arm slides out of the leg triangle.
- **Their hips are blocked.** The single most common escape is a forward
  somersault, and it is available the entire time your hand is not on their belt,
  their far hip, or their near hip.
- **You have to sit up.** Finishing flat on your back gives away most of the
  leverage and lets them roll. The finish is your hips moving toward their head,
  which cannot happen if your shoulders are still on the mat.

**It is also a sweep and a control position.** When they resist the rotation
they must post and turn, which tips them; when they defend the sweep they feed
the rotation. Even a completely failed omoplata usually ends with you sitting up,
their posture wrecked and their arm trapped — that is a better position than the
one you started in. Treat the tap as one of three acceptable outcomes.

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard, against someone your size.

1. Break their posture and get an angle — open the guard and shrimp your hips out
   to one side, roughly 45 degrees, so your body is turning perpendicular to
   theirs.
2. Trap the near arm: control their wrist and **drag their arm down and across so
   their forearm points away from your body**. If their hand ends up pointing
   back toward you, the arm will straighten and escape.
3. Swing your same-side leg over their shoulder and their trapped upper arm. Your
   other foot goes to their hip or their far shoulder to stop them stepping in.
4. **Triangle your legs** (or at minimum cross your ankles) above their trapped
   shoulder so the arm cannot slide free.
5. **Sit up onto your near hip** and turn your torso to face their legs. This is
   the step people skip and the step the technique depends on.
6. **Hug across their lower back** — belt, far hip, or a clasp around the waist.
   This is your roll block, not an optional grip.
7. Slide your hips 10–15 cm toward their head and lean your chest forward over
   your own knee. That hip travel, not the lean, is the finish. Slowly.

The two bolded steps — sit up, and block the hips — are the difference between
an omoplata and lying underneath somebody with your leg on their arm.

### Position-specific notes

| Entry | What changes |
|---|---|
| **From a failed triangle** | They posture and pull the arm out — your leg is already over the shoulder. Swing straight into the omoplata. |
| **From a failed armbar** | They stack you; as your leg comes free, thread it over the shoulder instead of fighting the stack. |
| **From spider / lasso** 🥋 | The lasso leg is already wrapped — you are one hip movement away. |
| **From De La Riva** 🥋 | Common entry as they try to free the hook; you end up under their arm. |
| **From the top (reverse omoplata)** | Same shoulder rotation applied from side control or the crucifix. Different setup, identical mechanism — and identical safety rules. |

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The omoplata is a shoulder rotation lock like the [kimura](./kimura.md), so
shoulder mobility dominates everything else. But it has a second variable the
kimura doesn't: **you are using a leg**, so the distances involved are your leg
length against their torso and arm length. Get that geometry wrong and your thigh
lands on their bicep instead of their shoulder, which controls nothing.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Very flexible shoulders** | Their hand goes to the middle of their own back without discomfort, and they will simply forward-roll out rather than tap. | Lift the elbow off their back, drive your hips toward their **head** rather than down, and pin the shoulder blade with your thigh. Then set up for the roll — see the follow below. |
| **Stiff shoulders** | End range arrives before you have finished sitting up. Your leg is far stronger than their shoulder. | Apply in centimetres of hip travel, not degrees of lean. They also can't roll out, so take the control and go slowly. |
| **Thick arms, big shoulders, heavy traps** | Your leg lands on their bicep rather than across their shoulder — no rotation, no control. | Pull the trapped arm 10 cm further through past your hip before you clamp, so your hamstring — not your knee — sits on their shoulder. Then triangle the legs; a big deltoid slides out of crossed ankles. |
| **Thin arms** | The arm slides straight out of the leg triangle. | Grip their wrist with your free hand and keep the forearm pointed away; triangle the legs rather than crossing the ankles. |
| **Long arms** | They post the trapped hand on the mat, grab their own belt, or reach back and grip your leg. | Pin their trapped wrist to their own lower back with your free hand, and clear any grip on your leg *before* you sit up. |
| **Short arms** | Less lever for you, but their defensive options collapse — they can't reach their own belt or your leg. | Work close and drive with your hips, not your torso. Slow down: a short arm reaches its limit sooner than you'll expect. |
| **Much heavier than you** | They posture up, flatten you and step over before you sit up. | Get the angle first and sit up immediately — the sit-up is the priority, not the leg. Use it as a sweep; their weight is the sweeping force. |
| **Much lighter than you** | They roll out fast, often twice, and their shoulder reaches end range early. | Block the hips hard, expect the roll and drill the follow. Apply at half speed. |
| **Long torso** | When you sit up, their far hip is further away than your arm reaches, so your roll block never arrives. | Grip their belt or near hip instead of reaching for the far side, and put your head against their back. |

### Very flexible shoulders — and the roll-out you have to follow

This is where the omoplata earns its 3/5 rating, and where most people's version
of it quietly stops working.

A shoulder with a lot of internal rotation can accept the omoplata's finishing
position as a stretch. Worse, that same flexibility is what makes the standard
escape — the forward somersault over the trapped shoulder — available and
comfortable. So the flexible opponent gets both: they don't tap, *and* they can
leave. Cranking harder on your leg is the wrong answer twice over, because your
leg has enough strength to injure a shoulder well before you have any feedback
that you're close.

**Adjust the geometry first.**

1. **Lift their elbow off their own back.** An elbow lying flat against their
   spine is a flexible person's resting position — the joint has nowhere further
   to rotate because it has already arrived. Reach under with your free hand and
   pick the elbow up 10 cm. That single change restores the rotation you thought
   you had.
2. **Drive your hips toward their head, not toward the mat.** Leaning forward
   compresses them; hip travel rotates them. Move your hips 15–20 cm past their
   shoulder line so the rotation vector runs along their spine rather than into
   it. The finish is a hip walk.
3. **Pin the shoulder blade.** Sit up until your thigh crosses their scapula and
   your chest is over your own knee. If their shoulder blade can slide, they will
   turn the whole shoulder girdle and absorb everything you apply.
4. **Take the slack out of the wrist.** Their forearm is right there and their
   wrist is not hypermobile just because their shoulder is. See
   [wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md) — legal from blue belt under IBJJF.

**Then expect the roll, and follow it.** Against a flexible opponent, assume the
somersault is coming and be ready. There are three good answers, in order of
preference:

- **Follow the roll.** As they somersault, keep your legs triangled and roll with
  them over your own shoulder in the same direction. You arrive sitting on top
  with the same lock — the arm never leaves your legs and their back is now flat.
  This is the standard answer and it is worth more drilling than the finish
  itself.
- **Take the back.** For a beat mid-roll, they are turned away with one arm
  trapped and no ability to defend their neck. Release the legs, grip the
  waist and ride to back control. Against opponents who roll instantly, this is
  often the better outcome than chasing the shoulder.
- **Switch to the [triangle](./triangle-choke.md) or [armbar](./armbar.md).** As
  they roll, their head and far arm come toward you. Their arm is bent for the
  omoplata and straight for the armbar — take whichever they hand you.

And if you refuse to follow the roll, you lose the position outright: they finish
the somersault, come up on top and you are in bottom side control. **Deciding in
advance which follow you're taking is the technique.**

### Stiff shoulders

- **Very little travel is available**, and your leg supplies enormous force. This
  is one of the highest-risk combinations in the fundamentals syllabus: a strong
  lever against a joint with almost no range.
- **Apply by hip centimetres.** Sit up fully, take the slack out, then move your
  hips a few centimetres at a time.
- **They can't roll out well** — the somersault needs shoulder range they don't
  have — so the position is very sticky. Take the control and stop reaching for
  the finish.

### Thick arms, big shoulders, heavy traps

- **Your leg lands in the wrong place.** On a heavily built opponent, swinging
  your leg over lands your knee on their bicep and your hamstring in the air.
  Nothing is on the shoulder, so nothing rotates.
- **Pull the arm further through.** Grip their wrist and drag the whole arm 10 cm
  past your own hip before you clamp, so your **hamstring** sits across the back
  of their shoulder. If you can feel their deltoid against the back of your
  thigh, you're right.
- **Triangle the legs, don't cross the ankles.** A big deltoid slips out of
  crossed ankles the moment you sit up.
- **A strong lat resists the rotation.** Lifting the elbow is the counter — it
  puts the arm where the lat cannot pull.

### Thin arms

- **The arm slides out.** There is not enough limb to fill your leg triangle.
- **Grip the wrist with your free hand** and keep their forearm pointing away
  from you, so the bend in the elbow does the retention work.
- **Triangle the legs** and keep your knees pinched together as you sit up.
- Expect a fast tap — less mass around the joint, less warning.

### Long arms

- **They post.** A long trapped arm can reach the mat behind them and post,
  killing the rotation and starting the roll. Pin their trapped wrist to their
  own lower back with your free hand.
- **They anchor.** They can reach their own belt or lapel, or reach *back* and
  grip your leg or your gi. Clear grips on your leg before you sit up — once
  you're sitting up, both your hands are busy.
- **You have a long lever.** A long humerus turns a small hip movement into a
  large rotation. Reduce your travel accordingly.

### Short arms

- **Their defensive options largely disappear.** They cannot reach their own
  belt, cannot post far behind them, and cannot reach your leg. If you get the
  clamp, you keep it.
- **You have less lever**, so drive with your hips rather than leaning with your
  chest — the hip walk toward their head is the whole finish here.
- **Go slower, not faster.** A short arm arrives at its limit with less warning
  travel.

### Much heavier than you

- **The sit-up is the whole fight.** A heavy opponent who is still upright and
  based when your leg goes over will flatten you, walk around your leg and pass.
  Get the angle first, then the leg, then sit up in one continuous movement.
- **Use it as a sweep.** This is the honest answer against a big opponent: their
  weight, once you have their shoulder pinned and their posture broken forward,
  goes over the top very easily. Sweep, come up, and re-attack the shoulder from
  the top if you want it.
- **Never hold a heavy person's shoulder with a straight leg.** Curl your heel
  toward your own backside so your hamstring is engaged.
- **Watch your own knee.** If they step over and turn while your leg is triangled
  around their arm, the torque goes into your knee. Release rather than defend.

### Much lighter than you

- **They roll immediately, and they roll again.** Light, mobile opponents treat
  the somersault as their first response, not their last. Block the hips
  aggressively and have the follow chosen before you sit up.
- **They can also pull the arm out** — less limb, more space. Triangle the legs.
- **Their shoulder gets there first.** Halve your speed and stop at the first
  sign that the joint has stopped moving freely.

### Long torso

- When you sit up and reach across for their far hip, a long-torsoed opponent's
  hip is simply not where your hand expects it. You end up reaching, which costs
  you the sit-up posture that makes the whole technique work.
- **Grip their belt or their near hip instead**, and put your head against their
  lower back for the roll block. Contact beats reach.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**If your legs are short:** getting your thigh across a taller opponent's
shoulder is genuinely harder — your knee arrives where their bicep is. Pull the
arm further through with your hands before you clamp, and prefer entries where
the leg is already over (failed triangle, lasso) rather than swinging it in from
scratch.

**If your hips are stiff:** the omoplata asks for a lot of external rotation at
your own hip. If that isn't there yet, the finish will feel like it's happening
in your hip rather than their shoulder. Use the sit-up-and-sweep version rather
than the finish, and work hip mobility separately — this one improves quickly.

**If you are much lighter than your partners:** the omoplata is one of the best
attacks available to you, because your leg beats their arm regardless of weight.
But the sit-up must be immediate — you cannot afford to lie underneath a bigger
opponent with your leg committed. Angle, leg, sit up, all as one movement.

**If you are much heavier than your partners:** your leg against a smaller
partner's shoulder is a large mismatch. Use your hands to apply the last part of
the rotation instead of your hips, so you can feel what you're doing, and stop
early.

**If your back is long or your core is weak:** sitting up from a supine position
with a person attached to your leg is the hardest physical demand on this page.
Post on your near elbow first, then to your hand, then up — don't try to crunch
straight to seated.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Leg is over, but nothing is controlled | Your knee is on their bicep, not your hamstring on their shoulder | Pull the arm 10 cm further through past your hip, then clamp |
| They somersault out every time | Their hips were never blocked | Belt or near-hip grip before you finish — and drill the follow-roll |
| You're applying it and they look comfortable | Their elbow is flat on their back; the rotation has already ended | Lift the elbow 10 cm and drive your hips toward their head |
| The arm slides out | Ankles crossed instead of triangled, or their forearm is pointing back at you | Triangle the legs; turn their forearm away from your body |
| You can't sit up | You never got the angle — you're square underneath them | Shrimp out 45 degrees first; the angle comes before the leg |
| It becomes a sweep every time | Nothing wrong — that's an outcome, not a failure | Take the top position and re-attack from there |
| They posture and step over your leg | You were flat on your back too long | Sit up immediately; if they step over, release rather than let your knee take the torque |
| You finish it and their elbow hurts, not their shoulder | The arm straightened during the rotation | Reassess — a straight arm is an armbar, and the mechanics differ |

## 6. Chains

### Their defense → your next attack

| Their defense | Your next attack | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Forward somersault | Follow the roll and keep the lock, or release and take the back | They turn away with one arm trapped |
| Posture up and pull the arm out | [Triangle](./triangle-choke.md) — their head is between your legs already | The same angle serves both |
| Post the trapped hand on the mat | Pin the wrist to their back, then re-rotate | Their post is the only thing holding them |
| Grab their own belt | Break the grip, or sweep instead of finishing | Their hand is committed and their base is not |
| Step over toward you | Sweep to the top; or switch to a leg entanglement as they step | Their stepping leg is unbased |
| Flatten you out | Sit up and take the sweep, or re-guard | You still have the arm |
| Flexible shoulder, no tap | [Kimura](./kimura.md) on the same arm, [wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md), or take the back | Same rotation, different joint or different lever |

### What feeds into it

| From | The trigger |
|---|---|
| Failed [triangle](./triangle-choke.md) | They posture and strip the arm out — your leg is already over |
| Failed [armbar](./armbar.md) | They stack; your freed leg goes over the shoulder |
| Closed / high guard | They post a hand past your hip |
| Lasso or spider guard 🥋 | The wrap is already there |
| De La Riva 🥋 | They fight the hook and end up under your leg |
| Top side control | The reverse omoplata — same rotation, applied from above |

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is dramatically better than late defense** — and unusually for a
submission, the atlas rates the *early* answer and the *late* answer as the same
technique done at different times: "posture up and step over toward them before
the shoulder loads, or roll forward through it."

**Early — before the leg is over.** Posture. An omoplata needs your posture
broken and your arm reaching past their hip. Keep your elbows connected to your
ribs and never leave a hand posted on the mat beside their hip in closed guard.
If their hips start shrimping out to one side, square back up — the angle is the
attack.

**Middle — the leg is over, they haven't sat up.** Step over toward them: bring
your outside foot across so you are standing over their body, then drive your
trapped shoulder forward and down. Simultaneously get your **posture up and your
trapped elbow tight to your ribs**. If you can get your free hand to the mat past
their head, do it — it stops the sit-up, which stops everything.

**Late — they are sitting up, hips blocked, your shoulder is loading.** The
forward somersault is your escape: tuck your trapped shoulder, look toward your
own far hip and roll over the trapped shoulder, straight through. Commit fully —
a half-hearted roll leaves you in the same position with less structure. Two
honest warnings: if they have your hips blocked with an arm or their head, the
roll is not available; and if their hips have already travelled past your
shoulder line, the rotation is on and rolling into it can hurt you more, not
less.

**Tap for the shoulder.** Your leg-versus-their-shoulder is an unfair trade, and
they may not feel how close it is. Shoulder injuries heal slowly and imperfectly.

## 8. Drills

**1. Angle, leg, sit-up (20 per side, no resistance).**
From closed guard: open, shrimp out 45 degrees, arm across, leg over, triangle,
**sit up onto your hip and hug the belt**. Stop there — do not finish. The rep is
everything up to the sit-up, because that is where the technique lives.

**2. The roll-follow (15 per side, cooperative).**
Set the omoplata; your partner deliberately somersaults out at 70% speed; you
follow the roll and arrive on top with the lock intact. Then swap and let them
practise rolling. This drill is why flexible opponents stop escaping you.

**3. Elbow-lift comparison (5 minutes, no reps to tap).**
Set the position on a flexible partner. Apply once with their elbow flat on
their back, once with it lifted 10 cm. Ask them which one they felt. This is the
adjustment most people never learn and it takes one round to internalise.

**4. Positional sparring — omoplata established (90-second rounds).**
Start with your leg over and triangled, them on their knees. Their goal: roll,
posture out, or step over. Yours: sit up, block the hips, finish or sweep.
Score the sweep as a win — it usually is.

## 9. Safety

- **Your leg is much stronger than their shoulder.** This is the core safety fact
  of the omoplata. You can pass a shoulder's end range without your body giving
  you meaningful feedback that you did.
- **Apply by hip travel, in small increments**, and stop the moment the rotation
  stops moving freely.
- **Never crank on a flexible opponent.** If it isn't there after two controlled
  attempts, take the sweep, the back, or a different joint. Adding force to close
  a flexibility gap is the mechanism behind most shoulder injuries in training.
- **Protect your own knee.** If they step over and turn while your legs are
  triangled around their arm, the torque goes through your knee. Release the
  triangle rather than trying to hold it.
- **Go slowly when you follow the roll.** Momentum from a somersault can load
  their shoulder much faster than you intended. Let the roll finish before you
  re-apply.
- **Release on any tap**, including one you feel on your leg rather than see.
- **Legality:** legal at every belt in IBJJF gi and no-gi, and in every
  mainstream ruleset. See the belt legality table in
  [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).
