# Kneebar

> An armbar for the leg: their knee hyperextended over your hips while you hug their shin to your chest. The one leg lock whose mechanism a beginner already understands — and the one that fails hardest against a big thigh.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Leg lock — knee hyperextension |
| **Comes from** | Saddle, 50/50, outside ashi, the back step from headquarters, top half guard, the truck, a turtled opponent's exposed leg |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | IBJJF: brown and black belts only, gi and no-gi. Illegal at white, blue and purple. |

**Also known as:** hiza juji gatame, leg armbar, straight kneebar, belly-down kneebar

*Ratings match [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).*

---

## 1. The mechanism

The kneebar is a three-point lever, exactly like an [armbar](./armbar.md).

- **The fulcrum** is your hips, placed against the back of their thigh just above
  the knee.
- **The far end of the lever** is their shin and foot, hugged into your chest.
- **The near end** is their femur, pinned by your knees or your figure-foured
  legs so it cannot rotate or retreat.

Extend your hips and pull the shin toward your chest and the knee is driven past
full extension. Once the joint passes roughly neutral, the posterior capsule, the
cruciates and the collateral ligaments take the load. The knee has almost no
tolerance beyond that point.

Two things make the kneebar behave differently from the armbar it resembles.
First, the muscles crossing the knee are enormous compared with those crossing
the elbow, so a strong hamstring genuinely can hold a mediocre kneebar off —
which is why **fulcrum placement beats effort every time**. Second, the femur can
rotate at the hip. Rotate the femur and the plane of the knee hinge rotates with
it, and your hyperextension becomes a harmless push. Almost every failed kneebar
is one of those two problems, not a grip problem.

The knee does give more warning than it does under rotation — hyperextension
stretches muscle and capsule, and that produces a signal. But it is not much
warning, and it disappears entirely on someone whose knees hyperextend naturally.
The atlas's leg lock rules still apply in full: apply slowly, tap early.

## 2. The standard application

From a leg entanglement, belly-up, with the option to finish belly-down. Same-size
partner.

1. **Trap the leg and clear the knee.** Their leg between yours; slide your hips
   up their leg until your body is on the **thigh** side of their knee, not the
   shin side. Your knees pinch the thigh.
2. **Pull the foot to your chest.** Wrap both arms around their shin — one over,
   one under, hands clasped — and pull their heel into your armpit or against
   your collarbone. **Hug the shin high, close to the ankle: the further from
   their knee you hold, the more angle you generate per inch you pull.**
3. **Kill the femur.** Squeeze your knees, and put your top leg over their hip or
   hook their far leg so they cannot rotate away. **This is the step that decides
   the submission — if their femur can turn, the joint will never load.**
4. **Set the fulcrum.** Slide your hips until your hip crease or pubic bone sits
   against the back of their thigh roughly a hand's width above the knee joint
   line — **on the femur, never on the joint itself and never down on the calf.**
5. **Remove the slack.** Pull the shin down and in first, until you feel the
   knee come to full extension with no give left. Nothing has happened yet; this
   is the setup, not the finish.
6. **Finish.** Extend your hips and arch, driving the fulcrum up into the thigh
   while the shin comes down to your chest. Two vectors, applied together and
   slowly.
7. **Follow if they roll.** They will usually try to spin toward you to unwind the
   leg. Follow them over and finish belly-down, chest to the mat, hips still
   loaded into the back of their thigh — this version is stronger and much easier
   to control.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The kneebar changes more against different builds than almost anything else in
this library, because your fulcrum is a soft part of your body pressed against a
variably soft part of theirs. On a lean leg the fulcrum is effectively bone on
bone. On a heavily muscled leg you are pressing a cushion into a cushion, and the
technique has to be rebuilt.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick thighs / big hamstrings** | Muscle mass pads the fulcrum and your knees will not close around the femur — the joint never reaches end range | Slide the fulcrum down to within a hand's width of the knee crease where the distal femur is near the surface; present a bony edge by rolling onto one hip; figure-four your own legs instead of pinching |
| **Thin, lean legs** | Fulcrum is easy, but the joint reaches end range very fast and your hips can slide onto the joint line | Set the fulcrum on the femur and check it before applying; then finish at a third of your usual speed |
| **Long femurs** | Getting your hips above the knee puts you far from their hip, so the femur rotates and they roll out | Take the belly-down finish early and hook their far hip with your top leg; or attack from the saddle where the femur is trapped across you |
| **Short legs** | Their knee sits closer to your torso — your hips end up on the joint line, and their hip and hands are within reach | Slide 3–4 in (8–10 cm) further up onto the thigh; expect the hand fight and control the free leg first |
| **Hyperextending knees (genu recurvatum)** | They reach what looks like full extension with no pain at all — you get nothing at the angle where everyone else taps | Add range mechanically — fulcrum closer to the joint, hips higher, shin pulled from the ankle. If it is still nothing, switch locks rather than adding speed |
| **Very mobile hips** | They rotate the whole femur to unwind the knee and roll straight out | Trap the femur, not the thigh: top leg over their far hip, or take the saddle; follow the roll to belly-down instead of resisting it |
| **Much heavier than you** | Your hip extension cannot lift their mass; you push yourself away instead of loading the joint | Stop bridging into them — roll to belly-down so your bodyweight and gravity supply the fulcrum pressure |
| **Much lighter than you** | They spin out before you finish, and their joint reaches end range far sooner | Control the far hip before you take any grip on the shin, then apply at half speed |

### Thick thighs and big hamstrings

This is the build that beats the textbook kneebar, and it beats it in two
separate ways at once.

**Problem one: the fulcrum is a cushion.** Your hips are not resting on their
femur, they are resting on a couple of inches of hamstring and adductor. When you
extend your hips, the tissue simply deforms. The effective fulcrum ends up
higher, softer and further from the joint than you intended, and the knee never
reaches end range no matter how hard you arch.

**Problem two: the clamp fails.** Your knees will not close around a large thigh,
so the femur is free to rotate, and a rotating femur turns the whole thing into a
push.

Four adjustments, in order of importance:

1. **Move the fulcrum distally — much further down the thigh than feels right.**
   Slide your hips until your contact sits about a hand's width (3–4 in / 8–10 cm)
   above the crease at the back of their knee. That is where the hamstrings have
   already narrowed into tendons and the distal femur is close to the surface with
   very little over it. The soft, thick part of the leg is higher up; you have to
   go past it. The limit is the joint line itself — if you feel your hips drop
   into the hollow at the back of the knee you have gone too far, and everything
   below that is calf, which gives you nothing.
2. **Present a bony edge instead of a flat surface.** Roll 20–30° onto the hip
   that is under their leg so the hard ridge at the front of your pelvis — the
   point of the hip bone, or the side of it — is the contact, rather than the flat
   of your lower abdomen. Same principle as putting the radius edge on an
   Achilles: shrink the contact area and the same force becomes far more
   pressure, and pressure is what gets you down to the bone through the padding.
3. **Replace the pinch with a wrap.** Stop trying to close your knees on
   something they will not close on. Cross your ankles or figure-four your own
   legs around their thigh so the clamp is a closed loop that does not depend on
   your knees touching or your adductors holding. On a very large thigh, hooking
   your top foot behind your own bottom knee is the version that holds.
4. **Lengthen the far end of the lever.** Move your grip from mid-shin to right
   at the ankle and pull the foot to your chest from there. Every extra inch out
   along the shin is extra angular displacement at the knee for the same amount
   of pull — which is exactly what you need when part of your range is being
   eaten by soft tissue.

And the honest fifth option: **if the leg simply will not load, change locks.**
Thigh mass protects the knee against extension far better than it protects it
against rotation or compression. On this build the [heel hook](./heel-hook.md)
(where legal for you) and the [toe hold](./toe-hold.md) are both better tools,
because neither cares how much muscle is between your hips and their femur.

### Thin, lean legs

The mirror image, and mostly a safety problem rather than a technical one.

The fulcrum is essentially bone on bone, the clamp closes easily, and the joint
reaches end range in a fraction of the range you are used to. Two specific
errors follow.

First, with no tissue to stop you, your hips slide easily and often end up
directly on the joint line or below it, on the top of the calf. That gives you
a strange compression rather than a hyperextension, and it does nothing. Check
the fulcrum by feel before you apply — you want the hard, flat-ish surface of the
back of the femur under you, not the hollow of the knee.

Second, everything arrives early. Take the slack out (step 5), stop, then finish
over three or four seconds. Most of the accidental kneebar injuries in gyms
happen on light, lean legs held by people who learned the technique on bigger
partners.

### Long femurs

A long thigh forces a choice: put your hips where the fulcrum should be, and your
upper body is now a long way from their hip; stay near their hip, and your
fulcrum is too high on the thigh to work.

- **Take the belly-down finish early rather than as a recovery.** Belly-down, your
  chest and hips are both loaded onto the back of the leg and their femur is
  trapped underneath your bodyweight, which removes the distance problem
  entirely.
- **Hook their far hip with your top leg** so that even at range you have a
  connection to the pelvis, not just to the thigh.
- **Or attack from the saddle**, where their femur lies across your hips and the
  length of it stops mattering. This is the general answer to long legs in every
  leg lock: control the femur across your body rather than alongside it.

### Short legs

The opposite geometry. Their knee arrives close to your torso, and you will
consistently find your hips sitting on the joint line rather than above it,
which produces a lock that feels tight and does nothing.

- Slide 3–4 in (8–10 cm) further up the thigh than your instinct says.
- Expect a hand fight. A short-legged opponent's hands reach their own ankle
  easily, and they will simply peel your grip. Control the free leg and get the
  shin high into your armpit before they can post.
- Short, compact legs also mean a shorter lever, so you will need more hip
  extension for the same effect — the belly-down version is worth taking here
  too.

### Hyperextending knees

Some people's knees hyperextend 10–15° passively without any discomfort at all.
Against them, you will reach what looks like a fully finished kneebar and get
nothing, and the temptation to add speed is exactly the wrong instinct — the
tissue is still failing on a normal schedule, they simply feel it later and less.
This is the same trap as the hypermobile ankle on the
[straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) page.

Mechanical adjustments that are legitimate:

- Move the fulcrum closer to the joint — as close as you can get without dropping
  into the hollow behind the knee — so more of your available hip extension
  converts into joint angle.
- Elevate the fulcrum: get your hips higher, which on a belly-down finish means
  posting on your toes and driving your hips upward rather than just arching.
- Grip at the ankle rather than mid-shin, for the longer lever.

And the adjustment that matters most: **if it is not there, let it go.** Do not
add speed to a lock that is not producing a reaction. Where your ruleset allows,
a [calf slicer](./calf-slicer.md) or a [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) will find
this person's limits far sooner, because extension laxity does not protect
against compression or rotation.

### Very mobile hips

Some opponents — often the smaller, more flexible ones — defend the kneebar
purely by rotating the femur and rolling out of it. You extend, they turn, the
hinge turns with them, and they end up on top of you.

The fix is to control the pelvis, not the leg:

- Put your top leg over their far hip, so their hips are pinned to yours.
- Where legal, take the saddle, where the femur is trapped between your legs and
  across your body simultaneously.
- **Follow the roll instead of fighting it.** If they spin, spin with them and
  land belly-down. You lose nothing and you arrive in the stronger version of the
  submission. Resisting the roll from belly-up is how you lose the position and
  the top spot at the same time.

### Much heavier than you

Hip extension is a lifting movement, and against a much heavier opponent it
simply does not lift. Worse, if their weight is settled over you, extending your
hips just pushes *you* away from *them*.

- **Take gravity out of the equation: go belly-down.** Chest and stomach to the
  mat, hips loaded into the back of their thigh, their shin trapped under your
  chest. Now your bodyweight is the force, and the weight difference stops
  mattering.
- **Do not bridge into their mass.** If you find yourself pushing and nothing is
  moving, you are on the wrong side of the physics; change position rather than
  adding effort.
- **Commit early.** A much heavier opponent who gets to stand up will pull their
  leg free and drag you around with it. Either take the belly-down finish
  immediately or release and take the position.

### Much lighter than you

They will spin out of a belly-up kneebar almost casually, because your knee
pinch has very little mass to hold. Finish the hip control before you take any
grip on the shin — top leg over the far hip, or the saddle.

And then slow down hard. A lighter opponent's knee reaches end range on a much
smaller angle, and their ligaments have less cross-section. The entire finish
should happen inside the last quarter of your available hip extension, applied
over three or four seconds. If you can still feel a lot of range left in your
own hips, you are already deep into theirs.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

- **Short torso or short arms.** Getting their shin to your chest while your hips
  are far enough up their thigh is a reach problem for you. Grip at the ankle
  rather than mid-shin, and use the belly-down finish, which shortens the
  distance your arms need to cover.
- **Wide hips.** A genuine advantage: a broad pelvis makes a bigger, more stable
  fulcrum and it is easier to keep on the femur. Your risk is the opposite one —
  a wide fulcrum smears the pressure out on a thick thigh, so remember to roll
  onto one hip to sharpen it.
- **Narrow hips or a lean frame.** Your fulcrum is naturally sharp, which is
  effective and dangerous in equal measure. Add a deliberate pause at the
  slack-removal step; you will reach end range sooner than your partners expect.
- **Poor hip extension (stiff hips, older back).** The belly-up kneebar depends on
  a movement you may not have much of. Make belly-down your default finish and
  drive from the toes rather than from an arch.
- **Lighter than most of your partners.** The belly-up finish is a bridge, and
  you will lose that contest. Learn the belly-down finish first, and prioritise
  entries that arrive with their hip already controlled.
- **Long legs.** You can figure-four around a thigh most people cannot pinch,
  which makes you unusually good against heavy-legged opponents. Use it.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tight position, they look comfortable | Your fulcrum is too high on the thigh, sitting on muscle | Slide the hips down to a hand's width above the back of the knee; roll onto one hip to sharpen the contact |
| The lock feels loose and mushy | Your hips are on or below the joint line, effectively on the calf | Slide 3–4 in (8–10 cm) up onto the femur |
| They rotate and roll out on top | The femur is free — no hip control | Top leg over the far hip, or take the saddle. Follow the roll to belly-down rather than resisting |
| You arch as hard as you can and nothing moves | You are trying to bridge a much heavier person, or thigh mass is eating your range | Go belly-down and let bodyweight supply the force |
| Their leg slides out through your knees | Knee pinch failed on a thick thigh | Stop pinching — figure-four your own legs around the thigh |
| You have full extension and they are not reacting | Hyperextending knees, or you never removed the slack in the shin | Re-check slack first; if it is genuine laxity, change locks rather than adding speed |
| They reach down and peel your grip | You took the grip before controlling the far leg, or they have short legs and long reach | Control the free leg and get the shin deep in the armpit first |
| It works belly-up on small partners only | You have only learned the bridge finish | Drill belly-down as a separate technique — it is the version that scales |
| They tapped instantly and limped afterwards | You applied a leg-length lever at arm speed | Four seconds, always. Take the slack out, pause, then finish |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They fold the leg and pull the heel to their own backside | [Calf slicer](./calf-slicer.md) — their defense builds it for you (brown/black) |
| They rotate the femur to unwind the knee | Follow into the saddle and take the inside [heel hook](./heel-hook.md) (brown/black, no-gi) |
| They spin toward you and try to come up on top | Follow the roll into the belly-down kneebar; if they beat you there, take their back as they turn |
| They point the toes and drive their foot past your head | [Straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) — legal at every belt |
| They grip your ankle/hip and stall the extension | [Toe hold](./toe-hold.md), which needs no hip extension at all (brown/black) |
| They pull the leg free and stand up | Chase the standing leg — single leg or ankle pick |
| They defend well and you are stalling in a neutral entanglement | Let it go and take the sweep. A kneebar is not worth a position |

**What feeds into it:** the back step from headquarters; a stalled guard pass
converted into an entanglement; 50/50 and the saddle; the truck; top half guard
when they push your hips away with a straight leg; and a turtled opponent whose
foot and lower leg are exposed — see
[turtle escapes](../escapes/turtle-escapes.md) for the other side of that
exchange.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is a movement. Late defense is a tap.**

**Early — as they take the position.** The kneebar has an unmistakable tell: your
leg is straight and someone's hips are travelling up your thigh. React to that,
not to the grip.

- **Bend the knee and fight to keep it bent.** A bent knee cannot be
  hyperextended, full stop. Curl your heel toward your own backside and hold it
  there, and drive that knee toward the mat.
- **Sit up and grab your own ankle or shin.** This shortens the lever and removes
  the far end of it from their control. Ugly, effective, and available early.
- **Turn to the outside**, in the direction that unwinds your femur, and get your
  hip off the mat.

**Middle — leg straight, grip closed, fulcrum set.** Two options and a time
limit:

1. **Rotate toward the attacker** — the universal leg lock principle from the
   atlas. Spin your body the same way they are turning your leg so the rotation
   goes to your hip instead of your knee, then hip escape out backward.
2. **Kick and hip escape.** Post your free foot on their hip or their head, push
   hard, and pull the trapped leg out backwards along its own line. This works
   far more often against a belly-up kneebar than a belly-down one.

**Late — hips loaded, extension started.** Tap. The knee gives you a little more
warning under hyperextension than under rotation, but "a little more" means a
fraction of a second, and there is no version of a spinning escape from a fully
loaded kneebar that ends well. Late defense against any leg lock is worse than
early defense, and against a belly-down kneebar the difference is a hinge joint.

## 8. Drills

1. **Fulcrum-finding, no finish.** Partner lies passive with one leg straight.
   Enter, then slide your hips up and down their thigh, stopping at three
   positions: on the calf, on the joint line, and a hand's width above the knee
   crease. Apply a token amount of pressure at each and have your partner report
   what they feel. 6 reps per side. Do it on a lean partner and a heavy-legged
   partner in the same session — that comparison is the whole of section 3.
2. **Belly-up to belly-down transition.** From a belly-up kneebar, partner spins
   to escape; you follow the roll and re-establish the fulcrum belly-down. 10
   reps per side. No finishing — the rep ends when your hips are reset.
3. **Slack-then-stop.** With everything set, pull the shin to your chest until
   the knee reaches full extension with no give, then stop and hold for two
   seconds without adding hip extension. 8 reps per side. The purpose is to teach
   your body where "the finish begins" actually is, which is the single best
   injury-prevention habit in leg locking.
4. **Positional sparring — back step to the leg.** Top player starts in
   headquarters and must back step into an entanglement; bottom player must
   recover guard or escape the leg. 2-minute rounds. Submissions allowed only if
   both players are brown belt or above and both agree beforehand.

## 9. Safety

- **Apply slowly.** The two atlas rules for all leg locks apply here: apply
  slowly, tap early. Hyperextension gives more warning than rotation, but on
  a lean or hypermobile leg that margin shrinks to almost nothing.
- **Take the slack out, pause, then finish.** Making that pause a habit is the
  single most useful safety behaviour on this page. It converts a fast,
  ballistic movement into a controlled one and it costs you nothing.
- **Never finish a kneebar with a jerk or a hip snap.** The knee is a long lever
  and your hips are strong; a snap can exceed the joint's tolerance before your
  partner's hand reaches your leg.
- **Belly-down is stronger than it feels.** Because your bodyweight supplies the
  pressure, you get less feedback about how much force you are applying. Go
  slower belly-down than belly-up, not faster.
- **Tap early. Knee ligaments do not recover like muscle.** The atlas says it
  plainly and it is worth repeating on every leg lock page in this library.
- **Release by lowering the leg**, not by letting go and letting it drop, and
  never by rolling away while still holding it.
- **Tell your partner about existing knee injuries before the round**, not
  afterwards. The atlas puts this in Tier 0 for a reason.

**Legality.** Kneebars are legal in IBJJF competition for **adult brown and black
belts only**, in both gi and no-gi. They are illegal at white, blue and purple.
Note also that many kneebar entries pass through leg entanglements where **knee
reaping** applies — reaping is illegal in the gi at every belt, and legal in
no-gi only for adult brown and black belts — so the route to the submission can
be penalised even where the submission itself is permitted. Check the current
official ruleset for your event.
