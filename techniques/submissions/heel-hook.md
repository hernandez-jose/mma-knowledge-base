# Heel Hook

> The heel is only the handle — the joint being destroyed is the knee. The fastest, most damaging submission in grappling, and the one that gives its victim the least warning.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Leg lock — knee rotation, applied through the heel as a lever |
| **Comes from** | Outside ashi garami, single-leg X, 50/50 (outside version); saddle / inside sankaku / honey hole (inside version) |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 outside · 4/5 inside |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | **Banned in every IBJJF gi division at every belt.** No-gi: adult brown and black belts only, post-2021. Permitted in most professional and submission-only no-gi rulesets — check the event. |

**Also known as:** heel hook, outside heel hook (OHH), inside heel hook (IHH),
reverse heel hook

*The atlas lists the outside heel hook (3/5, common 3/5) and the inside heel hook
(4/5, common 3/5) as separate entries; this page covers both, so the difficulty
line carries both numbers. The atlas calls the inside heel hook "the single most
dangerous submission in grappling," and nothing on this page should be read as
softening that.*

> **Before you read section 2.** [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) is explicit that
> leg lock *defense* should be taught before leg lock offense, precisely because
> beginners meet heel hooks at open mats long before their belt allows them. If
> you are not a brown or black belt training no-gi, or working under a coach who
> teaches leg locks systematically with a partner who has agreed to it, read
> sections 1, 7 and 9 and skip the rest for now. Heel hook offense is not a
> beginner skill and this page will not pretend otherwise.

## 1. The mechanism

The heel is a lever bolted to the foot. The foot is bolted to the tibia. If the
foot cannot rotate relative to the tibia — which is what your armpit clamp
achieves — then rotating the heel rotates the tibia. And if the femur cannot
rotate to follow — which is what your legs achieve — then all of that rotation
has to be absorbed by the knee.

**The knee is not built to rotate.** It is a modified hinge with a small amount
of screw-home rotation and essentially no capacity beyond that. The cruciates
and the collateral ligaments are the restraints, and they fail at small angles.
So the ankle is the handle, the knee is the target, and everything about the
danger of this submission follows from that one sentence.

**Why there is no warning.** In an armbar, the elbow is protected by muscles that
cross the joint and by a rich pain signal from the joint capsule long before
anything tears — you feel the stretch, you tap, nothing happens. The knee's
resistance to rotation comes almost entirely from ligament, and ligament has
poor proprioception. There is no muscular stretch reflex standing between you
and a torn ACL. The pain very often arrives *at the same time as* the damage, or
after it. The atlas states the rule directly: **apply slowly, because there is no
stretch reflex warning in a heel hook, and tap early, because knee ligaments do
not heal like biceps do.**

**Outside versus inside — which rotation, and why it matters.**

- **Outside heel hook.** You are outside their leg (outside ashi, single-leg X or
  50/50) and you rotate their heel laterally, away from their midline. That
  externally rotates the tibia under the femur, and the load typically lands on
  the medial structures — the MCL and the medial meniscus — with the ACL taking a
  share. Because your legs control their leg but not their whole hip, the
  defender retains one release valve: they can rotate their entire body in the
  direction you are turning and dissipate some of the torque at the hip. That is
  the "rotate toward the attacker" principle in the atlas.
- **Inside heel hook.** You are inside their legs, in the saddle, with their thigh
  across your hips and your legs figure-foured. You rotate their heel medially,
  toward their midline, which internally rotates the tibia. Resisting internal
  tibial rotation is largely the ACL's job, and the knee tolerates it worse than
  external rotation. Worse still, the saddle pins the femur and the hip
  completely — **the release valve is gone.** Every degree you turn goes into the
  knee, the defender cannot spin out of it, and the attacker has enormous
  mechanical advantage. That combination is why the inside heel hook is
  considered the most dangerous submission in the sport, and why it is rated 4/5
  in the atlas while the outside version is 3/5.

Both versions can tear an ACL in well under a second if applied quickly. Neither
is a "tighten it up and see" submission.

## 2. The standard application

Outside heel hook from single-leg X or outside ashi, against a consenting
partner, at drilling speed. Read section 9 first.

1. **Establish the entanglement and the knee line.** Their leg between yours;
   your outside foot on their far hip; your inside foot deep behind their
   hamstring or under their buttock. **In the gi this position is illegal to
   attack from at every belt, and in no-gi reaping is legal only for adult
   brown and black belts.**
2. **Kill the femur.** Pinch your knees and drive your outside foot into their
   hip so their thigh cannot rotate. **If their femur can rotate, they are not in
   danger and you are not in control** — everything downstream depends on this
   step, not on your grip.
3. **Expose the heel.** They will be defending with the "boot" — toes pointed,
   foot bladed into your forearm so the heel is hidden. Clear it by pulling their
   toes and the blade of their foot across your centreline with your free hand
   until the heel appears on the far side of your forearm.
4. **Clamp the foot.** The blade of their foot goes into your armpit and your
   shoulder and lat close on it, so the ankle cannot rotate relative to the
   tibia. This is the step people skip. **A foot that rotates inside your grip
   turns your knee attack into a sloppy ankle attack and leaks all your torque.**
5. **Take the heel.** The heel sits in the crook of your elbow — not in your hand.
   Close a figure-four (gripping hand to your own bicep) or palm-to-palm.
6. **Finish with your body, not your arm.** Keep the arm rigid, keep the foot
   clamped, and rotate your whole torso — dropping your outside shoulder back
   toward the mat and turning your chest away from their leg. Your knees stay
   pinched throughout. **Do not curl the arm. An arm curl is fast, uncontrolled,
   and it is how partners get hurt.**
7. **Apply at a crawl and release on the first sign of a tap, a shout, or a
   turn.** In drilling, the correct habit is to release *before* the tap — apply
   until you feel the slack disappear, then stop.

For the inside heel hook the position changes to the saddle and the rotation
reverses — the heel turns medially, toward their midline — but steps 2, 4, 6 and
7 are identical, and step 7 matters more, not less.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

Heel hooks are unusually sensitive to build, and the failure of a heel hook
against an unusual leg is rarely "it slipped" — it is "I could not expose the
heel," "the ankle absorbed the rotation," or "the torque escaped at their hip."
Each of those has a specific mechanical answer.

Read the two rows on hypermobility twice. They are the ones where the adjustment
is partly *don't*.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick, muscular calves** | The calf belly blocks your forearm from getting under the heel; you catch tendon and end up with a footlock | Change the angle instead of digging: pull the foot across your centreline first so the heel points at your chest, then take it in the elbow crease; clamp the toes with the shoulder |
| **Skinny calves** | Nothing fills the space; your forearm rides up above the heel onto the Achilles | Anchor the heel in the elbow crease and pinch the elbow to your ribs before rotating; figure-four so the arm cannot lengthen |
| **Big, long feet** | The foot rotates inside your armpit clamp and you leak torque; also a much longer lever | Clamp further out — shoulder over the metatarsals, lat squeezed — and use the foot's length deliberately: half your usual rotation produces the same knee angle |
| **Small feet** | Heel and toes compete for the same few inches; your elbow crease and your armpit fight each other | Figure-four grip, heel in the crook of the elbow rather than the wrist, elbow tight to the ribs |
| **Hypermobile ankles** | The ankle eats the rotation — you turn the foot, not the tibia. The "boot" defense is far stronger on this build | Add a second control point: heel in the elbow crease *and* the ball of the foot pinned by your shoulder, so the foot cannot rotate independently. Then rotate less, not more |
| **Lax or hypermobile knees** | More rotational range before anything registers, so you can be well past safe before they feel it | Treat range as meaningless. Apply by feel of *resistance*, not by their reaction, and stop when the slack is gone |
| **Thick thighs** | Your knee pinch will not close, so the femur rotates and the torque never reaches the knee | Stop trying to pinch — figure-four your own legs around their thigh, or move to the saddle where their femur sits across your hips |
| **Long legs / long femurs** | Their hip is far from your control point; rotation dissipates at the hip | Drive your inside knee up into their hip crease and get your outside foot high on the far hip; or take the saddle, which controls the femur directly |
| **Much heavier than you** | They flatten the entanglement, straighten your legs and rip the leg free | Fight for the hip, not the foot — deeper inside leg, higher outside foot. If they stand and start ripping, let go or transition; you will not win a strength race here |
| **Much lighter than you** | They spin out of the entanglement before you finish — and they are at more risk when you do | Control before rotation, every time. And halve your speed: less tissue, less margin |

### Thick, muscular calves

The commonest reason a heel hook turns into a failed footlock. A big calf belly
sits between your forearm and the heel, and the harder you dig, the further your
forearm rides up the leg — you end up with your radius across the Achilles,
which is a [straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) with a bad grip, not a
knee attack.

Do not fight the calf. Change the angle so the calf is no longer in the way:

1. **Pull the foot across your centreline first.** Before you take any grip, use
   your free hand on their toes to draw the whole foot across your body until the
   heel is pointing at your sternum rather than away along the line of their
   shin. You are rotating the target into your elbow crease instead of trying to
   burrow to it.
2. **Take the heel with a hooked wrist, not a flat forearm.** Let the thumb side
   of your wrist and the head of your radius catch the back of the heel, so you
   are hooking a small bony point rather than trying to lay a whole forearm
   across a leg that is too thick for it.
3. **Shorten the chain.** Grip your own bicep in a tight figure-four. The looser
   the chain, the more of your rotation gets spent taking up slack in a limb that
   already has more mass to move.
4. **Clamp the toes, not the mid-foot.** On a thick lower leg the foot tends to
   sit shallower in your armpit, so move the clamp point outward — your shoulder
   should be over the ball of their foot.

### Skinny calves

Everything is exposed and easy, which produces two errors.

The first: with nothing filling the space, your arm slides up the leg under
load and you finish on the Achilles instead of the heel. Anchor the heel into
the crease of your elbow, pinch your elbow into your ribs, and take a
figure-four so the arm physically cannot lengthen.

The second is a safety error. A thin lower leg makes the heel very easy to
isolate, which makes the whole thing feel effortless, which is exactly when
people apply faster than they mean to. Effortless entry does not mean the knee
at the far end has more tolerance. Slow down in direct proportion to how easy
the entry felt.

### Big, long feet

A long foot is a long lever, and it works both for and against you.

Against: there is more foot to control, so the foot rotates inside your armpit
clamp — you turn, the foot turns, the tibia does not. Fix the clamp before you
fix anything else. Move your clamp point outward so your shoulder sits over the
metatarsals rather than the mid-foot, and squeeze with your lat rather than
your arm.

For: once clamped, the extra length means every degree you rotate produces more
angular displacement at the knee than it would on a small foot. **This is a
safety instruction, not a bonus.** Rotate about half as far as you normally
would and re-assess. The classic injury on this build is an attacker who applies
their usual amount of rotation and is genuinely surprised at the result.

### Small feet

Heel and toes are only a few inches apart, so your armpit clamp and your elbow
crease are fighting for the same real estate — clamp the toes and the heel slips
out of the elbow, take the heel deep and the toes come free of the shoulder.

Take the heel in the crook of the elbow rather than at the wrist, which moves
the heel two or three inches further back and buys the space. Figure-four the
grip and pin your elbow to your ribs so the short chain stays short. If it still
will not resolve, the [toe hold](./toe-hold.md) fits a small foot considerably
better than a heel hook does.

### Hypermobile ankles

A hypermobile ankle is a rotational shock absorber sitting between your lever and
your target. You rotate the heel, the foot rotates inside the ankle joint, and
the tibia barely moves. On this build the standard "boot" defense is also far
more effective, because they have more range in which to hide the heel.

The adjustment is a second point of control, not more force:

1. Take the heel in the crook of your elbow **and** pin the ball of their foot
   with your shoulder, so the foot is controlled at two separated points. A
   single contact lets the foot spin; two contacts a hand's width apart make it
   a rigid body.
2. Drive the ball of their foot slightly toward their own shin as you clamp
   (a little dorsiflexion), which takes the ankle to the end of its available
   rotation before you start. Now the rotation you add goes to the tibia.
3. **Then rotate less, not more.** This is the critical safety point on this
   build: a hypermobile ankle does not imply a hypermobile knee. Once you have
   removed the ankle's slack, their knee is an entirely ordinary knee with
   ordinary tolerances — and you have just spent all the warning signs getting
   there.

### Lax or hypermobile knees

Some people have genuinely more rotational laxity at the knee. They will not
react at the angle where your usual partners react. The temptation — turn
further until something happens — is precisely how ligaments get torn in
training.

Change what you are reading. Stop judging by their reaction and start judging by
**resistance**: apply until the slack disappears and you feel the joint start to
take load, then stop, regardless of whether they have made a sound. If a partner
tells you they have loose knees, treat every rep on them as a positional
exercise and never as a finish.

### Thick thighs

Everything about a heel hook depends on the femur being unable to follow the
tibia. On a heavily muscled thigh your knees will not close around the leg, the
pinch does not hold, and the femur rotates — the whole rotation dissipates at
the hip and the knee never loads.

- **Stop trying to pinch and start trying to wrap.** Figure-four your own legs —
  cross one ankle behind the other knee — so the clamp is a closed loop that does
  not depend on your adductor strength or on your knees actually touching.
- **Move to the saddle where the rules and your belt allow it.** In the saddle
  their thigh lies across your hips and your figure-foured legs trap the femur
  directly, which is a structural solution rather than a strength one. This is
  the single best answer to a thick-thighed opponent, and it is also the most
  dangerous position in the sport — which means slower, not faster.
- **Get your inside knee higher**, up into their hip crease, so you are
  controlling the femur near the hip rather than near the knee where the leg is
  thickest.

### Long legs and long femurs

A long femur means your control point at the thigh is a long way from their hip,
and the further your control is from the hip, the more of your rotation the hip
can quietly absorb. Long-legged opponents also have more room to reach your
entangled leg and start prying.

- Drive your inside knee up toward their hip crease rather than leaving it
  mid-thigh.
- Get your outside foot high on their far hip, not on their thigh, so you are
  pushing the pelvis rather than the leg.
- Where legal, take the saddle. It removes the length problem entirely by
  trapping the femur across your body instead of alongside it.

### Much heavier than you

Weight beats grip in leg entanglements. A much heavier opponent will flatten the
entanglement, straighten your legs, and either walk out or rip the foot free —
and they can do it without knowing anything about leg locks.

- **Invest in the hip, not the foot.** A deeper inside leg and a higher outside
  foot are worth more than any grip you can take on the heel.
- **Expect the stand-up.** If they get to their feet with your entanglement
  intact, you are on a clock. Either finish, or let go and take the sweep or the
  scramble.
- **Never chase.** The worst injuries in leg locking happen when a smaller
  attacker holds a rotating grip while a bigger opponent rips their leg out at
  speed. The knee tears during their escape, not during your finish. If they are
  ripping, open your hands.

### Much lighter than you

Two things, and the second is more important than the first.

A lighter opponent will spin out of the entanglement freely — there simply is
not enough mass for your pinch to hold against their rotation. The answer is to
finish the control before you start the rotation, not to grip harder.

And a lighter opponent has less tissue between your lever and their ligaments.
The same rotation you would apply to a 100 kg training partner is proportionally
much more on a 55 kg one. Apply at half speed and stop at the first sign of
resistance, not the first sign of pain.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

- **Short arms.** Your elbow crease is closer to your armpit, so on a big foot you
  will be forced to choose between clamping the toes and holding the heel. Choose
  the clamp — a controlled foot with an imperfect heel grip is a working heel
  hook; a perfect heel grip on a spinning foot is nothing.
- **Long arms.** Surplus length becomes slack, and slack in a heel hook means you
  rotate a long way before anything happens, then it happens all at once. Pinch
  the elbow to your ribs and shorten to a figure-four as a default.
- **Small hands or weak grip.** Good news: correctly applied, this submission
  needs no grip strength at all. The heel sits in the crook of your elbow and the
  finish comes from torso rotation. If you are gripping hard, your mechanics are
  wrong.
- **Short legs.** Controlling a long femur is harder for you. Prioritise the
  saddle and the deeper single-leg-X inside leg over outside ashi, where leg
  length does most of the work.
- **Lighter than most of your partners.** Your entanglements will get ripped
  apart. Build your game around entries that arrive with the hip already
  controlled — K-guard and the imanari roll into the saddle rather than fishing
  for outside ashi from a scramble.
- **Stiff hips.** The saddle requires you to sit with one leg figure-foured under
  a load; if that is not available to you, the outside heel hook from single-leg X
  will be a better home position than the inside version.
- **Any body, and this is the one that counts:** if you cannot apply a lock slowly
  and stop it mid-rotation on command, you do not yet have the control to be
  attacking a knee. That is a training-age question, not a body-type one.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You rotate and their whole body turns with you | Their femur is free — your knee pinch or hip control has failed | Figure-four your legs around the thigh, or move to the saddle; drive the inside knee into the hip crease |
| You turn the foot and nothing reaches the knee | The ankle is absorbing the rotation — a clamp problem, often on a hypermobile ankle | Two contact points: heel in the elbow crease, ball of the foot pinned by the shoulder; slight dorsiflexion before rotating |
| You cannot find the heel at all | They are booting, and/or a thick calf is in the way | Clear the toes across your centreline with the free hand first, then take the heel with a hooked wrist |
| Your forearm ends up on the Achilles | You are on a thin leg and the arm has ridden up | Re-anchor the heel in the elbow crease and pinch the elbow to the ribs — or accept it and finish the straight ankle lock |
| It felt effortless, then they yelped without tapping | You applied faster than you thought — there is no stretch reflex to warn them | Stop. Release fully. This is a near-miss, not a success, and it should change how you drill |
| They spin out every time | You are rotating before you have finished controlling | Control the hip, then the femur, then the foot, then rotate. In that order, every time |
| Tight lock, they stand up and walk out | Much heavier opponent; you are holding a leg, not a hip | Let go rather than hold on while they rip. The knee tears during their escape |
| A referee stops you and gives a penalty | Wrong belt, wrong uniform, or a reaping position | Heel hooks are banned in all IBJJF gi divisions and legal only for adult brown/black no-gi. Know the ruleset before the match, not during |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They boot the foot and hide the heel completely | [Toe hold](./toe-hold.md) — it attacks the direction the boot does not defend (brown/black) |
| They straighten the leg hard to escape the entanglement | [Kneebar](./kneebar.md) — the straightened leg is what a kneebar wants |
| They rotate toward you to relieve the torque | Follow them into the saddle, or take the back as they turn away |
| They fold the leg and drive their knee to the mat | [Calf slicer](./calf-slicer.md) from the folded position (brown/black) |
| They sit up and hand-fight your grips | Release and sweep — single-leg X into the standard sweep, or the wrestle-up |
| They cross their free leg over to defend | Take 50/50, or step through into the saddle on the other leg |
| They give you the heel but you have no belt/ruleset permission | [Straight ankle lock](./straight-ankle-lock.md) — legal everywhere, from the same entanglement |

**What feeds into it:** the imanari roll, K-guard, the back step from
headquarters, a failed guard pass turned into an entanglement (the atlas rates
that transition 4/5 and 3/5 for frequency), 50/50 from a failed sweep, and
single-leg X from butterfly or seated guard.

## 7. Defending it

**Late defense against a heel hook is not defense. It is a bet on your ACL.** The
gap between "I am in trouble" and "something has torn" is smaller here than in
any other submission in the sport. Everything worth knowing lives in the first
two phases.

**Early — before the entanglement is set.** This is the real defense, and it is
the reason the atlas puts leg lock defense in Tier 2 of the syllabus and leg lock
offense in Tier 6.

- Do not let your leg go between theirs in the first place. When someone back
  steps, imanari rolls, or sits to your leg, get your knee to the mat pointing
  toward them and your heel toward your own backside.
- **Hide the heel before it is a problem.** The atlas calls this the "boot" —
  point your toes hard and blade your foot so the flat of your foot is what
  their forearm meets. Rated 4/5 for difficulty, which is fair: it takes real
  practice to do under pressure.
- Get your free foot on their hip or their far knee and start prying the
  entanglement open immediately, not after they have gripped.

**Middle — entanglement set, grip closing.** Two things at once:

1. **Rotate toward the attack**, not away from it. This is the universal leg lock
   principle in the atlas: spin the same direction they are turning your leg, so
   the rotation happens at your hip rather than at your knee. Against an
   **outside** heel hook this genuinely works, because your hip is free.
2. **Free the hip.** Hip escape hard to get your trapped-side hip off the mat,
   and pull the knee of the trapped leg toward your chest to shorten the lever.

**A specific and important warning about the inside heel hook.** From the saddle
your hip is trapped between their legs, so the "rotate toward the attack"
release valve is largely gone. Trying to spin out of a well-set inside heel hook
often just accelerates the tear, because you supply the rotation they were about
to supply. If you are caught in a saddle with the heel exposed and their grip
closed, the correct response is to tap immediately.

**Late — grip closed, rotation started.** Tap. Now. Do not test it, do not try to
kick free, do not wait to feel pain — on this submission pain and injury are
often the same event. The atlas is blunt: tapping early is a technique, not a
failure, and it is the only reason you get to train tomorrow. There is no roll,
no medal and no ego worth an ACL reconstruction and a year off the mat.

## 8. Drills

1. **Boot drill (defense first, for everyone).** Partner takes outside ashi and
   tries to expose your heel; your only job is to boot the foot and keep it
   hidden. 60-second rounds, four rounds per side. This is the drill that
   belongs in every gym regardless of belt, because everybody meets heel hooks
   before they are allowed to use them.
2. **Control-only entanglement rounds.** Both players work for the saddle,
   single-leg X and 50/50. **Grips on the heel are forbidden.** Score a point for
   arriving in a controlling position with the femur trapped. 3-minute rounds.
   This builds the part of the submission that actually decides it and removes
   the part that hurts people.
3. **Slow-motion finish, four seconds minimum.** With the entanglement and the
   clamp already set, take four full seconds to reach the point where your
   partner taps — then release with your hands, not by throwing the leg. 5 reps
   per side. If you cannot consistently take four seconds, you are not ready to
   use this in a roll.
4. **Positional sparring, defense-weighted.** Start with the attacker in the
   saddle, heel already exposed, grip *not* yet closed. Defender wins by escaping
   or reaching a hand-fight standoff; attacker wins by closing the grip — and
   then stops. Nobody finishes. 90-second rounds.

## 9. Safety

**This is the most dangerous submission on this shelf. Treat everything below as
non-optional.**

- **Apply slowly. There is no stretch-reflex warning before a knee ligament
  tears.** Unlike an armbar, the joint will not scream first. A heel hook applied
  at speed can tear an ACL before the person feels anything at all. Four seconds
  minimum, every time, forever.
- **Tap early. Knee ligaments do not recover like muscle.** A torn ACL is
  surgery and roughly a year. A strained bicep is a fortnight. These are not
  comparable risks and should not be gambled with comparably.
- **Never crank, never explode, never "test" a heel hook.** If you feel the slack
  go and nothing has happened, stop and reset rather than adding force.
- **Release with your hands and let the leg down.** Throwing the leg away on the
  tap has injured plenty of people after the submission was already over.
- **Talk before you roll.** Agree with your partner whether leg locks are on the
  table, and both of you say so out loud. The atlas puts injury communication in
  Tier 0, before anybody touches anybody.
- **Both rotations are dangerous; the inside one is worse.** The outside heel hook
  externally rotates the tibia and leaves the defender one escape route through
  the hip. The inside heel hook internally rotates the tibia — the direction the
  ACL resists — from a position that pins the hip, so there is no escape route
  at all. Rated 4/5 by the atlas and described as the single most dangerous
  submission in grappling. If you are learning, learn the outside version first
  and learn it under supervision.
- **Not a beginner skill, and not a self-teaching skill.** The atlas's syllabus
  puts rotational leg attacks last in Tier 6, explicitly "with a coach and slow."

**Legality.**

| Context | Status |
|---|---|
| IBJJF, gi, any belt | **Banned.** No exceptions at any level. |
| IBJJF, no-gi, white / blue / purple | **Banned.** |
| IBJJF, no-gi, adult brown and black | Legal (post-2021 rules) |
| Knee reaping, gi | Illegal at every belt — which bans the positions as well as the submission |
| Knee reaping, no-gi | Legal for adult brown and black only |
| Most professional and submission-only no-gi events | Generally permitted — check the specific event's ruleset |

Knee reaping legality matters as much as submission legality here, because the
saddle and most inside-heel-hook positions are inherently reaping positions. A
legal submission from an illegal position is still a penalty or a
disqualification. Rules change; check the current official ruleset for your
event, as [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) advises.
