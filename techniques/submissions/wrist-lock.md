# Wrist Lock

> The most under-valued submission in jiu-jitsu: available from almost every position on the map, top or bottom, winning or losing — and the one that gives your partner the least warning before something tears.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Joint lock (wrist — flexion, extension or rotation) |
| **Comes from** | Almost anywhere: closed guard, mount, side control, *bottom* side control and mount, back control, turtle, grip fighting, and from inside the kimura, americana and armbar grips |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | **Illegal for IBJJF white belts.** Legal blue belt and up, gi and no-gi |

**Also known as:** mão de vaca, wristy, gooseneck, kote gaeshi (the rotational
judo/aikido version)

**Read the legality line before you read anything else.** Under IBJJF rules the
wrist lock is banned at white belt and permitted from blue upward — see the belt
legality table in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md). Other organisations vary, some
gyms restrict them in open mats, and plenty of training partners will be annoyed
by them. Ask your coach, check the event ruleset, and tell a new partner before
you use one on them.

---

## 1. The mechanism

The wrist is a stack of small bones held together by short, tight ligaments. It
has, as a rule of thumb, somewhere near 80 degrees of flexion (palm toward the
inside of the forearm), 70 degrees of extension (back of the hand toward the
outside of the forearm), and much less side-to-side deviation. Push past the end
of any of those and the ligaments and the joint capsule take the load. A wrist
lock is simply the joint being taken past its end range — usually in flexion,
sometimes in extension, sometimes with rotation added.

There are three families:

| Family | What you do | Typical setup |
|---|---|---|
| **Flexion** | Fold their palm toward the inside of their own forearm | Their hand flat on your chest or the mat; the classic version |
| **Extension** | Bend the back of their hand toward their forearm | Their gripping fist; the "gooseneck" |
| **Rotation** | Twist the forearm and deviate the hand at the same time | Kote gaeshi; from the kimura grip |

**Two things make or break every version.**

**The elbow must be controlled.** A wrist lock leaks. If their elbow can bend or
their shoulder can rotate, your force travels up the arm and dissipates —
they will simply follow the pressure and nothing loads. Straighten and pin the
elbow, or trap the whole arm, and the wrist becomes the only joint left to
absorb what you're doing.

**The force comes from your body, not your hands.** Your fingers will never beat
someone's forearm. The finish is your chest curling forward, your hips driving,
or your torso turning — with their hand held still against you.

**And one thing that is not like other submissions: there is almost no warning.**
A choke gives seconds of unmistakable feedback. An armbar stretches. A wrist
just... arrives. The gap between "they can feel it" and "something is damaged"
can be a few degrees and a fraction of a second. Everything in section 9 follows
from that.

## 2. The standard application

The flexion lock from closed guard, against a posted straight arm — the most
common version and the easiest to learn safely.

1. They post a hand on your chest or sternum, arm straight, palm flat. (Beginners
   do this constantly; so does everybody, under fatigue.)
2. Trap their hand flat against your chest with **both** of your hands, one over
   the back of their hand, the other clamping over the top. Their palm stays
   flat, their fingers point up toward your chin.
3. **Keep their elbow straight.** Pull their arm slightly toward you so the elbow
   locks out. A bent elbow is an escape route.
4. Close your guard high on their back and pull with your legs while you **curl
   your chest and shoulders forward over their hand**. Their hand cannot travel,
   so their wrist folds.
5. **Apply at a crawl.** Take up the slack over a full second, then add pressure
   in the smallest increments you can produce.
6. Release the instant they tap — or the instant you feel the joint stop moving
   freely, whichever comes first.

The two details people get wrong: gripping the *fingers* instead of the hand
(small-joint manipulation, banned in every ruleset and a genuine injury risk),
and applying with the arms instead of the torso, which is both weaker and much
harder to control.

### Where else it lives — a short tour

| Position | The version |
|---|---|
| **Inside the [kimura](./kimura.md) grip** | You already have their wrist in a figure-four. Roll your grip toward their fingers and rotate — the shoulder lock and the wrist lock share a setup. |
| **Inside the [americana](./americana.md)** | Your pinning hand can extend their wrist while the figure-four holds the elbow. |
| **Inside the [armbar](./armbar.md)** | They clasp their hands to bicep-curl. The top hand is now folded and completely available. |
| **Mount** | They push your chest with a flat palm — same mechanics as the guard version. |
| **Side control / knee-on-belly** | They frame with an open hand on your hip or neck. |
| **Bottom side control or bottom mount** | They post a hand on the mat beside you. One of very few submissions available from a losing position. |
| **Back control** | Their hands are busy defending their collar; a folded defending hand is a target. |
| **Grip fighting** 🥋 | Fold their gripping hand back over their own knuckles. The classic answer to a strong lapel or sleeve grip. |
| **Guard passing** | Their hand grips your ankle or pant leg — trap it against your own leg and step. |

## 3. Adjusting for their body

Wrist range varies more between individuals than almost any other joint in the
body, and — critically — it varies in ways you cannot see. Two people with
identical-looking forearms can be 40 degrees apart. The wrist lock is therefore
the technique where "adjust for their body" is not an optimisation; it is the
difference between a submission, a wasted grip, and an injury.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Very flexible wrists** | Their palm reaches their forearm with no discomfort. You run out of your own range of motion before they run out of theirs. | Stop using pure flexion. Add ulnar deviation and rotation so the hand travels toward their own elbow on a diagonal — combined directions run out far sooner than any single one. |
| **Stiff wrists** | End range arrives almost immediately, and often before you feel anything change. Weightlifters, older grapplers and previously injured wrists all sit here. | Take up slack over two or three seconds and expect the tap before it feels "on". This is where wrist locks break people. |
| **Thick forearms, strong grip** | They clench a fist and tense, which shortens the available range and armours the joint; and a gripping fist won't fold forward. | A closed fist limits flexion but not extension or deviation. Bend it **backwards** or sideways instead, or fold their own gi grip over their knuckles. |
| **Long arms** | Best-case scenario: they post and frame constantly, so targets are everywhere. But their elbow is far from you and slow to help them. | Attack the posted, loaded, straight arm. Their elbow is too far away to rescue the hand in time. |
| **Short arms** | They keep their hands close to their body and their elbow bends easily, so the lock leaks up the arm. | Pin the elbow first — your knee, your other hand or your chest on it — then attack. Without elbow control there is nothing to attack against. |
| **Much heavier than you** | Nothing, really. Their mass is irrelevant to a joint the size of a wrist. | Don't try to move them. Trap the hand and use *their* forward pressure as the force — you supply the angle, they supply the power. |
| **Much lighter than you** | They pull the hand free before you set anything, and their wrist reaches end range faster. | Elbow first, hand second. Then apply at a fraction of your normal speed. |

### Very flexible wrists

Some people can lay their palm flat against the inside of their own forearm.
Against them, the textbook flexion lock is an ordinary stretch, and you will
reach the limit of your own posture — your chest can only curl so far — long
before their joint notices.

- **Change the plane.** Pure flexion is huge on a hypermobile wrist. Flexion plus
  **ulnar deviation** (their little finger travelling toward their own elbow) plus
  a few degrees of forearm rotation is much smaller. Fold their hand on a
  diagonal, not straight down.
- **Try the other direction.** Large flexion range does not imply large extension
  range. Turn the same trapped hand into a gooseneck and bend it back instead.
- **Take the elbow instead.** A hypermobile wrist very often sits on an ordinary
  elbow. You are already holding the arm — go to the [armbar](./armbar.md) or
  the [kimura](./kimura.md).
- **Or spend the grip.** Even when it won't finish, a trapped hand is a broken
  frame, a stripped grip and a stalled pass. The wrist control is worth having on
  its own.
- **Never respond to flexibility with speed.** This is the most important
  sentence on the page. On a wrist, speed does not overcome range — it overcomes
  *warning*.

### Stiff wrists

This is the build that gets hurt, and it is extremely common: anyone who lifts,
anyone with a desk-job wrist, anyone with an old sprain, and most people over
about forty.

- **Their end range may be barely past neutral.** By the time you feel resistance,
  you may already be at their limit.
- **Take up slack over two to three seconds.** Not one second. Not "quickly but
  gently."
- **Expect the tap before you feel the lock.** If someone taps to a wrist lock
  you thought wasn't on yet, they were right and you were wrong.
- **Prefer the version you can feel.** Attack with your chest or hips against a
  fixed hand, not with a sharp twist of your hands — the slower the force
  application, the more feedback you get.

### Thick forearms and a strong grip

- **The clenched fist is their armour.** A tight fist and a braced forearm both
  reduce flexion range and add support. Trying to fold a clenched fist forward is
  a strength contest you will lose.
- **Attack the direction the fist doesn't protect.** A fist barely limits
  extension or ulnar deviation. Bend the hand *backwards* over the wrist
  (gooseneck), or push it sideways.
- **Use their own grip against them.** When they grip your lapel, sleeve or
  wrist, trap their hand flat against you with your other hand and fold their
  wrist over their own knuckles. Their commitment to the grip is what stops the
  hand escaping — the harder they grip, the better it works. This is the single
  best answer to a grip you can't break.
- **Attack open hands, not closed ones.** Framing hands, posting hands and palms
  on your chest are all open and loaded. Those are your targets.

### Long arms

Long-armed opponents post, frame and reach more than anyone else, and every one
of those is a straight, loaded arm with an open hand on the end of it.

- **Attack the loaded post.** A hand bearing weight cannot be withdrawn quickly.
- **Their elbow is far away.** With a long arm, the elbow is a long way from the
  hand and a long way from you, so they cannot bend it and rescue the wrist
  before the lock arrives. You need less elbow control than usual.
- **Use their reach to your advantage from the bottom.** Long-armed passers put a
  hand on your hip or your knee; that hand is available whether you're winning or
  losing.

### Short arms

- **The lock leaks.** With everything close to their body, they simply bend the
  elbow and follow your pressure, and your force disperses into the elbow and
  shoulder.
- **Pin the elbow first, every time.** Knee on the elbow, your free hand clamping
  it, or your chest lying across the upper arm. Then attack the hand.
- **Prefer the trapped-arm versions** — from inside a kimura or americana grip,
  where the arm is already immobilised — over opportunistic grabs at a posting
  hand.

### Much heavier than you

The wrist lock is the great equaliser, and this is the row that explains why.
Their weight, their strength and their base are all irrelevant to a joint that
small. They cannot muscle out of it because there is no muscle that acts on the
end range of a wrist.

- **Don't try to move them.** Trap the hand against a fixed surface — your chest,
  your shoulder, the mat — and let your body supply the angle.
- **Use their pressure.** When a heavy opponent drives their weight through a
  posted hand onto your chest, the force pressing that hand into you is theirs,
  not yours. Fold the hand and *let them do it to themselves* by continuing to
  drive.
- **This is a bottom-position weapon.** From bottom side control and bottom
  mount, their posting hands are the only thing you can reach — and this is one
  of the very few legitimate submissions available from a losing position.

### Much lighter than you

- **They withdraw fast.** A lighter, quicker opponent will snatch the hand back
  before you set anything. Control the elbow before you commit to the hand.
- **Their end range arrives sooner** — smaller joint, less soft tissue, less
  warning. Halve your speed again.
- **Do not use bodyweight as the force.** "Curl your chest over their hand" means
  something very different when there's a 30 kg difference. Apply with your hands
  and stop early.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**If your hands are small:** you cannot palm someone's whole hand. Stop trying to
grip it — trap it flat between your hand and a surface (your chest, your
shoulder, the mat) and move the surface. Two hands on one of theirs, always.

**If your grip is weak:** good. Weak grips force the correct technique, because
the correct technique never uses grip strength. The force comes from your torso
curling or your hips driving with their hand pinned.

**If your arms are long:** you will tend to apply the lock too far from your body,
where you have leverage but no feel. Bring their hand in to your chest before you
fold it.

**If you are much heavier than your partners:** you can produce far more force
than any wrist needs. Use your hands rather than your bodyweight so you can feel
what you are doing, and go slower than feels sensible.

**If you are much lighter than your partners:** this technique may be the highest
return-on-investment submission in your whole game. It ignores strength and
weight entirely, and it is available from underneath. Learn it properly, and
learn the safety rules first.

**If your own wrists are unreliable:** many of these locks require you to hold a
position under load with your own wrist in extension. Use the versions that clamp
with your forearms and chest rather than your hands, and tell your partners
before drilling.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You fold their hand and they just bend their elbow | No elbow control — the force is leaking up the arm | Pin the elbow first with a knee, a hand or your chest |
| Their hand slides out of your grip | You're gripping instead of trapping | Trap the hand flat against your chest or the mat with two hands |
| You fold it fully and they feel nothing | Genuinely flexible wrist | Add ulnar deviation and rotation, or switch to extension, or take the elbow |
| Your fingers are burning and nothing is happening | You're applying with your hands | Fix the hand in place, then move your torso or hips |
| It only works on brand-new partners | You're catching posts, not creating them | Attack loaded, committed hands — grips and frames, not idle ones |
| They tap instantly and seem annoyed | Nothing wrong mechanically — this is normal for wrist locks | Slow down, warn people, and check your gym's norms |
| Their fingers are in your grip | You're attacking small joints | Stop. Small-joint manipulation is banned in every ruleset and is a genuine injury |
| You caught it in competition and got penalised | White belt under IBJJF | It's illegal at white belt — check your division's rules |

## 6. Chains

### Their defense → your next attack

| Their defense | Your next attack | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Bend the elbow and pull back | [Kimura](./kimura.md) — the arm is now bent and in front of you | A bent arm is a shoulder lock |
| Straighten and push through | [Armbar](./armbar.md) or straight armlock | A straight arm you already control |
| Clench the fist and brace | Extension (gooseneck) or deviation instead of flexion | A fist doesn't protect those directions |
| Rip the hand away entirely | Take the position they gave up to do it | Ripping a hand out costs them a frame or a grip |
| Flexible wrist, no tap | Elbow or shoulder — [armbar](./armbar.md), [kimura](./kimura.md) | Wrist flexibility doesn't travel up the arm |
| Roll toward you to relieve it | Follow to the back or to mount | They're turning away to escape |

### What feeds into it

| From | The trigger |
|---|---|
| [Armbar](./armbar.md) | They clasp their hands and bicep-curl — the top hand is folded and free |
| [Kimura](./kimura.md) grip | Slide the grip toward their fingers and rotate |
| [Americana](./americana.md) | The pinning hand extends their wrist while the figure-four holds the elbow |
| [Omoplata](./omoplata.md) | Their trapped forearm is right in front of you |
| Grip fighting 🥋 | Any lapel or sleeve grip you can't break — fold it instead |
| Bottom side control / bottom mount | Their posting hand on the mat |
| Guard passing | Their hand on your ankle or knee |

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is the only good defense.** There is no equivalent of the
"survive and escape" phase you get with a choke. Once the lock is set, the choice
is a tap or an injury, and there is not much time between them.

**Early — before they trap the hand.** This is almost entirely a matter of hand
habits:

- Don't post with a flat, open palm on someone's chest or on the mat. **Post on
  a fist, or on the blade of your forearm.** A closed fist is far harder to fold.
- Don't frame with an open hand on their neck or hip; frame with a straight arm
  and a closed hand, or with your forearm bone.
- Don't leave a lapel or sleeve grip hanging when they've trapped your hand
  against their body — that grip is now a handle for them.
- Keep your elbows connected to your ribs. Most wrist locks need your arm out and
  extended.

**Middle — they've trapped the hand but haven't loaded the joint.** Bend your
elbow and bring your hand back to your own body. That is the whole answer:
shortening the arm makes the wrist unreachable and makes their leverage
disappear. Turn your hand so the little-finger edge leads — this rotates the
joint out of the direction they're folding. Then, if you can, **grip their gi,
their arm or your own gi** — a closed hand on something solid is very hard to
wrist lock.

**Late — the joint is loaded.** Tap. Immediately.

That is not a hedge, it is the correct instruction. There is no late defense
worth the risk: you cannot feel how close to the limit you are, and neither can
they. The most common wrist injury in jiu-jitsu is somebody who thought they had
a little more room. **Tap early, and tap loudly enough to be heard** — a wrist
lock often happens with your hand where your partner can't see it move.

## 8. Drills

**1. The slow-fold (10 per side, cooperative, no tap).**
Partner posts a flat hand on your chest. Trap it, lock the elbow, and curl your
chest forward *over three full seconds*, stopping the moment they say "there".
Then release. The rep is the speed control, not the finish. If you cannot do this
drill at this speed, you are not ready to use the technique in rolling.

**2. The range audit (once, 10 minutes, high value).**
Take a light flexion lock on five different partners and stop where each first
feels it. Note how far apart those points are — this is the widest variation
you'll find anywhere in the sport, and feeling it once will change how you apply
every wrist lock afterwards.

**3. Grip-fold circuit 🥋 (10 each side).**
Partner takes a strong lapel grip. Trap their gripping hand flat against your
chest with your free hand, then fold their wrist over their own knuckles by
turning your torso. Stop at first contact with the joint. This is the practical,
everyday version of the technique and it doubles as a grip break.

**4. Positional sparring — attack the frames (2-minute rounds).**
Start in bottom side control. Your only permitted submission is the wrist lock;
their job is to hold you down without posting on the mat or framing with an open
hand. Both of you improve: you learn to see the targets, they learn the habits
that make the targets disappear.

## 9. Safety

**This is the section that matters most on this page.**

- **There is almost no warning.** Unlike a choke — which gives clear, escalating
  feedback for several seconds — a wrist goes from comfortable to sprained across
  a few degrees. Neither person reliably feels the last moment before injury.
  Treat every application as though your partner cannot feel it coming, because
  often they can't.
- **Apply at a crawl. Always.** Take up slack over two to three seconds, then add
  pressure in the smallest increments you can make. Never snap, jerk or twist
  quickly. Speed is the injury mechanism here — not force.
- **Never use it to "surprise" someone.** Half the point of a wrist lock is that
  it's unexpected; that is precisely why it needs the most conservative
  application of any submission you know.
- **Tell new partners.** Especially anyone you haven't rolled with, anyone
  training with a wrist injury, and anyone whose belt suggests they may not have
  been taught to expect it.
- **Never attack fingers.** Small-joint manipulation is banned in every ruleset
  listed in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md), and finger injuries in grappling are
  common enough already.
- **Release instantly.** Wrist locks often trap the hand where your partner
  cannot tap you with it; accept verbal taps, foot taps, or any sound at all.
- **If you are on the receiving end, tap early.** There is no heroic escape
  available. Wrist and hand injuries also disproportionately interfere with
  everything else you do — gripping, working, driving.
- **Legality:** **illegal for IBJJF white belts**, legal from blue belt in both
  gi and no-gi. Other rulesets differ, and some gyms restrict wrist locks in open
  mats regardless of belt. Check before you compete and before you visit.
