# Americana

> The kimura's mirror image: the same figure-four, rotating their shoulder the other way, with their hand pinned to the mat beside their own head — the first submission most people ever finish, and the first one that stops working.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Joint lock (shoulder — external rotation) |
| **Comes from** | Side control, mount, north–south, kesa gatame, knee-on-belly |
| **Difficulty** | 1/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** keylock, ude-garami, V-armlock, bent armlock, figure-four armlock, "paintbrush"

**A note on the 1/5 rating.** It is correct for what it measures — a complete
beginner can be taught this in one class and tap somebody with it that night, and
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) rates difficulty on exactly that basis. But
1/5 describes *learning* it, not *finishing* it. Against a mobile shoulder the
americana is one of the harder submissions in the fundamentals syllabus, and
against a hypermobile one it may not exist at all. Read section 3 before you
decide it's a beginner move.

---

## 1. The mechanism

The americana is the **mirror rotation of the [kimura](./kimura.md)**. Same
figure-four grip, same bent arm, opposite direction: where the kimura rotates
their shoulder **internally** (hand toward their feet, behind their back), the
americana rotates it **externally** (hand toward their head, up the mat). The
load lands on the front of the shoulder capsule, the subscapularis, and the long
head of the biceps tendon.

Because the rotation runs the other way, everything about how you set it up
inverts too — and two details carry the whole technique.

**Their hand and wrist must be flat on the mat.** The mat is one of your two
anchor points. You pin their hand there with your palm across the back of theirs
and press it into the ground; their hand does not move again. Everything after
this happens by moving their *elbow*, not their hand. If their hand comes off the
mat, you are curling their arm around in space and there is nothing to rotate
against.

**Their elbow must stay low.** The lock exists only when their elbow is at or
below the line of their shoulder. Every centimetre the elbow rides up toward
their head converts your rotation into harmless abduction — they end up in a
"surrender" position that is comfortable to hold all day. The classic cue is to
**drag the elbow toward their hip** while the hand stays pinned: paint the mat
with their knuckles as the elbow travels down. That is the whole finish.

## 2. The standard application

From side control, attacking the arm on **your** side of their body (their near
arm), against someone your size.

1. Establish real side control first: crossface or head-side arm under their
   head, hip-side arm blocking their far hip, chest heavy on their sternum. The
   americana fails from a floating side control.
2. Wait for — or create — the moment their near arm bends, hand up near their own
   head. Framing on your neck or shoulder is the most common gift. Feed it there
   by driving your shoulder into their jaw so they push back.
3. **Pin their wrist to the mat with your head-side hand**, palm on the back of
   their hand, your fingers curling toward their thumb. Press it flat. Not a grip
   — a pin.
4. Swim your hip-side arm **under their elbow**, then grip your own wrist. Your
   forearm is now the fulcrum under their upper arm.
5. Keep your chest on their chest so **their shoulder blade cannot lift off the
   mat**. A scapula that can rise gives them 20 or 30 degrees of free rotation
   and you'll never notice where it went.
6. Lift their elbow one or two centimetres off the mat with your threading
   forearm to take out the slack, then **drag the elbow toward their hip** while
   the hand stays pinned where it is.
7. Finish by dropping your bodyweight and shoulder into the movement, not by
   curling with your arms. Slowly.

Two common errors: gripping the wrist with your fingers instead of pressing the
hand flat (their hand slides and you lose the mat anchor), and lifting the elbow
*up* toward their head instead of dragging it *down* toward their hip (the
"surrender position" — comfortable, controlling, not a submission).

### Position-specific notes

| Position | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Mount** | Attack whichever arm they raise to push you. Pin that wrist with your opposite-side hand, thread the other under the elbow. Base wide — this is the moment they bridge. |
| **North–south** | Both arms are already up beside their head. Excellent americana position, and the elbow drags toward their hip very naturally. |
| **Kesa gatame** | Their near arm is already trapped under your armpit; the far arm is often available. Keep your hips low or the pin collapses. |
| **Knee-on-belly** | Available when they push at your knee with a bent arm, but you must drop into side control to finish — the elbow drag needs your chest weight. |
| **Far-arm variant** | If they reach across to frame on your far shoulder, the same lock is available on the far arm. Rarer, and the elbow drag is toward their far hip. |

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The americana asks a single anatomical question: **how much external rotation
does this shoulder have?** People vary enormously, and unlike the
[armbar](./armbar.md) — where you can usually feel the geometry going wrong —
the americana can look perfect and be doing nothing at all. Learning to tell
those two states apart is the skill.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Very flexible shoulders** | Their hand lies flat on the mat above their head with no discomfort at all. You have already used the full range of motion the technique is designed to exhaust. | Reduce the abduction — walk their elbow in toward their ribs before rotating — and drag the elbow *below* their shoulder line toward their hip. Pin the scapula. If it isn't there in two attempts, switch to the [kimura](./kimura.md) direction. |
| **Stiff shoulders** | They reach end range before you have applied anything. The tap comes at a point that doesn't feel "on" to you. | Move in degrees. Set the figure-four in the air rather than expecting their hand to reach the mat, and stop the instant resistance appears. |
| **Thick, muscular arms** | You cannot close the figure-four, and their bicep bottoms out against their forearm before the joint reaches end range. | Grip your own forearm or sleeve rather than your wrist, thread deeper so your armpit meets their triceps, and lift the elbow to finish instead of dragging it. |
| **Strong grip, strong lats** | They grab their own belt, lapel or thigh and the arm becomes part of their torso; or they clamp the elbow down and refuse to be moved. | Break the grip before you build the lock — knee or shin on the bicep, or peel a single hand with two of yours. Never fight the lat directly. |
| **Thin arms** | Your hand slides off theirs, and the loop drifts down to their fingers. | Press the hand flat with your palm rather than gripping, and keep the elbow at 90 degrees actively with your threading forearm. |
| **Long arms** | Their long arm can straighten and slide free over their own head, and it reaches their belt to anchor. | Block the escape line with your head or shoulder above their arm, and pin the elbow at 90 degrees before you rotate. |
| **Short arms** | Very little lever between elbow and hand — you'll be working close and generating little torque. | Set your threading arm deep so your **elbow**, not your hand, is the fulcrum, and finish with bodyweight rather than arm strength. |
| **Much heavier than you** | They bridge you off, or their arm is simply too heavy to move with your arms. | Widen your base, head down on the mat past their far shoulder, and drive with your hips and chest. Block their far hip before you commit both arms. |
| **Much lighter than you** | They shrimp out and recover the elbow to their ribs before you can close the loop, and their end range arrives quickly. | Kill the hips with your knee first, lock second. Then apply at half speed. |

### Very flexible shoulders — the case where the technique may not exist

This is the biggest single reason white belts stop finishing americanas around
the time they stop rolling exclusively with other white belts.

External rotation with the arm abducted to 90 degrees is, as a rule of thumb,
somewhere in the region of 90 degrees for an average adult — which is roughly
"hand flat on the mat beside your head." That is exactly the finishing position
of the standard americana, and it is why the technique works on most people. But
plenty of people have considerably more: throwers, swimmers, climbers, dancers,
anyone generally hypermobile. On them, the "finished" americana position is a
comfortable stretch. You are applying a submission that is already at its end
point and has nowhere left to go.

The instinct is to lift harder on the elbow. That does nothing but slide their
hand out of your pin, and if you finally do overcome the range, you overcome it
all at once — which is how shoulders get hurt.

Fix the geometry:

1. **Adduct first, rotate second.** External rotation range is at its maximum
   with the arm out at 90 degrees from the body. Bring their elbow in toward
   their ribs by 10 to 15 cm *before* you rotate anything and a surprising amount
   of their range disappears. This is the same principle as the kimura
   adjustment, in the opposite direction.
2. **Drag the elbow below the shoulder line.** Not level with it — below it,
   toward their hip. This is what "paint the mat" actually means, and on a
   flexible shoulder it is not optional. Their knuckles stay exactly where they
   are while their elbow travels 10 cm toward their belt.
3. **Pin the shoulder blade.** Chest to chest, weight through your sternum onto
   theirs. A flexible person's first cheat is lifting the scapula off the mat and
   turning slightly toward you — they'll do it without knowing it, and it hands
   them back all the range you just took.
4. **Take the wrist slack out.** Your pinning hand can also extend their wrist
   slightly. Many "americana" taps from flexible partners are wrist taps.
5. **Then stop and switch.** A shoulder with big external rotation very often
   does *not* have big internal rotation. Keep the same grip, roll their arm the
   other way and finish the [kimura](./kimura.md) — from side control this is a
   single continuous movement and it is the highest-percentage answer on this
   page. Failing that, straighten the arm for a [straight armlock or
   armbar](./armbar.md), or take the [wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md).

**The rule:** two controlled attempts at the rotation, then change joints or
change direction. Never change *speed*.

### Stiff shoulders

- **The margin is tiny.** Someone with limited external rotation is at end range
  before their hand touches the mat. If you set the technique up expecting the
  hand to reach the ground, you will have already injured them by the time it
  does.
- **Set the figure-four where their hand naturally sits**, in the air if
  necessary, and apply from there.
- **Take the early tap seriously.** With a stiff shoulder the tap arrives while
  you still feel slack, which makes it easy to dismiss. Don't.
- The upside: stiff-shouldered opponents cannot easily escape by rotating out
  either, so control is easy. Take the control and skip the crank.

### Thick, muscular arms and strong grips

- **The loop may not close.** Grip your own forearm or your own sleeve instead
  of your wrist. A loop closed lower down the limb still transmits rotation; a
  loop that doesn't close transmits nothing.
- **Thread deeper.** Push your threading arm through until their triceps sits in
  your elbow crease or armpit. On a big arm your hand is too far out to be a
  useful fulcrum.
- **Lift, don't drag.** On a very thick arm, the bicep and forearm meet before
  the elbow can travel far, so the "elbow toward the hip" drag runs out of room.
  Lift the elbow off the mat instead while keeping the hand pinned — same
  rotation, different path.
- **The grip defense is the real problem.** Strong opponents grab their own belt,
  their own lapel or their own thigh, and now you're trying to rotate their
  torso. Break the connection *before* you build the figure-four: drive your knee
  or shin across their bicep, or put two hands on one of theirs and peel at the
  thumb. Once the loop is built you have no free hands left to fix this.
- **Never fight the lat.** If they clamp the arm down and you cannot lift the
  elbow, don't grind. Switch to the kimura direction, or move to north–south
  where that clamp doesn't work.

### Thin arms

- **Pin, don't grip.** Press your palm flat across the back of their hand rather
  than wrapping fingers around a thin wrist — a wrap slides, a press doesn't.
- **Hold the 90-degree elbow angle actively** with your threading forearm; a thin
  arm straightens out of the loop with almost no effort.
- **Expect a fast tap** and apply accordingly.

### Long arms

- **They can slide out over their own head.** A long arm has enough length to
  straighten and travel up past their ear, out the top of your loop. Block that
  line: your head or shoulder sits above their upper arm so it cannot travel
  toward their head.
- **They can anchor.** Long arms reach belts, lapels and thighs. Strip first.
- **You get more torque than you expect.** Once the elbow is at 90 degrees and
  the hand is pinned, a long humerus is a long lever in *your* favour. Reduce
  your force accordingly — this is a common source of accidental injuries.

### Short arms

- **Little lever.** Elbow to hand is your entire moment arm and it is short.
- **Make your elbow the fulcrum, not your hand.** Thread until their triceps
  rests in your elbow crease. That moves your point of contact closer to their
  shoulder and buys back leverage.
- **Finish with weight.** Drop your shoulder and chest into the rotation rather
  than trying to move a short, stiff lever with your arms.
- The compensation: a short arm cannot reach its own belt, so the grip defense
  that ruins this technique against long-armed opponents isn't available.

### Much heavier than you

- **Their bridge is the threat, not their shoulder.** Both your arms are
  committed to the loop, which means your base is your legs and your head.
  Knees wide, toes gripping, head down on the mat past their far shoulder.
- **Block the far hip first.** If you can't stop their hips turning into you,
  don't build the lock — take the control and improve position instead.
- **Move their arm with your body.** A heavy limb will not move for your biceps.
  Drop your chest and shoulder through the rotation and let your bodyweight
  supply the force.
- **From mount, expect to lose it.** A much heavier opponent will bridge you off
  the moment your hands leave the mat. Consider taking the arm from side control
  or north–south instead, where you're not balanced on top of them.

### Much lighter than you

- **They escape before you build it.** A light opponent shrimps, recovers the
  elbow to their ribs and re-guards while you're still threading. Block their
  near hip with your knee before your second hand leaves the mat.
- **Their end range comes early**, and the tissue around the joint gives you less
  warning. Half speed, and be ready to stop at the first sign of resistance
  ending.
- **Do not use your weight as the finishing force.** With a large weight
  difference, "drop your chest into it" becomes a very different instruction. Use
  your hands and stop early.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**If your arms are short:** closing the figure-four around a bigger partner's arm
may be impossible from a standard side control. Grip your own sleeve or forearm,
and choose north–south, where their arm comes to you rather than you reaching
across their body.

**If your hands are small:** the wrist pin is a press, not a grip, which suits
you — put your palm on the back of their hand and drive it into the mat with your
shoulder behind it. Do not try to encircle their wrist.

**If you are much lighter than your partners:** the americana asks you to hold
someone down with your chest while both your arms are busy. That is the worst
possible trade for a small grappler against a big one. Prefer it from
north–south and mount rather than side control, prefer the [kimura](./kimura.md)
as your default shoulder attack, and use the americana mainly against people your
size or smaller.

**If you are much heavier than your partners:** every part of this technique is
easier for you and the margin for error is correspondingly smaller. Use your
arms, not your bodyweight, to apply the rotation, and stop the moment their
elbow stops moving freely.

**If your shoulders are stiff:** threading under their elbow while keeping your
chest on theirs can be uncomfortable. Drop your hip-side knee to the mat and
lower your whole body rather than reaching with the shoulder alone.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Position looks perfect, they're comfortable | They have more external rotation than the technique needs | Adduct the elbow to their ribs, then drag it below the shoulder line toward their hip |
| Their hand keeps sliding up the mat | You're gripping the wrist instead of pinning the hand | Palm flat on the back of their hand, press into the mat, drive from your shoulder |
| Their elbow is up beside their head | You're lifting toward the head instead of dragging toward the hip | Elbow travels down; the hand does not move at all |
| Their shoulder rolls off the mat and it loosens | Scapula isn't pinned | Chest to chest, weight through your sternum; block their far hip |
| Arm straightens and slips out | Elbow was above 90 degrees before you closed the loop | Re-bend the elbow first, or accept it and take the [armbar](./armbar.md) |
| You can't close the figure-four | Their arm is too thick for your reach | Grip your own forearm or sleeve; thread deeper |
| They grab their own belt and it stalls | You built the lock before breaking the grip | Break the grip first — knee on the bicep, or two hands on one |
| You get bridged off every time from mount | Both hands committed with no base | Base wide, head to the mat, or attack from side control instead |
| It only ever works on beginners | You've been relying on them leaving the arm out, not on the mechanics | Section 3 — the mechanics are the elbow height and the scapula |

## 6. Chains

### Their defense → your next attack

| Their defense | Your next attack | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Flexible shoulder, no tap | [Kimura](./kimura.md) — same grip, opposite rotation | Big external rotation rarely comes with big internal rotation |
| Straighten the arm | [Straight armlock / armbar](./armbar.md) | A straight arm is a different, equally available submission |
| Grab their own belt or lapel | Break the grip, or switch to the far arm | The far side is now undefended |
| Turn into you to hide the arm | Take mount, or step over to north–south | Their turn opens the far hip |
| Turn away from you | Take the back, or the far-side [armbar](./armbar.md) | Turning away is the one defense that costs position |
| Bridge hard | Ride the bridge to mount, or switch to knee-on-belly | Their bridge is their base leaving the mat |
| Bury the hand under their own hip | [Wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md) or attack the neck instead | A buried hand cannot defend a collar |

### What feeds into it

| From | The trigger |
|---|---|
| Side control | They frame on your neck or shoulder with the near arm |
| Mount | They push your chest with a bent arm |
| North–south | Both arms already up beside their head |
| Kesa gatame | Their free arm comes up to defend |
| Failed [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) | Their arm is across, bent and available |
| Failed [kimura](./kimura.md) | Same grip, roll the rotation the other way |

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense is easy; late defense barely exists.** Once the figure-four is
closed and your hand is pinned to the mat, your shoulder's fate is mostly in
their hands. Everything worth doing happens before that.

**Early — before the loop closes.** Do not leave a bent arm lying beside your own
head under side control or mount. Frame with a **straight** arm into their neck
or hip, or keep the elbow tight to your ribs. If you must frame with the near
arm, frame low on their hip, not high on their neck — a high frame is the
invitation.

**Middle — they've pinned your hand, but haven't closed the figure-four.** Two
things, in this order. First, **straighten the arm**: a straight arm has no
handle for a figure-four, and it converts their attack into an armbar attempt you
can then defend on its own terms. Second, if straightening isn't available,
**turn onto your side toward them** and drive your elbow down to your own ribs —
this both lowers the elbow out of the rotation and lets your scapula come off the
mat, which is the range they were counting on.

**Late — the loop is closed and your elbow is being dragged toward your hip.**
Grab your own belt, your own lapel, your own thigh — anything that connects the
arm to your body. Then bridge and turn into them to relieve the rotation. Be
honest about the timeline: this works against a loose americana and does nothing
against a tight one, and the difference between them is about a second. **Tap for
the shoulder.** A dislocated shoulder or a torn labrum will cost you a year, and
unlike an elbow, it may never feel quite the same again.

## 8. Drills

**1. Pin and paint (20 per side, no resistance).**
Partner lies with a bent arm beside their head. Pin the hand flat, thread, close
the loop, lift the elbow one centimetre and drag it three centimetres toward
their hip. Stop there. The rep is the *path* of the elbow — most failed
americanas are elbows travelling in the wrong direction.

**2. The range audit (once, 10 minutes).**
Put a light americana on four or five different partners and stop at the point
each of them first feels something. Note how far apart those points are. Then
find the person whose hand goes flat to the mat with no sensation and practise
the adduct-and-drag adjustment on them specifically. This is the single most
valuable ten minutes on this page.

**3. Americana ↔ kimura switch (10 each way).**
From side control with the arm isolated, roll the same figure-four grip from the
americana direction to the kimura direction and back, without releasing. The
switch should take under a second. Against a flexible shoulder it *is* the
technique.

**4. Positional sparring — arm isolated (60-second rounds).**
Start in side control with their near arm bent and their wrist already pinned.
Their job: straighten the arm or turn in. Yours: close the loop and finish.
Rotate partners so you meet several different shoulders in the same session.

## 9. Safety

- **The shoulder is a worse injury than the elbow.** Labral tears and
  dislocations are slow, incomplete healers. Treat every americana as more
  dangerous than the 1/5 difficulty rating implies.
- **Apply slowly, in small increments**, and stop moving the instant their elbow
  stops moving freely.
- **Never add speed to beat flexibility.** If two controlled attempts do nothing,
  their range exceeds your technique. Change direction or change joints — do not
  accelerate. Arriving at a hypermobile person's end range with momentum is the
  classic mechanism for dislocating a shoulder in training.
- **Beginners injure people with this move** more than with almost any other,
  precisely because it is taught on day one and feels easy. Teach the release
  before the finish.
- **Release on any tap** — hand, foot, voice, or a slap you feel rather than see.
- **Ask about shoulder history.** Previous dislocations are extremely common
  among grapplers, and a shoulder that has come out before comes out again
  easily and with less warning.
- **Legality:** legal at every belt in IBJJF gi and no-gi, and in every mainstream
  ruleset. See the belt legality table in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).
