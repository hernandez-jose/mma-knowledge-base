# D'Arce

> A head-and-arm strangle threaded **under their near armpit and across the front of the neck** — the front headlock's most reliable finish, and the answer when a neck is too thick to wrap with a guillotine.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) |
| **Comes from** | Front headlock, half guard top, side control as they turn in, turtle top, a failed guillotine |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 4/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets *as a strangle*. A shallow version that finishes on the spine is a neck crank — IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt |

**Also known as:** Brabo choke, no-gi Brabo

---

## 1. The mechanism

The D'Arce is an [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) assembled from the front, with
your arm supplying the wedge that your bicep supplies in a kata gatame. The loop
contains their head and one of their own arms. **Their own trapped shoulder
closes the carotid on the near side; your threading arm — specifically the meat
of your bicep as it crosses the front of their neck — closes the far side.**

The thread matters more than the grip. Your choking arm enters **under their near
armpit**, palm up, travels across the front of their neck, and your hand emerges
past the **far** side of their neck, where it catches your own opposite bicep.
Your other hand goes behind their head or on their far shoulder. If you have
threaded deeply enough for that grip to exist, your upper arm — not your wrist —
is the surface lying on their neck. If you can only reach your own forearm, you
are shallow, and a shallow D'Arce is a crank on the spine rather than a strangle.

Where does the D'Arce differ from the [anaconda](./anaconda.md)? **Which hole the
arm enters.**

| | Arm enters | Arm exits | Elbow ends up | Finish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **D'Arce** | Under the **near armpit** | Past the **far side of the neck** | Deep, near their far shoulder | Flatten them, drop to your hip, pinch |
| **Anaconda** | Under the **neck** | Out the **near armpit** | Under their chin | Roll them ("gator roll"), then pinch |

Both finish the same way: elbows drawn together, chest driving into their trapped
shoulder, and your **hips falling away** from their head. The grip is a fastener,
not a lever.

## 2. The standard application

From a front headlock or half guard top against a same-size opponent, with their
head down and their near arm extended.

1. Establish the position: their head below their hips, their near arm extended
   under you, your chest heavy on their near shoulder.
2. Flatten the near shoulder toward the mat. A raised shoulder blocks the thread;
   a flattened one opens it.
3. Thread your choking arm **under their near armpit, palm up**, and drive it
   across the front of their neck.
4. Keep threading until your hand appears past the far side of their neck.
   **Your bicep, not your wrist, must be the surface on their neck.**
5. Catch your own opposite bicep. That other hand goes to the back of their head
   or to their far shoulder blade, palm flat.
6. Close the shape: elbows drawn toward each other, chest dropped onto their
   trapped shoulder.
7. **Drop to your hip on the free side and sprawl your hips away from their
   head.** The choke closes when your hips fall, not when your arms curl.
8. If it is still loose, walk your knees toward their head and lower your chest.
9. Release the instant they tap.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The D'Arce is the most build-tolerant of
the head-and-arm chokes, because the armpit is a large, reliable hole that exists
on every human being — where the [guillotine](./guillotine.md) needs a gap under
the jaw that some people simply do not have. But "tolerant" is not "immune", and
the specific adjustments differ a lot from body to body.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | Your hand cannot emerge past the far side of the neck, so you never reach your own bicep | Thread **lower and flatter** — aim at their far collarbone, not their far ear; the path across the base of the neck is shorter and your bicep still lands on the neck |
| **Thin neck** | You over-thread; you get a strong grip with your bicep parked on their far shoulder and nothing on the carotid | Back the arm out 3–5 cm until the crook of your elbow is on their near shoulder line |
| **Broad shoulders / thick deltoids** | The thread is much longer, and their raised shoulder blocks the entry | Straighten and pin their trapped arm before threading; flatten the shoulder to the mat with your chest first |
| **Short arms** | Their trapped arm doesn't extend, so their own shoulder never wedges into their neck | Supply the wedge yourself: near knee against the back of their trapped shoulder, driving it forward |
| **Long arms** | They post the far hand and stand up, or pinch the near elbow to their ribs so you can't thread | Bait the arm out with a guillotine threat, or drag the elbow across with your free hand first; then take the grip before the position |
| **Much heavier than you** | They stand up, shrug you off, or drive over the top from their knees | Attach to their hips with a leg over the back; finish from your side using bodyweight, never from your knees |
| **Much lighter than you** | They spin out or granby before you lock; and the choke closes far faster than you expect | Grip first, position second; block the far hip with your knee; apply slowly |
| **Very flexible / good scramblers** | They roll forward through it to relieve the pressure | Block the roll with your near knee against their shoulder — or ride the roll; a D'Arce finishes in motion if the elbows stay pinched |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

Start with the good news: this is the choke you switch to when the guillotine
fails on this build, because you are not trying to encircle a thick neck with a
fixed-length arm from the front. You are entering through the armpit, which
bypasses the trapezius entirely. But you can still run out of arm before your
hand emerges on the far side, and if you cannot reach your own bicep, you have no
grip and no choke.

Five adjustments, in order:

1. **Thread lower and flatter.** Aim your fingers at their **far collarbone**,
   not their far ear. The path across the base of the neck is measurably shorter
   than the path across the throat, and it still works — because the choking
   surface is your **bicep**, which will end up on the neck regardless of where
   your fingers finish. People fail this choke by trying to thread high on thick
   necks and running out of arm halfway.
2. **Pull the trapped arm further across their own body before you thread.**
   Every centimetre their trapped elbow travels toward their far hip is a
   centimetre less neck for your arm to cross. Drag it with your free hand, or
   trap their wrist under your knee, before the threading arm goes anywhere.
3. **Flatten the shoulder first.** Drive their near shoulder to the mat with your
   chest and get on top of it. A flattened shoulder loses height, opens the
   armpit, and drops the whole neck a few centimetres lower relative to your arm.
4. **Change the grip target.** If your hand will not reach your own bicep, take
   what it can reach: your own **forearm**, or a hand hooked over your own
   shoulder. An "incomplete" D'Arce grip still finishes if your hips fall away
   and your elbows pinch, because the grip was never the choke.
5. **Roll them slightly toward the choking side** as you drop to your hip.
   Rotating their body a few degrees toward your threading arm uses gravity to
   drive their own shoulder into their own neck — free compression that costs no
   arm length.

If your arm still stops at the wrist, you have a **crank**, not a strangle.
Release it. Cranks are banned by IBJJF at every belt, and this is the build where
people apply them by accident.

### Thin neck

The over-threading problem. Your arm sails through, you grab your bicep easily,
the grip feels enormous — and your bicep is sitting on their far shoulder with
open air on both carotids. It looks like the tightest D'Arce you have ever had
and it will hold them for five minutes without finishing.

- **Back out 3–5 cm.** The reference point: the crook of your elbow should sit on
  their **near shoulder line**, with your bicep across the side of their neck.
  Feel for the neck with your upper arm, not with your hand.
- **Finish with the chest, not the arm.** On a thin neck, pulling the head with
  the threading arm is what converts this into a crank. Drop your chest onto
  their trapped shoulder and let your hips fall instead.
- **Expect a fast finish** once the bicep is placed correctly. Go progressively.

### Broad shoulders and thick deltoids

Their deltoid does the near carotid very well, so half of the choke is free. The
cost is a longer thread and a shoulder that physically blocks the entry.

- **Straighten and pin the trapped arm before you thread.** An extended arm lays
  the shoulder flat; a bent one keeps it bunched up in your way. Pin their wrist
  to the mat with your free hand or your knee while your other arm goes in.
- **Enter along the mat, not over the top.** Slide your hand between their
  shoulder and the mat rather than trying to burrow through the muscle above it.
- **Drop your chest into the notch** between their trapped shoulder and their
  head — that is where your weight actually reaches their neck on this build.

### Their short arms

The wedge needs their trapped arm extended so their own shoulder is driven into
their own neck. A short arm does not reach past your body and the near carotid
never closes properly. You end up with an impressive grip and a half-choke.

- **Replace their arm with your knee.** Put your near knee against the **back of
  their trapped shoulder** and drive it forward while you sprawl your hips back.
  You are manufacturing the extension their arm cannot supply.
- **Or use your ribcage.** From half guard top, dropping your ribs onto the back
  of their trapped shoulder does the same job with your bodyweight.
- Short-armed opponents are also worse at defending the thread — the elbow-to-ribs
  pinch that long-armed people use to deny the entry needs length. Take advantage
  and go early.

### Their long arms

Two distinct problems. Long arms can pinch the near elbow tight to the ribs so
there is no gap to thread, and long arms can post far away and stand up out of
the front headlock entirely.

- **Bait the arm out.** Threaten a [guillotine](./guillotine.md) or a snap-down;
  both make them reach. The classic sequence is guillotine attempt → they defend
  by driving the arm in → you take the D'Arce on the arm they just extended.
- **Drag the elbow first.** Use your free hand to pull their near elbow across
  their own body before the threading arm commits. Threading into a pinched elbow
  is a strength contest with the biggest muscle group they have.
- **Grip first, position second.** Against a long-armed opponent who wants to
  stand, the grip is what survives the scramble. Get your hand to your bicep and
  worry about your hips afterwards.
- **Block the stand-up** with a leg over their back or a knee against their far
  hip.

### Much heavier than you

The D'Arce is genuinely good against bigger opponents — you catch them driving
forward, which is what heavier people do — but the finish cannot be a wrestling
match on your knees.

- **Attach to their hips.** A leg thrown over their back, or half guard top with
  a knee across, stops them standing up and walking out of it.
- **Finish from your side, not your knees.** Drop to the hip on the free side and
  let your bodyweight hang on the shape. On your knees, the finish is arm
  strength versus a bigger person's neck; on your side, it is your entire mass
  versus their carotid.
- **Take it off the sprawl.** A heavier opponent shooting a takedown gives you
  the deepest, most committed front headlock you will ever get; that is the
  moment, not thirty seconds later.
- **Do not hold it while they stand.** Same rule as the guillotine: release
  rather than be lifted.

### Much lighter than you

The thread happens instantly and the finish arrives before you have registered
it. The dangers are all on their side.

- **Apply slowly and progressively.** Smaller necks close under a D'Arce with
  very little warning, and the crank version arrives first if you rush.
- **Grip first, block the spin.** Lighter opponents escape the D'Arce by spinning
  out or granby-rolling before you close, not by defending the choke. Your near
  knee at their far hip stops both.
- **Watch that your bicep is on the neck** and your forearm is not simply
  levering their skull toward their chest. On a small frame it is easy to do the
  latter without noticing.

### Very flexible and good scramblers

Their answer is to roll forward through the choke, or to invert and spin out.
Both are fast and both happen before you are ready.

- **Block the roll** by driving your near knee into their shoulder and keeping
  your hips low and heavy.
- **Or ride it.** A D'Arce with the elbows already pinched finishes perfectly
  well mid-roll — go with them, land on your side, and keep the shape. Panicking
  and opening your elbows to post is how the grip is lost.
- **Take the position if the choke goes.** A defender who rolls out of a D'Arce
  usually surrenders their back or lands under side control; both are wins.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Thread low and flat as your default, grab your own **forearm**
rather than your bicep, and treat the hip drop as your primary source of
compression — dropping to your side is worth several centimetres of arm. Also
lean on the near-knee-in-the-shoulder wedge, which does not care about your arm
length at all.

**Long arms.** You will over-thread. Set the reference point consciously: crook of
the elbow on their near shoulder line, bicep on the neck. A grip you can take
easily on a thin-necked partner is usually a grip that has gone too far.

**Thick arms.** Your bicep fills the loop beautifully once you are in — but you
may not fit under their armpit in the first place. Flatten their shoulder to the
mat first, and enter along the mat rather than through the muscle.

**Small hands or a weak grip.** Bicep and forearm grips only. Never grip your own
wrist here; the D'Arce loads the grip laterally and a wrist grip slides.

**Much lighter than your partners.** The whole finish must come from bodyweight
and hips. Drop to your side every time, attach to their hips so they cannot
stand, and take the choke off their forward drive rather than trying to
manufacture it from a static position.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You can't reach your own bicep | Thread is too high and too shallow for that neck | Re-thread aiming at their far collarbone, and pull their trapped elbow further across first |
| Massive grip, no reaction | Over-threaded — your bicep is on their far shoulder, not their neck | Back out 3–5 cm until your elbow crook is on their near shoulder line |
| They complain about their neck and then escape | Shallow thread: this is a spinal crank, not a strangle | Release. Re-thread deeper or switch to the anaconda |
| It's tight but only one side is compressed | Their trapped shoulder isn't wedged — their arm is bent or not extended | Straighten and pin the trapped arm; drive the shoulder with your knee or ribs |
| You're squeezing with your arms and going nowhere | You're finishing from your knees | Drop to your hip on the free side and let your hips fall away from their head |
| They stand up out of it | Nothing was attached to their hips | Leg over the back or knee across the far hip before you invest in the grip |
| They roll forward and you lose it | You opened your elbows to post | Keep the elbows pinched and go with the roll; land on your side and finish there |
| Your arm gets stuck at the elbow | Their shoulder is raised and blocking | Flatten the shoulder with your chest before threading, and enter along the mat |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They pinch the near elbow in and you can't thread | [Anaconda](./anaconda.md) — go under the neck instead of under the arm |
| They flatten out and stop defending the arm | [Arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md) — same wedge, from the side |
| They turn away from you | Take the back; the trapped arm is already out of the fight |
| They roll forward through it | Follow the roll and finish, or land in mount / side control |
| They post the far hand and stand | Peruvian necktie, or drop to a single leg and put them back down |
| They defend the neck by burying the chin | Switch to the guillotine if the head lifts, or go behind — the front headlock's best exit is usually the back |
| They get the trapped hand back to their own forehead | Reset the head position; re-drag the elbow before threading again |

The [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), the D'Arce and the
[anaconda](./anaconda.md) are one wedge with three entries. If a body defeats one
of them, one of the other two usually fits.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (before the thread).** Two rules, and they cover nearly
everything: **do not leave your head below your hips**, and **do not leave a near
arm extended and unsupported.** In a front headlock, get your near hand to your
own forehead or under your own chin — that closes the armpit and denies the
thread. Post the far hand wide, widen your base, and start walking toward their
choking-arm side.

**Middle defense (arm threaded, grip not yet locked).** Fight the grip, not the
choke. Get your trapped shoulder off the mat and drive your head into them —
posture is the enemy of a head-and-arm wedge. Simultaneously **turn toward their
threading arm**: moving toward the choke unwinds it, moving away tightens it. If
you can get the trapped hand's palm to the mat and straighten that arm, the
wedge collapses.

**Late defense (grip locked, they are on their hip, hips sprawled away).** Much
worse than early defense, and the D'Arce is a specifically dangerous one to
gamble on because it is a **creeping** choke — it tightens gradually over several
seconds and defenders routinely misjudge how much time they have left.
Furthermore, a shallow D'Arce compresses the spine before it compresses the
carotids, so "toughing it out" risks a neck injury rather than a nap. **Tap.**

Positional detail lives in [turtle escapes](../escapes/turtle-escapes.md),
[side control escapes](../escapes/side-control-escapes.md) and
[knee-on-belly escapes](../escapes/knee-on-belly-escapes.md).

## 8. Drills

**1. Thread reps on three necks.** With three partners of clearly different neck
thickness, thread and take a grip on each — no finishing. On the thickest, thread
low toward the far collarbone; on the thinnest, back out until your bicep is on
the neck. **10 reps each, both sides.** The skill is choosing the depth, not
getting deep.

**2. Hip-drop finish.** Assemble a loose D'Arce with your partner flat and your
weight on your knees. Without changing anything above the waist, drop to your hip
on the free side and sprawl your hips away. Have them tell you how much it
changes. **3 sets of 8 per side.**

**3. Front headlock positional sparring.** Start in a front headlock, their near
arm already in. Top player wins with a D'Arce, an anaconda, or a back take;
bottom player wins by standing up, getting to guard, or sitting out. **90-second
rounds, reset, switch.** This is the highest-value single drill on this page.

**4. Guillotine-to-D'Arce.** Partner defends a guillotine by driving their arm in
and their head down — the standard defense. You transition to the D'Arce on that
arm. **10 reps per side.** This is how most D'Arces actually happen in live
rolling.

## 9. Safety

A blood choke and **legal at every belt in every major ruleset** when it is
actually a blood choke. The failure case is specific and common: a **shallow**
D'Arce, where only your wrist and forearm reach the neck, compresses the cervical
spine into flexion instead of compressing the carotids. That is a **neck crank**,
which IBJJF bans at every belt (see [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part XII),
and it hurts people. The test is simple — if you cannot reach your own bicep or
forearm and you are pulling on their head to compensate, let go.

Apply progressively. The D'Arce creeps: it tightens over five to ten seconds and
the person inside it often does not register the danger until it is very late.
That means two things — release the moment they tap, and if a partner suddenly
goes quiet without tapping, release first and ask afterwards.

If you finish through a roll, roll over your **shoulder**, never your head, and
make sure the mat is clear behind you.
