# Loop Choke

> The punishment for putting your head down. One collar grip, and then your own body rotation loops your arm all the way around their neck — the turn is the choke, not the pull.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke) — 🥋 gi-only |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, seated/butterfly guard, turtle top, front headlock, a stuffed guard pass, a snap-down from standing |
| **Difficulty** | 3/5 |
| **How common** | 3/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major gi rulesets |

**Also known as:** the loop, rolling loop choke, *ude nui jime*-family collar chokes

---

## 1. The mechanism

A blood choke with two compressing surfaces: **their own collar** against one carotid, and **the blade of your forearm or your bicep** against the other. Your arm and the cloth together form a closed loop around their neck, and their head is inside it.

What makes this choke distinctive — and what makes people fail at it for years — is how the loop closes. **It closes by your rotation, not by your pull.** Once the collar grip is set and your forearm is across the back of their neck, pulling with your arm just drags their head toward you and opens the loop at the front. Turning your torso away from their head, and dropping your elbow past their skull toward the mat, sweeps your whole arm around the neck and shrinks the loop from every direction at once. Your arm behaves like a hand of a clock, and their neck is at the pivot.

The second thing to understand is the entry condition. **The loop choke needs their head to be down and forward.** It is not a choke you apply to a postured opponent — it is the specific punishment for someone who ducks their head under your arm to pass, to snap out of a grip, or to drive into you. Which is why it appears constantly against people who defend the [cross collar choke](./cross-collar-choke.md) by burying their head, and why every good loop choke setup is really a setup to get their head down.

---

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard against a same-size opponent whose head is on your right side. Everything mirrors.

1. **Get their head down and across.** Break their posture with a collar or head grip, or bait it — a threatened sweep or a strong sleeve pull will usually make them drive their head forward. Their forehead should end up past your centreline, near your right shoulder.
2. **Reach your right hand over the back of their neck** and take a deep grip on their **far** (left) collar, **four fingers inside, thumb outside**. Your right forearm now lies diagonally across the back of their neck. **Four fingers in, not thumb in** — you need the radial blade of your forearm as a compressing surface, and thumb-in points the wrong bones at the neck.
3. **Kill their posting arm.** Your left hand grips their right sleeve or elbow and pulls it across their own centreline, or frames on their far shoulder. **This is the grip that decides whether the choke happens** — if they can post and lift their head, the loop opens and nothing you do with your right arm matters.
4. **Keep their head down** with pressure from your chest or by pulling their collar toward your own hip. Head up = no loop.
5. **Rotate.** Turn your torso hard to your left, away from their head, hip-escaping to that side as you go. Do not pull. The rotation drags your right elbow down past their skull toward the mat and sweeps your forearm around the far side of their neck.
6. **Drop the elbow to the mat** on the far side of their head and curl your right hand in toward your own chest. The collar closes the far carotid; your forearm and bicep close the near one.
7. **Finish with your hips, not your arm** — keep turning and, if you have it, bring a leg over their back or press your knee into their shoulder to stop them rolling out of the rotation.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The loop choke is a fixed-circumference loop made of your arm plus a length of their collar, and every build below either changes the size of the loop, changes whether their head can get into it, or changes whether they can post their way out. Their gi is the third compressing surface, so gi fit changes this choke as much as their neck does.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Thick neck, heavy traps | Loop is too small; your hand never returns to your chest and the arm sits on their trap | Grip **shallower** — far collarbone, not behind the neck — so cloth spans the neck instead of arm; drive their head further past your centreline; rotate further; or take the **arm-in** loop |
| Thin neck | Loop closes with slack in it; you gather cloth and nothing bites | Grip **deeper**, past the back of the neck; roll the wrist to eat slack before you rotate |
| Broad shoulders | Their deltoid jams your bicep and stops the elbow travelling past their head | Hip-escape away *first* to make room, then rotate; take the grip lower on the collar |
| Long arms | They post far out and lift their head out of the loop | Kill the post before you rotate — sleeve grip pulled across their centreline |
| Much heavier / much stronger | They simply lift their head and rip out of it | Change the entry: take it from turtle top or from under a committed passing attempt, where their own forward drive closes it |
| Much lighter | They pull their head straight back out of the loop | Block the far shoulder with your knee; take the grip before you break their posture |
| Very stiff / strong posture | They never put their head down, so the entry never exists | Manufacture it — snap-down, sleeve pull, or bait a sweep so they drive forward; or attack from turtle top where the head is already down |
| Very flexible | They roll forward through the loop | Follow the roll; the loop choke survives a forward roll better than almost any other guard attack |
| Tight / thick / starched gi | Collar will not fold around the neck; deep grips unavailable | Take the collar *lip*, keep the wrist rigid, and let the stiff cloth act as a blade — the shallow version is the better version here |
| Loose, worn-out gi | Deep grip, two handfuls of slack, nothing closes | Roll the wrist a full turn; pin the collar to their neck with your chest as you rotate |

---

### Thick neck and heavy traps

The loop choke has a hard geometric limit: the loop's circumference is your forearm plus your bicep plus the length of collar you have gripped, and if their neck and traps are bigger than that, your hand never comes back to your own chest and the whole arm ends up resting on the muscle behind their neck.

The counter-intuitive fix is to **grip shallower, not deeper.** Take the far collar at their **collarbone** rather than behind the neck. That sounds backwards, but it works because it changes what is spanning the neck: with a deep grip, your *arm* has to go all the way around, and your arm is a fixed length. With a shallow grip, a length of *collar* spans the far side of the neck, and cloth costs you nothing. You have converted an arm-length problem into a cloth-length problem, and you have far more cloth than arm.

Second: **drive their head further across your centreline before you rotate.** If their head is only just past your midline, the part of them sitting in your loop is neck *plus trap ridge*. Push their head further across — past your opposite shoulder — and what sits in the crook is neck alone, above the trapezius. On a thick-necked opponent this can be the difference of five or six centimetres of effective circumference, which is everything.

Third: **rotate further and add a hip escape.** Turn your torso until your chest faces almost 90° away from their head, and shrimp your hips out to that side as you turn. The extra rotation closes the loop from a direction their neck size does not defend.

If all three fail, **take the arm-in loop.** Trap their near arm inside the loop alongside their neck. This makes the thing you are encircling bigger, and it still works better — because their own shoulder now presses into their own carotid, exactly the way an arm triangle does. Against a neck you genuinely cannot get around, arm-in is the correct version rather than a fallback.

### Thin neck

Everything wraps easily and then nothing happens, because you have a loop with air in it. You will feel the collar bunched in your fist and their neck moving freely inside your arm.

**Grip deeper** — all the way past the back of the neck, so the collar is drawn tight against the far side before you even begin — and **take the slack up before you rotate**. Roll your gripping wrist a full turn inward so a wrap of cloth gathers around it, then rotate. On a slim neck the rotation has less material to compress, so all the tightening has to be present before you turn.

Also aim more carefully. On a small neck it is easy to end up with your forearm across the windpipe rather than the carotid. Angle your elbow drop slightly toward their far ear rather than straight down their sternum.

### Broad shoulders

The problem is travel, not size. Their deltoid sits against the inside of your bicep and physically blocks your elbow from getting past their head, so the rotation stalls halfway and the loop never closes.

**Make room with your hips before you rotate.** Hip-escape away from their head first — a single strong shrimp to the side you are about to turn toward — which creates the two or three inches of space your elbow needs to clear their shoulder. Then rotate into the space you just made. Rotating first and hoping to shove through a deltoid does not work.

Take the collar grip a little lower as well. A grip high on the collar leaves your forearm sitting on the top of their trapezius where their shoulder mass is thickest; a grip nearer their collarbone drops the whole arm into the softer channel beside the neck.

### Long-armed opponents

Long arms are a posting problem. They put a hand on the mat far out in front of themselves, straighten that arm, and lift their head clean out of the loop — no strength required, just structure.

**Kill the post before you commit to the rotation.** Grip the sleeve or the elbow of their posting arm with your free hand and pull it across their own centreline, or under your own armpit. An arm that cannot reach the mat cannot lift a head. This is the single highest-value adjustment against tall opponents and it applies at almost every belt: the loop choke fails to long-armed people far more often through the post than through the neck.

### Much heavier or much stronger than you

Against a much bigger opponent, the closed-guard version is a losing race — you rotate, they lift their head, and mass beats leverage.

Change the entry rather than the technique. The loop choke's power source is **their** forward commitment, so attack it from positions where their weight is already travelling into you and they cannot easily reverse it:

- **From turtle top.** Their head is already down and their weight is already forward. Reach over the back of the neck, take the far collar, and rotate around their head. This is the highest-percentage loop choke available and it barely cares about the weight difference.
- **From a committed guard pass.** As they drive their head past your hip to pass, you already have the entry — their own passing pressure is what closes your loop.
- **Off a snap-down from standing or seated.** Snap the head down, take the collar as it comes, rotate.

And sequence it properly: **grip, head down, rotate.** Not grip, rotate, hope. Against a strong opponent the head must be down before you turn, because your rotation cannot fight their neck extensors.

### Much lighter than you

Lighter opponents beat the loop by simply reversing out — head straight back, hips back, gone — because they can move faster than you can turn.

Block the exit before you turn: your knee or your shin against their far shoulder, or a leg over their back. And take the collar grip *before* you break their posture rather than after — the grip is what lets you draw their head back down if they start to escape, and against a mobile lightweight you will need it more than once.

### Very stiff or very strong posture

Some people simply never put their head down. Against them the loop choke has no entry, and no amount of technique fixes an entry condition that does not exist.

Manufacture it. A hard **snap-down** — two hands on the head or collar, sharp downward yank — makes the head go down for a fraction of a second, which is all you need if the collar grip is already in place. A strong **sleeve pull** to one side does the same. Baiting works too: threaten a sweep they have to drive forward to stop, and take the loop off their defensive drive. Or ignore the guard entirely and hunt them in **turtle**, where their head is down by definition.

### Very flexible

Flexible opponents will somersault forward through the loop rather than fight it. Do not resist that — **follow it.** The loop choke is unusually robust through a roll, because both the collar grip and your arm position remain valid at any orientation. Ride the roll, land on top or on your side, keep rotating, and finish. This is where the name "rolling loop choke" comes from and it is a real, high-percentage version rather than a save.

### Tight, thick or heavily starched gi

Their jacket is the third compressing surface, and a stiff one changes this choke in both directions.

**What breaks:** the collar will not fold around the neck. It stands off, springs back when you release it, and four fingers barely fit inside. A deep grip past the back of the neck is essentially unavailable on a heavily starched collar, so the standard "grip the tag" instruction fails outright.

**What improves:** a rigid collar transmits force along its whole length like a plank. On a soft gi a shallow grip is useless because the cloth folds and the force dies in the fabric. On a stiff one, the same shallow grip drives a hard edge straight into the side of the neck as you rotate. So on a starched gi, **take the collar's top lip with four fingers, lock the wrist, and rotate** — the shallow version is not a compromise here, it is the better technique. Conveniently, this is the same adjustment you want on a thick neck.

Most rulesets police excessive stiffness at the gi check, so in competition you can broadly rely on a conforming collar. In an open mat you cannot assume anything, and the first grip will tell you which choke you are doing.

### Loose, worn-out or oversized gi

The reverse problem, and it is the more frustrating one. The collar folds around anything, your hand goes wherever you want, and then you rotate and finish nothing because you have a fistful of stretched cotton and the loop is fifteen centimetres too long.

**Roll the wrist** a full turn inward after the grip lands to gather the slack. **Grip lower** — the collar is tightest where it is anchored near the sternum and slackest at the top. And **pin the collar to their neck** as you rotate: on a loose jacket the collar peels off the neck and travels with your hand instead of staying on their throat, which is why the choke seems to slide off. Press your chest or your free forearm against the cloth on their neck so the collar cannot follow your rotation.

Expect them to strip a loose grip more easily too — the whole jacket slides on their body, so they can shrug the collar out from under you without ever touching your hand.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Your loop is short, which makes thick necks genuinely hard. Make the shallow-grip, cloth-spans-the-neck version your default rather than your fallback, and prioritise driving their head across your centreline — that adjustment is worth more to you than to anyone.

**Long arms.** You can loop almost anyone, and you will consistently be too loose. Grip deeper than feels necessary, roll the wrist to take up slack, and rotate further, since your elbow has further to travel before it clears their head.

**Small hands.** Four fingers in a thick or starched collar is a struggle. Hook two or three fingers on the lip and keep the wrist rigid — for this choke the grip only has to prevent the loop from lengthening, and a rigid wrist does more work than a full fist.

**Thick forearms.** Your forearm fills more of the loop, so it closes sooner than your reach suggests. Take shallower grips than the instructional you learned from, and go slower on the finish — this choke arrives faster for you than for a slim-armed partner.

**Poor hip mobility.** The rotation is a hip movement before it is a torso movement. If you cannot shrimp hard to the side, the loop stalls. Prefer the turtle-top and front-headlock entries, where the rotation is a walk around their head rather than a shrimp.

**Lighter than most of your partners.** The loop choke is a good fit for you specifically because it is powered by *their* forward drive. Stop hunting it from closed guard against bigger people and start hunting it from turtle top and off failed passes, where their commitment is the engine.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You pull hard and their head just comes toward you | You are pulling instead of rotating | Freeze the grip; turn your torso away from their head and drop the elbow to the mat |
| Your arm is resting on their traps, not their neck | Loop too small; head not far enough across | Grip shallower at the collarbone and drive their head past your opposite shoulder |
| The loop closes but there is slack in it | Thin neck or worn gi | Grip deeper and roll the wrist a full turn before rotating |
| Your elbow will not clear their head | Their shoulder is blocking it | Hip-escape away first to create space, then rotate |
| They post a hand and stand their head up | You never killed the posting arm | Sleeve or elbow grip pulled across their centreline before you rotate |
| They never put their head down at all | No entry condition exists | Snap them down, bait a sweep, or hunt it from turtle top instead |
| The collar peels off their neck as you turn | Loose jacket travelling with your hand | Pin the cloth to their neck with your chest or free forearm |
| They roll forward and out | Nothing was blocking the roll — and that is fine | Follow the roll and finish; this is the rolling loop choke, not a failure |
| It is tight on the windpipe and they are coughing | Elbow dropping down their sternum rather than toward their far ear | Re-aim the elbow drop diagonally toward their far ear |
| You get passed while attacking | You committed both arms and abandoned your legs | Keep a foot on the hip or a knee on their shoulder throughout |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| Posture up hard and lift the head | [Cross collar choke](./cross-collar-choke.md) or an armbar — the head is up and the arms are extended |
| Post a strong arm to stop the rotation | Take the omoplata or the arm drag on the posted arm |
| Duck the head even further and drive | Take the back — the head-down drive is the classic back-take invitation |
| Roll forward through it | Follow the roll and finish; or land on top in a front headlock |
| Pull the head straight back out | Guillotine — the head is retreating along exactly the right line |
| Turtle up to survive it | Clock choke, or the [Peruvian necktie](./peruvian-necktie.md) from front headlock |
| Strip the collar grip cleanly | Re-take the near collar and attack the [cross collar](./cross-collar-choke.md) from the other side |

Feeding in: the loop choke is the standard punishment for anyone who defends a cross collar choke by burying their head, for anyone who ducks their head to knee-cut past your guard, and for anyone who reacts to a snap-down by driving forward. It is also one of the best things available from turtle top, where the entry condition is permanently satisfied.

## 7. Defending it

**Early — and this one really is decided early.** Keep your head up. That is not a platitude; it is the entire defence, because the choke has no entry against a postured opponent. If you must lower your head to pass or to fight grips, keep it **outside** their arm, not under it, and never let a hand travel over the back of your neck unopposed. Fight the collar grip with two hands on their one gripping hand and strip toward their thumb.

**Middle — collar grip set, rotation beginning.** Do two things at once. **Post and stand your head up**, hard, on a straight arm — an extended post beats a rotation because it moves the pivot point away from them. And **turn your head toward their gripping elbow**, not away from it; turning away feeds your near carotid straight into their forearm. Simultaneously, back your hips away so the loop lengthens. If you can get your near hand inside the collar as a wedge between the cloth and your neck, do that too.

**Late — elbow past your head, hand curling to their chest, hips turning.** This is very bad and it is bad quickly. The loop choke has a reputation for putting people out because they do not realise how tight it is until it is finished, and because the attacker is often turned away and cannot see their face. There is no real late defence: rolling forward is the only structural option and against someone who follows the roll it changes nothing. **Late defense here is much worse than early defense** — the pressure curve is short and steep and the warning is minimal. Tap early and honestly, and tap loudly, because they may not be able to see you.

## 8. Drills

**1. Rotation-only reps (15 reps per side).** Partner kneels with their head down and offers no resistance. Set the grip, then close the choke using **only** hip and torso rotation — hands frozen. When it finishes without a pull, you have learned the mechanism. Most people cannot do this on the first day and everyone can by the tenth.

**2. Head-down entry hunting (3 × 2 minutes).** From closed guard, they attempt to open your guard and pass normally. You may only attack the loop choke, and only when their head goes down. This teaches the entry condition — which is the real skill — rather than the finish.

**3. Body-type ladder (10 reps each).** Same choke on the thickest neck in the gym, the thinnest, and someone your size, deliberately changing grip depth each time and saying it out loud: *shallow / deep.* Note where their head has to be relative to your centreline in each case.

**4. Gi swap (2 × 5 minutes).** Same partner, same drill, in the stiffest gi in the gym and then the softest. Two different chokes. This is the fastest way to learn the shallow-versus-deep grip rule.

**5. Turtle-top positional round (90 seconds).** Start with them turtled and you riding them. You may attack the loop choke and its chains; they escape normally. This is where the choke is highest-percentage and it is under-drilled.

---

## 9. Safety

**The loop choke puts more people to sleep by accident than most chokes in this folder**, and the reason is structural, not technical: you finish it turned away from them, often with your own arm across your body and your eyes somewhere else, so you cannot see their face. Meanwhile the pressure curve is short — it goes from "annoying" to "done" in a very small window, and people who have never felt one do not recognise the warning.

So: **apply it progressively, keep your head where you can see or hear them, and pause the instant anything changes.** Agree with new partners that a verbal tap or a foot tapping the mat counts, because their arms are frequently trapped or posting.

If it is on their windpipe rather than their carotid, they will cough and go red and not tap. That is a bad choke, not a determined opponent — re-aim the elbow drop and back off the pressure. Do not substitute cranking their head forward for a strangle that is not landing; that is a neck crank and IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt.

Legality is otherwise simple: it is a strangle, legal at every IBJJF belt and in every major gi ruleset. It is gi-only — without a collar there is no second compressing surface, and what remains is an arm-in guillotine or a headlock rather than a loop choke.
