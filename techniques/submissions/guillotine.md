# Guillotine

> The front-headlock strangle available from guard, from standing, and off every badly-shot takedown — the most-attempted submission in most gyms, and the one most often applied as a crank without the person applying it realising.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Strangle (blood choke when high; a flexion crank when low) |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, butterfly and half guard bottom, front headlock, standing, off a stuffed shot |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets *as a strangle*. A low-elevation version that finishes on the spine is a neck crank — IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt |

**Also known as:** mae hadaka jime, front choke, front headlock choke; the palm-to-palm arm-out version is the **10-finger guillotine / Marcelotine** (atlas: Diff 3, Com 3)

---

## 1. The mechanism

There is no single guillotine. There are four, and they finish on different
tissue. Two axes: **high elevation vs low elevation**, and **arm-in vs arm-out**.

**High elevation** means their head is lifted so their neck is *above* the line
of their own shoulders and extended. That extension exposes several centimetres
of throat below the jaw, and the blade of your radius lands on it. Applied
correctly, the loop closes both **carotids** — one under your forearm, one
against your bicep or their own shoulder — and it is a genuine blood choke.

**Low elevation** means their head is down, chin toward their chest, and your
forearm is sitting low across the base of the neck near the collarbones. You
cannot reach the carotids from there; their own shoulders are in the way. What
you are doing instead is levering the head into flexion against the cervical
spine. It produces taps, quickly, on plenty of people — but the tap is neck pain,
not a strangle. On thick-necked and short-necked opponents, *almost every
guillotine defaults to this without you choosing it.*

**Arm-in** means their near arm is inside the wrap, between their head and your
body. Their own deltoid closes one carotid and your forearm closes the other —
mechanically it is a standing-up [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md). It is more
forgiving of thick necks and much easier to hold.

**Arm-out** means both of their arms are outside the loop. Tighter, faster, and
much less forgiving: if they get one hand inside, it dies immediately.

The finish in all versions comes from **hips forward and chest back at the same
time**, not from pulling with your arms. Your arms hold the shape; your body
supplies the force.

---

## 2. The standard application

High-elevation, arm-in, from closed guard against a same-size opponent. This is
the default because it is the version that survives contact with real bodies.

1. Catch the head as their posture breaks. Their head goes to the choking-arm
   side of your chest, not to the middle.
2. Slide the choking arm through until the **blade of your radius — the thumb
   side of your forearm — is under their chin against the throat, thumb pointing
   at the ceiling.** A bent wrist puts the soft flat of your forearm on the neck
   and costs you 3–4 cm of bite.
3. Decide arm-in or arm-out **now**, before you take a grip. Arm-in: leave their
   near arm inside and clear their shoulder off your sternum with your free hand.
   Arm-out: fight the arm out before you close anything.
4. Take the grip. Arm-in: choking hand to your own opposite bicep, elbow high.
   Arm-out: gable / palm-to-palm.
5. **Elevate.** Shrug your choking-side shoulder up and pull their head toward
   your own armpit so their neck sits above their shoulder line. This is the step
   that makes it a choke rather than a crank, and it is the step people skip.
6. Angle your chest. Arm-out: turn toward the choking-arm side. Arm-in: turn
   *away* from it, so your ribs press their trapped shoulder into their own neck.
7. Finish: **drive your hips forward into them and pull your chest back at the
   same time.** Legs stay closed around their waist or, better, one foot moves to
   their hip and extends them.
8. If it is not there within a few seconds, open the guard and put a foot on the
   hip to add extension, or let go and take the position instead. A guillotine
   you hold too long is a Von Flue choke waiting to happen.

## 3. Adjusting for their body

**This is the section that matters.** The guillotine varies more by neck
architecture than any other choke, because unlike the triangle and the arm
triangle it has no second wedge to fall back on unless you deliberately build
one by taking the arm in. Two people of the same weight can require completely
different guillotines.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Thick neck, heavy traps** | Your forearm cannot get past the trap shelf onto the throat; you finish on the jaw or the collarbone | Switch to **arm-in**, elevate hard so the neck extends, and use the blade of the wrist along the jawline rather than trying to wrap further |
| **Short neck / high shoulders** | There is no gap between jaw and collarbone at any elevation | Stop trying to wrap. Go arm-in and treat it as an arm triangle, or abandon to the [D'Arce](./darce.md) or the back |
| **Thin / long neck** | You over-insert; the blade lands past the throat on the far jaw and finishes nothing | Stop with your elbow under their chin, not past it; take arm-out with a short grip (hand to opposite bicep) |
| **Long arms** | They get a hand inside the loop early and post far away to rebuild posture | Trap the near hand with your leg, go arm-in, and control the trapped elbow |
| **Short arms** | With the arm in, their trapped arm doesn't extend far enough to wedge the shoulder into their own neck | Drive the trapped shoulder in yourself — knee or thigh against the back of it — instead of relying on their arm's length |
| **Much heavier than you** | From closed guard they stack you, stand up, or simply walk you into the mat | Do not finish from closed guard. Open, foot on the hip, or take the arm-in from your side; release rather than get lifted |
| **Much lighter than you** | The loop is far too big; the head slides out backwards, and any pressure you do apply is a crank | Shortest grip you own, pull straight up rather than around, and go slowly — this is where people hurt training partners |
| **Very flexible / scrambly** | They spin, invert or roll out before the grip closes | Control the far hip with a leg over the back before you finish the grip |
| **Very muscular, thick shoulders and lats** | Arm-out, they shrug and armour the neck completely | Arm-in — their own bulk becomes the wedge that closes one side |

### Thick neck and heavy traps

The classic guillotine killer, and the reason many people conclude the guillotine
"doesn't work in competition." What actually happens is that your forearm cannot
travel past the trapezius shelf onto the throat, so you end up with the wrist on
their jaw (a crank), or across the top of their shoulder (nothing at all), and
you squeeze anyway.

In order of what to try:

1. **Switch to arm-in.** With their near arm inside, you no longer need your
   forearm to close both sides of the neck — their own deltoid closes one and you
   only have to reach one carotid. This single change converts an impossible
   geometry problem into a solvable one, and it is why arm-in is the correct
   default on thick-necked opponents rather than a variation.
2. **Elevate much more than feels reasonable.** Shrug the choking shoulder,
   stand up if the position allows, or get to butterfly or high guard and lift
   the head. An extended thick neck exposes 3–5 cm of throat below the jaw that
   simply does not exist when the chin is on the chest. Elevation is worth more
   here than depth.
3. **Blade, don't wrap.** Stop driving the arm around the neck. Place the radial
   edge of your wrist directly under the jawline and pull **up along the jaw**
   rather than around the throat. On a thick neck the only place the arm fits is
   the notch under the mandible, and the pull direction has to match.
4. **Shorten the loop.** Take the high-elbow grip — choking hand cupping your own
   opposite bicep or shoulder with the elbow held high — rather than
   palm-to-palm. Palm-to-palm is the *longest* configuration and is the wrong
   choice on the build where length is the constraint.
5. **Know when to quit.** If after all of that your forearm is at the base of the
   neck and their chin is on their chest, you have a **low-elevation crank**, not
   a choke. Cranking a thick-necked person is banned under IBJJF rules at every
   belt and unkind everywhere else. Let go and take the position — the
   [D'Arce](./darce.md), the [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md), or the back are
   all better answers to this build than the guillotine is.

### Short neck / high shoulders

Distinct from a thick neck: the head simply sits low between high shoulders, so
there is no gap under the jaw at any elevation. This build defeats the wrap
entirely, and no grip change fixes it.

- **Arm-in only**, and change what you are aiming at. You are no longer wrapping
  a neck; you are building a head-and-arm wedge with your forearm on one carotid
  and their own shoulder on the other. Angle your chest away from the trapped arm
  and drive your ribs into their trapped shoulder.
- **Better still, do something else.** The [D'Arce](./darce.md) enters through
  the armpit rather than the neck and does not need the gap that does not exist
  here. So does the [arm triangle](./arm-triangle.md). Both are purpose-built for
  this body.
- Best of all: use the front headlock to **go behind**. The atlas notes the front
  headlock is the hub connecting takedown defense, guillotines, D'Arces and back
  takes — against a short, thick neck, the back take is the highest-percentage
  road out of that hub.

### Thin or long neck

The failure here is over-insertion, and it feels like success. Everything sinks
in, you get a beautiful grip, and the blade of your wrist has travelled past the
throat and is resting on the far side of their jaw. You are cranking their head
sideways and compressing nothing.

- **Stop when your elbow is under their chin,** not past it. Sight along their
  sternum; the point of your elbow should be roughly on the centreline of their
  chest.
- **Take arm-out** on this build — it is available and it is tighter — with the
  **shortest** grip: choking hand to your own opposite bicep, not palm-to-palm.
  A long neck inside a long loop is slack.
- **Spend less on elevation, more on the hips.** They already have exposed
  throat; you do not need to manufacture more. Put the effort into the
  hips-forward/chest-back extension.
- Expect it to arrive quickly and be ready to release.

### Their long arms

Long-armed opponents beat the guillotine in two ways: they get a hand inside the
loop before you close it, and they post far away to rebuild posture and lift you.

- **Kill the inside hand first.** Their near hand is the only thing that can
  enter the loop; either take it in deliberately (arm-in) or fight it down and
  out before you take a grip. Half-committing is how you end up with a hand in
  the choke and no submission.
- **Trap the arm with your leg.** From closed or high guard, throwing a leg over
  their near arm removes the frame and the reach in one move, and it sets up the
  transition to a triangle or omoplata if the guillotine fails.
- **Go to your side.** The "hip-out" guillotine — hips turned toward the choking
  side, your weight dropping on the choke — puts your head and grip somewhere a
  long arm cannot reach around.
- **Control the trapped elbow** in the arm-in version. A long trapped arm that is
  allowed to extend forward reduces the shoulder wedge; pulled across, it
  increases it.

### Their short arms

With the arm in, the strangle depends partly on their trapped arm being extended
so their own shoulder is jammed into their own neck. A short trapped arm does not
reach past your body and the wedge is weak — you feel a lot of head control and no
strangle.

- **Supply the wedge yourself.** Put your knee or thigh against the back of their
  trapped shoulder and drive it forward while you pull the head back. You are
  replacing the leverage their arm length was supposed to provide.
- **Or clamp with the guard.** Closing your closed guard high, above their
  trapped elbow, compresses their shoulder into their neck directly.
- Short-armed opponents are also worse at getting the defensive hand inside — so
  arm-out is more available on this build than you would expect. Try it.

### Much heavier than you

The guillotine is the submission most often used badly against bigger people,
because it is available early in a scramble and feels like it is working.

- **Do not finish from closed guard.** A heavier opponent stands, stacks, or
  simply picks you up. Open the guard, put a **foot on their hip**, and extend
  them — extension is what makes the choke, and a foot on the hip supplies it
  without a strength contest.
- **Better: get to your side or to butterfly.** From your hip you can drop your
  bodyweight onto the choke instead of pulling with your arms, and their weight
  no longer stacks straight down onto you.
- **Arm-in, always.** Against a bigger opponent, an arm-out guillotine that fails
  gives them your back or a pass; an arm-in guillotine that fails still leaves
  you attached to their arm.
- **If they stand up with you attached, let go.** Holding a half-finished
  guillotine while a bigger person lifts you is how the guillotine's worst
  injuries happen, and in MMA and self-defense contexts it is how you get slammed.
  Slams are banned in almost every grappling ruleset — that is a rule protecting
  you, not a tactic you can rely on.

### Much lighter than you

Their neck is inside a loop that is much too big for it, which means every
adjustment that "tightens" it actually converts it into a crank.

- **Use the shortest configuration you have** — hand to your own opposite
  shoulder — and pull **straight up along their jawline** rather than trying to
  wrap further around.
- **Prefer arm-in.** It gives you a wedge instead of requiring circumference.
- **Go slowly and expect it to arrive early.** This is the most common way people
  hurt smaller training partners: the loop feels loose to you, so you pull
  harder, and what you are pulling on is a spine.

### Very flexible and scrambly

They will not defend the choke — they will spin out, invert, or roll through it
before your grip closes.

- **Take the hip before you take the grip.** A leg over their back or a hand on
  their far hip removes the direction they want to spin. From the front headlock,
  the same job is done by putting your near knee against their far shoulder.
- **Grip first, position second.** Against scramblers the grip is the thing that
  survives the chaos; positioning can be fixed afterwards, a lost grip cannot.
- Be ready to **take the back** when they spin. A spinning defender is turning
  their back to you by definition.

### Very muscular, thick shoulders and lats

Heavy shoulder girdles can shrug up and armour the neck completely against an
arm-out guillotine — there is nowhere for the blade to sit.

- **Arm-in.** Their bulk stops being an obstacle and starts being the wedge:
  a thick deltoid driven into their own neck closes a carotid better than a thin
  one does.
- **Angle away from the trapped arm** so your ribcage compresses that shoulder
  in, and finish with hips rather than arms.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**Short arms.** Arm-in is your default, not your variation. Use the high-elbow
grip (hand to your own opposite bicep or shoulder) as standard, invest heavily in
elevation, and get comfortable with the front headlock as a *position* rather
than a race to a grip — for you the back take will out-earn the choke.

**Long arms.** You can wrap almost anyone, which means over-insertion is your
standard failure. Set the same hard rule as with the RNC: the elbow stops under
their chin, on their sternum line. You can also use the arm-out and 10-finger
versions that shorter-armed people cannot reach.

**Small hands or a weak grip.** Never grip your own wrist — it slips and it puts
your grip strength in the load path. Gable (palm-to-palm) and hand-to-bicep grips
hold by geometry. Avoid the S-grip under load; it pops at the worst moment.

**Short legs.** Closing a closed guard around a big torso to finish is not
available to you. Default to the foot-on-hip extension or the butterfly-hook
elevation, both of which produce more extension than a closed guard does anyway.

**Much lighter than your partners.** Your finishing force has to come from
extension and bodyweight, not from arm strength. That means: open guard with a
foot on the hip, or on your side with your weight hanging on the choke. It also
means letting go early is a technique — a guillotine held past its moment
donates position to someone who can take it.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| They're grimacing and complaining about their neck, then escaping | Low-elevation crank, not a choke — your forearm is on the base of the neck | Elevate the head above their shoulder line, or release; a crank is banned under IBJJF rules at every belt |
| Your forearm is on their jaw and it's sliding | The neck is too thick for the wrap, so the arm found the narrowest part | Switch to arm-in and blade along the jawline, or abandon to the D'Arce |
| You have a great grip and they're calm | Over-insertion — the blade is past the throat on the far jaw | Back out 3–5 cm until the elbow is on their sternum line |
| They got a hand inside and it's dead | You took a grip before winning the near-arm fight | Decide arm-in or arm-out *before* gripping; if the hand is already in, take the arm in and finish that version |
| They stand up and lift you | You're finishing from closed guard against someone who can carry you | Open the guard, foot on the hip; if they're already standing, release |
| They pass to side control while you hold on | You held past the moment, and you're now feeding a Von Flue | Release and re-guard; the Von Flue is the standard punishment for exactly this |
| Arm-in feels loose | Their trapped shoulder isn't being driven into their own neck | Angle your chest away from the trapped arm and press your ribs into that shoulder; add a knee behind it |

## 6. Chains

| Their defense | Your next attack |
|---|---|
| They posture and pull the head out | Follow them up — sweep to top, or take the front headlock and go behind |
| They drive forward into you to relieve pressure | Hip out to the choking side and take the back |
| Arm-in stalls, their shoulder won't wedge | [D'Arce](./darce.md) — same trapped arm, entered through the armpit instead |
| They turn their head away and duck | [Anaconda](./anaconda.md) — the gap they just made under the neck is the anaconda's entry |
| They stack you in closed guard | Open the guard, foot on the hip, or transition to a [triangle](./triangle-choke.md) — the arm-out guillotine and the triangle share a setup |
| They defend by hugging your leg | Sweep — a defender committed to your leg has no base |
| Nothing is available and you're losing position | Let go and pass, or re-guard. Holding a dead guillotine is how the Von Flue happens |

Coming *into* the guillotine: a stuffed shot, a snap down, closed guard posture
breaks, butterfly hooks, and the front headlock. The atlas rates "guillotine on
the shot" at Diff 2 / Com 4 and flags it as highly effective and highly abused —
both halves of that are true.

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense (before the grip closes).** The whole fight. Never let your head
travel below your hips in a scramble, and if it does, get a hand to your own neck
before their arm closes — one hand inside the loop kills the arm-out version
outright. Posture with a hand on their hip, and pull your head straight out
backwards while their grip is still forming.

**Middle defense (grip closed, position not yet finished).** Follow the atlas
line: **hips to the choking-arm side**, chin down, two hands on their choking
wrist, then peel and pass. Moving your hips toward the choking arm removes the
extension that the choke depends on; staying square feeds it. Drop your shoulder
into their jaw as you go. If you can get to side control on the choking-arm side
with their arm still around your neck, you have the **Von Flue** available — the
atlas rates it Diff 3 / Com 3, and it is the standard punishment for a
guillotine held too long.

**Late defense (elevated, extended, both carotids closed).** Substantially worse
than early defense. Once your neck is above your shoulder line with their hips
driving into you, you are in a real blood choke and the clock is short. There is
no strong late technical answer, and the crank versions can injure your neck
before you pass out. **Tap.** Do not attempt to stand and slam your way out —
slams are banned in almost every ruleset and they injure the person doing the
slamming as often as the person being slammed.

## 8. Drills

**1. Elevation reps.** Partner kneels in front of you with their head down. Take
the wrap, then *lift* until their neck is above their shoulder line — no
squeezing, no grip closing. **3 sets of 15 per side.** You are training the
difference between a choke and a crank as a physical sensation.

**2. Arm-in / arm-out sorting.** Partner presents a random head position and arm
position; you have two seconds to commit to arm-in or arm-out and take the
matching grip. **20 reps.** The failure this fixes — gripping first and deciding
later — accounts for most dead guillotines.

**3. Three necks.** Take a guillotine on three partners of clearly different neck
thickness and finish each with whichever version works: high arm-out, high
arm-in, or a deliberate release-and-transition. **Two reps each.** The correct
outcome for the thickest neck may be "let go and take the back" — that is a pass,
not a failure.

**4. Guillotine positional sparring.** Bottom player starts with the wrap already
in place but no grip; top player starts with posture broken. Bottom wins by
finishing or sweeping; top wins by passing or getting their head free. **90-second
rounds.** Add the Von Flue as a legal win condition for the top player and the
bottom player will learn very quickly when to let go.

---

## 9. Safety

Be honest about which guillotine you have. The **high-elevation** version is a
blood choke — safe, fast, and legal at every belt in every major ruleset. The
**low-elevation** version, on many body types, is a **flexion crank on the
cervical spine**. IBJJF bans neck cranks at every belt (see
[`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md) Part XII), and even where a ruleset permits it,
it hurts before it is dangerous and is dangerous before it makes anyone tap.
If your partner is reporting neck pain rather than going quiet, release.

Apply progressively. Do not jump to a guillotine from standing on someone who can
lift you, and **release immediately if they stand up with you attached** — the
worst injuries associated with this technique come from a defender lifting and
dropping a person who would not let go. Slams are banned in almost every ruleset,
which is a rule that exists to protect you; it is not a rule you can lean on.

Finally: a guillotine you hold from bottom side control is a gift to a Von Flue
choke. Letting go is a technique.
