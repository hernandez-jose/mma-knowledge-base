# Kimura

> A figure-four grip that rotates their shoulder behind their back — and, more valuable than the tap, a grip that controls a whole side of their body while you decide what to do with it.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Joint lock (shoulder — internal rotation) |
| **Comes from** | Closed guard, half guard, side control, north–south, turtle top, standing and single-leg counters |
| **Difficulty** | 2/5 |
| **How common** | 5/5 |
| **Legal** | All belts, all major rulesets |

**Also known as:** gyaku ude-garami, double wristlock (DWL), chicken wing (wrestling), reverse keylock

The rating of 2/5 is honest about the grip and generous about the finish. Taking
the kimura grip is a day-two skill; *finishing* it on someone with mobile
shoulders is not. That gap is what section 3 is about.

---

## 1. The mechanism

The kimura is a **shoulder rotation lock**, not an arm bend. Their elbow is
folded to roughly 90 degrees and their upper arm is held roughly square to their
body; you then rotate the whole humerus **internally** — their hand travels away
from their head, toward their feet and behind their back. The load lands on the
front of the shoulder capsule and the rotator cuff, mainly the tissue that
resists that rotation. Some people will feel it at the elbow or the wrist first,
because the figure-four also loads both.

The reason the figure-four grip exists is leverage transfer. One hand grips their
wrist; your other arm threads under their upper arm and grips your own wrist.
That closed loop turns your entire torso rotation into torque at their shoulder —
you finish by turning your shoulders and dropping your elbow, not by pulling with
your arms.

Two things must be true or nothing rotates:

- **Their elbow stays bent.** A straight arm has no handle. This is why "extend
  the arm" is the first line of kimura defense in [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).
- **Their shoulder blade is pinned.** If the scapula can slide across their back,
  they rotate the whole shoulder girdle and you are just turning their body.
  Every good kimura has their torso trapped — by the mat, your chest, your leg
  over their back, or your hips.

**The kimura is also a control system.** The grip alone stops them posting,
stops them turning away, and steers their far shoulder. It sweeps
(the kimura sweep from closed guard is rated 2/5 difficulty and 4/5 frequency in
the atlas), it takes backs, it stops guard passes and it kills their frames. If
the finish is not there, you have not wasted the grip — you have bought a
position.

## 2. The standard application

From closed guard, against someone your size.

1. Break their posture and wait for — or bait — a hand posting on the mat beside
   your hip.
2. Sit up on your same-side elbow and grip that posted wrist, **thumbless**
   (four fingers over the back of the wrist, thumb alongside them). A thumb-round
   grip is weaker and easier to strip.
3. Thread your other arm over their shoulder, around the outside of their upper
   arm, and grip your own wrist. **Your threading arm's elbow should be tight to
   their arm**, not flared — the tighter the loop, the less slack there is to
   fight through.
4. **Throw your bottom leg over their back** — usually the leg on the far side
   from the trapped arm — and pull them down with it. Skip this and they will
   either posture up or roll straight over you.
5. Fall back and off to your side, taking their arm with you. You want an angle;
   finishing square from flat on your back is the weakest version.
6. Drive their elbow *up* off their back with your threading forearm, and pull
   their wrist *down* toward their belt line.
7. Finish by rotating your torso and dropping your bottom elbow toward the mat.
   Slowly.

The single biggest error is step 6 done in one direction only: people pull the
hand and forget to lift the elbow. Without the lift, the rotation runs out of the
shoulder and into their back, where their own body blocks it.

### Position-specific notes

| Position | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Side control** | Trap the near arm to the mat, step over the head or drive your knee into their ribs so they can't roll. Their hip must be blocked or they bridge into you. |
| **North–south** | Excellent kimura position — their arm is already elevated and their lat cannot engage. Sit through toward their hip as you rotate. |
| **Half guard bottom** | Take the grip on their far arm as they underhook. This is the classic answer to a strong underhook, and it sweeps as often as it finishes. |
| **Turtle top** | Reach under their armpit for the wrist and roll them over the trapped shoulder. |
| **Single-leg defense** | They shoot, you take the far wrist and figure-four it — the atlas lists this as a takedown defense in its own right (3/5, 3/5). |

## 3. Adjusting for their body

The kimura is the most **flexibility-dependent** submission in the standard
curriculum. Where the [armbar](./armbar.md) changes with limb *length*, the
kimura changes with shoulder *range* — and shoulder range varies between people
far more than most grapplers expect. The same grip that makes one person tap in
half a second can be a comfortable resting position for the person next to them
on the mat.

| Their build | Why it breaks the standard version | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Very flexible shoulders** | Their hand reaches the middle of their own back without discomfort. You reach the end of *your* range before they reach the end of theirs, so cranking does nothing but strip your grip. | Change the rotation vector, don't add force: adduct their elbow to their ribs first, then rotate toward their **opposite hip pocket**. If two attempts fail, cash the grip in for a sweep, a back take or a different joint. |
| **Stiff shoulders** | They tap almost as soon as the loop closes, sometimes before you have applied anything. The whole torso moves with the arm. | Pin the torso and move a few degrees, not a few inches. Expect the tap earlier than looks reasonable — and take it seriously. |
| **Thick, muscular arms** | The figure-four will not close — you cannot reach your own wrist — and their lat clamps the elbow to their ribs. | Grip further down toward their hand where the limb is thinner, or grip your own forearm/sleeve instead of your wrist. Peel the elbow off their ribs with a wedge before you rotate. |
| **Thin arms** | Your grip slides down to their hand and the loop loses the elbow angle. | Grip on the wrist bones themselves, knuckles on the back of their hand, and keep the elbow forced to 90 degrees with your threading forearm. |
| **Short arms** | Very little lever between their elbow and their wrist — you need far more force per degree of rotation. | Take your grip as far toward their hand as you can, elevate their elbow above their shoulder line, and rotate with your torso. Their arm is also too short to reach their own belt, so the classic grip defense is unavailable. |
| **Long arms** | They reach their own belt, lapel or thigh and anchor the arm, or straighten it out of the loop. | Break the anchoring grip first, then pin the elbow above 90 degrees of bend with your chest or shoulder so it cannot straighten. |
| **Much heavier than you** | They stand up out of your guard or flatten you before you can angle off. | Treat it as a sweep, not a finish. Leg over the back, off-balance them onto the posting arm, come up on top with the grip still in your hands. |
| **Much lighter than you** | They forward-roll out of it fast, and their shoulder reaches end range sooner than you expect. | Block the roll with your shin across their back and a grip on the far hip. Apply at a fraction of your usual speed. |
| **Long torso** | From guard, your leg over their back lands on their belt line instead of their shoulder blades, so it doesn't stop them posturing. | Hook their far shoulder with your instep instead of lying the leg across, or switch to the underhook side and finish from a half-guard angle. |

### Very flexible shoulders — the one that decides the page

This is the defining problem of the kimura, and it is the case where the
instinctive response is exactly wrong.

Textbook internal rotation at the shoulder is somewhere around 70 to 90 degrees
as a rule of thumb, and the standard kimura finish exploits that limit. But
plenty of people — swimmers, gymnasts, dancers, anyone generally hypermobile, and
a fair number of ordinary humans with no athletic history — have far more. Their
hand goes flat to the middle of their own back with no more sensation than a
stretch. You will run out of grip strength, position and patience long before
their capsule notices anything.

**Do not crank harder.** Force applied to a joint that still has range is force
applied to nothing; all you actually achieve is stripping your own grip, or
generating enough sudden torque that when you *do* reach their limit, you blow
past it. That is how kimuras dislocate shoulders.

Change the geometry instead:

1. **Adduct before you rotate.** Internal rotation range is largest with the arm
   held out at 90 degrees from the body and much smaller with the elbow pinned
   against the ribs. Before you rotate at all, drag their elbow in to their own
   side. On a very flexible opponent this alone can take 30 or more degrees out
   of their available range.
2. **Change the vector.** Instead of driving their hand straight up their spine
   (pure internal rotation), pull it toward their **opposite back pocket**. That
   adds extension and adduction to the rotation, and the combination hits end
   range far sooner than any single direction does. This is the most useful
   single adjustment on this page.
3. **Kill the scapula.** A large part of what feels like shoulder flexibility is
   the shoulder blade sliding. Get your chest, your knee or your hip on their
   shoulder blade and pin it to the mat or to their own back. Only the ball joint
   should be free to move.
4. **Take the slack out of the elbow and wrist.** A hypermobile shoulder does not
   imply a hypermobile elbow. Slide your gripping hand right down to the base of
   their hand and rotate their forearm as you turn — many finishes against
   flexible people are actually elbow or wrist finishes wearing a kimura costume.
5. **Elevate the elbow.** Their elbow should be higher than their shoulder line.
   An elbow lying flat on their back is a rest position; lifting it 10 cm
   restores the rotation you thought you had.

**And then have a rule.** Two honest attempts at the rotation. If it is not
there, stop attacking the shoulder and spend the grip — you are holding one of
the best control grips in jiu-jitsu:

| Instead of finishing | Do this |
|---|---|
| Kimura from closed guard | Kimura sweep — they base against the rotation, you roll them over it |
| Kimura from half guard | Come up to the dogfight, or take the back |
| Kimura from side control | Step over to north–south, then to the [armbar](./armbar.md) — their elbow is not more flexible than average |
| Kimura anywhere | Switch rotation: try the [americana](./americana.md) direction. Big internal rotation rarely comes with equally big external rotation |
| Kimura with the wrist already owned | [Wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md), blue belt and up under IBJJF |

### Stiff shoulders

The mirror problem, and the more dangerous one for your partner.

- **Almost no travel is needed.** Someone with limited internal rotation may be
  at end range as soon as the figure-four closes. Apply in degrees, not inches.
- **Their torso comes with the arm.** Because they cannot rotate at the shoulder,
  the rotation transfers to their spine and they simply turn. That looks like
  them escaping. The fix is to pin the torso harder — leg over the back, chest on
  the shoulder — not to pull harder.
- **Beware where the pain lands.** With a stiff shoulder the elbow and wrist
  absorb the load first. If they tap at what feels to you like "nothing", that is
  a wrist or elbow tap. Respect it identically.

### Thick, muscular arms

- **The loop will not close.** Move your gripping hand toward their hand where
  the forearm is narrower, and grab your own **forearm** rather than your wrist —
  or, in the gi, your own sleeve. A loop closed further down the limb is worth
  more than a loop that doesn't close at all.
- **Thread deeper.** Push your threading arm through until your armpit, not your
  hand, is what contacts their triceps. The deeper the thread, the shorter the
  slack.
- **Their lat is the defense.** A strong opponent clamps the elbow to their ribs
  and the arm becomes part of their torso. Peel it off before you rotate: wedge
  your knee or shin under their elbow, or move to north–south, where the lat
  cannot engage in that direction at all.
- **Good news:** a thick arm cannot straighten out of the loop as easily, and a
  bulky bicep bottoms out at around 90 degrees of elbow bend, which is exactly
  the angle that transmits rotation best. Once the loop is closed on a big arm,
  it stays closed.

### Thin arms

- Your grip creeps down onto their hand as you rotate, which turns the shoulder
  lock into a weak wrist lock you didn't plan.
- **Grip the wrist bones**, thumbless, with your knuckles across the back of
  their hand, and re-grip if it slides. Do not chase it with squeezing.
- **Hold the elbow angle actively.** A thin arm straightens easily; use your
  threading forearm to keep their elbow folded rather than assuming it will stay.

### Short arms

- The distance from their elbow to their wrist is your entire lever arm. Short
  arm, short lever, more force needed for the same torque.
- **Grip as far toward their hand as you can** without sliding onto it, to
  recover a couple of centimetres.
- **Elevate the elbow above their shoulder line** so the rotation is pure — with
  a short lever you cannot afford to waste any of it on slack.
- **Rotate with your body.** Turn your shoulders and drop your bottom elbow to
  the mat; do not try to curl their hand with your arms.
- The upside: short arms cannot reach their own belt, waistband or far lapel.
  The most common grip-based defense simply isn't available to them, so if you
  get the loop, you keep it.

### Long arms

- **They can anchor.** A long-armed opponent reaches their own belt, their own
  thigh, or the far lapel and turns the arm into part of their body. Strip that
  grip before you commit: two hands on one of theirs, peel at the thumb, or use
  a leg to kick the hand off.
- **They can straighten.** Long limbs give them more room to extend out of the
  loop. Keep the elbow above 90 degrees of bend by pressing your chest or
  shoulder into it and pulling the wrist in, not out.
- **You have more lever.** Once you own it, a long arm is the easiest kimura in
  the room — a small movement of your torso is a large rotation at their
  shoulder. That is also a safety issue. Move less than you think.

### Much heavier than you

- **From guard, the kimura is a sweep.** A much heavier opponent will stand up
  and lift you rather than let you finish. Take the grip, throw the leg over
  their back, break their base over the posting arm and come up on top; you keep
  the grip through the whole sweep and can finish there instead.
- **Never trade downward force with them.** Their weight beats your arms. Angle
  off so their weight travels past you rather than through you.
- **From side control**, block the far hip with your hand or knee before you
  rotate — a heavy bridge with your arms committed to the loop puts you on your
  back.

### Much lighter than you

- **They will roll.** The forward somersault over the trapped shoulder is the
  standard kimura escape and light, mobile opponents do it instantly. Block it:
  shin or knee across their back, or a grip on their far hip; if the roll starts
  anyway, follow it and keep the loop — you land in the same lock on the other
  side.
- **Their end range arrives early.** Less muscle around the joint means less
  buffer. Half speed, and be ready to stop the moment their shoulder stops
  moving freely.

### Long torso

From closed guard, the "leg over the back" step assumes their shoulder blades
are roughly where your knee lands. On a long-torsoed opponent your leg lies
across their belt instead and does nothing about their posture.

- **Hook the far shoulder with your instep** rather than laying the leg flat.
- Or **switch sides**: attack from an underhook and finish from a half-guard or
  dogfight angle, where the distance from their hips to their shoulder no longer
  matters.

## 4. Adjusting for your body

**If your arms are short:** closing the figure-four around a large arm may be
physically impossible. Grip your own sleeve or forearm instead of your wrist and
accept a slightly weaker loop, or choose positions where the loop is easier to
close — north–south and turtle top put their arm in front of you rather than
across you.

**If your hands are small or your grip is weak:** thumbless grips depend on
finger strength you may not have. Compensate with a deeper thread — armpit to
triceps — so your skeleton, not your fingers, holds the loop, and finish by
turning your body rather than squeezing.

**If you are much lighter:** the kimura is one of your best grips precisely
because it does not require you to hold anyone down. Use it as a control from
guard and half guard, and finish from angles where their weight is not on you
(north–south, and the finish after the sweep rather than before it).

**If you are much heavier:** you can close the loop on almost anyone and rotate
almost anyone, which means you can injure almost anyone. Cut your speed in half
and stop the instant the arm stops moving freely — with your leverage advantage
you will reach their end range without noticing you got there.

**If your shoulders are stiff:** the finish requires you to rotate your own torso
under load. If that is uncomfortable, favour the sit-through finish (turning to
face their hips) over the fall-back finish.

## 5. Failure modes

| What you feel | What's actually wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Their arm rotates a long way and nothing happens | Genuinely flexible shoulder | Adduct the elbow to their ribs, rotate toward their opposite hip pocket, pin the scapula — then switch attacks |
| Grip keeps getting stripped | You're pulling with your arms instead of turning with your torso | Take the slack, then rotate your shoulders and drop your bottom elbow |
| Their whole body turns with the arm | Their torso isn't pinned | Leg over the back, chest on the shoulder blade, or block the far hip |
| The arm straightens out of the loop | Their elbow was above 90 degrees of extension before you committed | Re-bend the elbow with your threading forearm; never chase a straight arm — take the [armbar](./armbar.md) instead |
| You can't close the figure-four | Their arm is thicker than your reach allows | Grip your own forearm or sleeve; thread deeper |
| They forward roll and come out on top | Nothing was blocking the roll | Shin across their back, grip the far hip — or follow the roll and keep the grip |
| Finish feels like it's in their elbow | The elbow is straightening as you rotate | Not necessarily wrong, but stop and reassess — if it's a straight arm, it's a different submission |
| It works from side control and never from guard | You're skipping the leg over the back | Leg first, fall back second |

## 6. Chains

### Their defense → your next attack

| Their defense | Your next attack | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Straighten the arm | [Armbar](./armbar.md) — the straight arm is the setup | You already own the wrist |
| Grab their own belt / lapel / thigh | Break the grip with a leg kick or two-on-one, or attack the free side | A hand on their belt is a hand not defending their neck |
| Clamp the elbow to their ribs | Move to north–south, then re-rotate | The lat cannot resist in that direction from there |
| Post hard against the rotation | Kimura sweep — roll them over the posting arm | Their base is committed to one side |
| Forward roll | Follow the roll and keep the loop, or release and take the back | They give you their back for a beat |
| Flexible shoulder, no tap | [Americana](./americana.md) direction, [wrist lock](./wrist-lock.md), or take the back | Different joint, different range |
| Turn away to hide the arm | Take the back — the grip already stops them defending the neck | The kimura grip and the seatbelt fit together |

### What feeds into it

| From | The trigger |
|---|---|
| Closed guard | They post a hand on the mat beside your hip |
| Half guard bottom | They dig a deep underhook — take the far wrist |
| Side control | They frame with the near arm, or turn away |
| North–south | Their arms are already up beside their head |
| Turtle top | Reach through the armpit for the wrist |
| Single-leg defense | They shoot, their far arm is exposed |
| Failed [guillotine](./guillotine.md) | The arm you couldn't choke with is a kimura arm |

## 7. Defending it

**Early defense wins, late defense mostly doesn't.** The atlas puts it plainly:
"fight the grip early and extend the arm; once it's bent behind you, you're
mostly done." That is a fair description of the reality.

**Early — before the loop closes.** Do not post a straight arm on the mat next to
someone's hip in closed guard; post on their body or on your own knee instead.
If a hand goes to your wrist, strip it immediately — a kimura grip is much easier
to prevent than to remove. Keep your elbows connected to your ribs.

**Middle — the loop is closed, your arm is still in front of you.** Straighten
the arm. That is the whole defense and it must happen now. Reach for your own
belt, your own thigh, or the far lapel and anchor. Simultaneously, turn *into*
them and get your elbow back to your own ribs — hiding the arm under your body
removes the space they need to rotate. If you can, put your shoulder blade flat
to the mat: a pinned scapula defends you as well as it attacks you.

**Late — the arm is bent behind your back.** Your options narrow to the forward
roll over the trapped shoulder, and it only works if you go *now* and if their
grip on your hips is loose. If they have your hip blocked with a shin or a hand,
the roll is not available and you are choosing between an injured shoulder and a
tap. **Tap.** Shoulder capsules and rotator cuffs heal slowly and incompletely,
and the kimura is fully capable of dislocating a shoulder.

A note on kimura *control*: many opponents will hold the grip without attacking
because they want the position, not the tap. Do not relax into it. The grip alone
is costing you your posts, your frames and your ability to turn; treat it as an
emergency even when nothing hurts.

## 8. Drills

**1. Grip-and-thread (30 per side, no resistance).**
Partner posts a hand on the mat. Sit up, thumbless wrist grip, thread, close the
loop, leg over the back, fall to your side. Stop before rotating. The rep is the
entry, not the finish — most failed kimuras fail at step 3.

**2. The flexible-partner audit (once, 10 minutes, high value).**
Take a light kimura grip on four or five different training partners and rotate
*slowly* to the point where each of them first feels it. Note how wildly
different that point is. This is the drill that stops you cranking on the
flexible ones — you cannot un-learn how much variation there is once you've felt
it.

**3. Vector drill (10 reps each, three directions).**
On a cooperative partner, take the same grip and finish three ways: hand straight
up the spine; elbow adducted to the ribs first; hand toward the opposite back
pocket. Same grip, three different end ranges. Learn which one your partner runs
out of first.

**4. Positional sparring — kimura grip, no finish allowed (90-second rounds).**
Start with the grip already established from closed guard. You may sweep, take
the back or pass with it, but you may not finish the kimura. This teaches the
control system, which is where most of the technique's value actually is.

## 9. Safety

- **Apply slowly and rotate in small increments.** The shoulder gives some
  warning, but the failure — a dislocation or a labral tear — is far more serious
  than most joint injuries in the sport and can end a training year.
- **Never crank on a flexible shoulder.** If they aren't tapping after two
  controlled attempts, they have range you cannot reach. Adding speed to close
  that gap is exactly how people get injured, because you arrive at their end
  range with momentum.
- **Watch for the elbow and the wrist.** The figure-four loads all three joints.
  A tap may be coming from a joint you weren't attacking; it counts the same.
- **Release on any tap, including a verbal one or a body slap you can't see.**
- **Standing kimuras and rolling kimuras** add momentum you cannot control. Do
  not drill these at speed, and do not use them on a partner who has not agreed
  to them.
- **Old shoulder injuries are extremely common in grappling.** Ask before rolling,
  and if a partner tells you their shoulder is unreliable, take the grip for
  control and skip the rotation.
- **Legality:** legal at every belt in IBJJF gi and no-gi, and in every
  mainstream ruleset. See the belt legality table in
  [`jujitsu.md`](../../jujitsu.md).
